## The opening selects directions

Light from the top of an object continues approximately straight through the opening and reaches the lower screen. A lower point reaches the upper screen. This direction selection forms an inverted image without a lens. For object distance a and screen distance b, image height divided by object height is −b/a.

A finite hole admits a bundle rather than one ray from each point. Rays crossing different parts of the opening land in a geometric patch. A smaller hole reduces this blur while admitting less light: halving diameter gives about one quarter of the area and admitted power.

## Compare shape separately from light power

Exploration enlarges the receiving-screen detail. Patches have normalized brightness so their size remains visible. Admitted power is listed separately relative to a 0.35 mm hole. Reduce diameter and compare both width scales. Hold it fixed and change screen distance to compare image size with blur.

## Geometry has a wave-optical boundary

<div className="formula">
  Geometric patch diameter = d(1 + b/a)
  <br />
  Circular-aperture far-field diffraction scale ≈ 2.44λb/d
</div>

The diffraction diameter refers to the first dark ring in the far field, not an exact point-spread function at every distance. The model uses 550 nm light, a 600 mm object distance and an ideal zero-thickness round opening. The drawn opening is enlarged. A root-sum-square of the two scales illustrates the competing trends without solving finite-distance Fresnel diffraction.

Where the scales become comparable, far-field conditions may be marginal. Their intersection is therefore not advertised as an exact optimal aperture. A real choice also depends on spectrum, exposure, screen irradiance, edge quality and desired resolution. Neither a smaller hole nor a larger image guarantees more detail without these qualifications.
