<h2 id="understand">Compare shared electrical properties</h2>

Page 2 of required volume 3 briefly links lightning and frictional electricity through Franklin’s work; it does not give a complete kite apparatus. The historical evidence concerns electricity from different sources being stored and used to repeat similar electrical effects.

This diagram translates that relationship into two modern equivalent systems with identical capacitance and charge indicators. They make accumulation, leaf opening and release after isolation comparable. **The apparatus and parameters are neither a reconstruction nor historical measurements.**

<h2 id="try">Separate effects, values and conclusions</h2>

Change both voltage magnitudes, then reverse only the modeled cloud-source sign. The indicators can still open equally because like charges on two leaves repel. Opening alone cannot identify sign; shared properties do not require equal charge quantities.

Isolate the sources and connect the release branches. Charge marks and indicators change together. Repeatable electrical behavior supports the connection. An animation clarifies that inference but does not replace the original historical evidence.

<h2 id="deeper">What the text and model each establish</h2>

<details>
<summary>Sources, approximations and limits</summary>

The original report dated 19 October 1752 describes electricity from clouds charging a phial and supporting experiments normally performed with rubbed glass. This scene paraphrases that evidential claim, without outdoor procedures or a lightning strike on a kite as the mechanism.

The modern model uses equal 1 nF capacitors, a 1 s charging time constant and 0.8 s release time constant. The two conductors carry equal opposite charge. Indicators balance electrostatic repulsion of like-charged leaves against gravity, neglecting their loading of the capacitors. Release begins at the preceding charged state and decays continuously. Clouds do not have one fixed polarity; the negative sign is a switchable example.

Storage jars and indicators form a modern teaching comparison. Collecting storm electricity is not a classroom activity.

</details>
