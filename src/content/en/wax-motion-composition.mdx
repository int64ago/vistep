title: 'The wax rises and the tube moves right: what is the combined motion?'
---

<section id="understand">

PEP Required Book 2 §5.2 (pages 6–9) places red wax in a sealed water-filled tube. After inversion the float first accelerates and then rises approximately uniformly. Translate the tube horizontally during this steady stage and observe motion relative to the board.

Both components use one time: x = vₓt and y = vᵧt. Constant components give y = (vᵧ/vₓ)x, a straight line. Combined speed is √(vₓ² + vᵧ²); the component ratio determines direction.

</section>
<section id="try">

Compare a stationary tube with uniform translation. Each one-second point comes from one clock. Projection lines represent simultaneous displacement components, not consecutive movements.

Switch to horizontal acceleration while retaining a 10 cm rise each second. Default acceleration is 0.08 m/s² from rest, reproducing the page 9 exercise’s successive horizontal increments of 4, 12, 20 and 28 cm. The trajectory curves but vector composition still applies.

</section>
<section id="deeper">

<details>

<summary>Startup and steady rise</summary>

The inversion demonstration uses constant net buoyancy and linear drag, giving an exponential approach from rest to a 0.1 m/s example terminal speed with a 0.25 s time constant. This distinguishes startup from steady rise; it is not a fit to textbook measurements. Composition begins with a new origin in the terminal regime.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Idealized conditions</summary>

The accelerated-tube case follows the textbook thought experiment: smooth walls transmit horizontal motion while the vertical component stays approximately unchanged. Real accelerating-fluid pressure, wall friction and float orientation can affect this approximation. The scene does not claim full fluid–solid coupling. The tube is about 1 m long and the demonstration ends before upper-cap contact.

</details>

</section>
