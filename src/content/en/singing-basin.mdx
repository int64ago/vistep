<h2 id="understand">Slow hands, a rapidly vibrating basin</h2>

Page 54 of Selective Compulsory 1 opens its section on forced vibration and resonance with a bronze washing basin. Water is poured inside and the handles are rubbed by hand; the basin hums and can throw up fountains. Slow hand movement can sustain much faster basin vibration. Equating the hand’s back-and-forth rate with the sound frequency misses the energy-transfer mechanism.

Modal experiments and nonlinear studies of the Chinese “dragon washbasin” identify **friction-induced self-excited vibration**. Under suitable conditions friction supplies energy to a small disturbance. The vibration grows until nonlinear losses limit its amplitude. Once rubbing stops, the existing vibration decays. See [Liu and colleagues, 1995](https://pubs-en.cstam.org.cn/article/id/ams_128837) and the [coupled elastic-structure study, 1998](https://pubs-en.cstam.org.cn/article/id/ams_128878).

<h2 id="try">Track energy and the locations of the fountains</h2>

- Keep the four-antinode mode and change effective friction input. A weak input cannot overcome losses; stronger input lets vibration build.
- Choose “Release after 8 seconds” and move time beyond 8 seconds. Modal energy decreases, followed by the disappearance of the spray.
- Compare the four- and six-antinode modes. Regions of large wall motion, and strong water response, change position. This is a controlled mode comparison, not a claim that simply rubbing faster will reliably select another mode.
- Increasing the water load lowers the illustrative modal frequency through added mass. At zero water load the basin can vibrate, but cannot spray water.

<h2 id="deeper">Self-excitation is different from a periodic external drive</h2>

An externally forced resonance experiment specifies the driving frequency. Friction-driven self-excitation instead depends on feedback between motion and friction. Its frequency relates to the coupled basin, water and elastic hand system. Wang’s experimental overview records four- and six-fountain states and discusses the hand’s elastic dynamics in mode selection. The **natural frequency of that elastic subsystem** is not the slow rate of hand rubbing. See [Wang, 2005, Section 3](https://journals.ub.ovgu.de/index.php/techmech/article/download/890/866/2444).

<summary>What the visualization actually computes</summary>

<div className="formula">dE/dt = 2(g − d)E − 2gE²; A = √E</div>

E is normalized modal energy, g effective friction gain and d the loss rate. This cycle-averaged envelope retains disturbance growth, saturation and free decay after the input ends. Its analytic solution makes seeking reproducible. It is not a fitted model of a particular physical basin.

The spatial mode is cos[n(θ−θ₀)], with n=2 or 3 giving four or six antinodes. Illustrative half-filled frequencies of 100/300 Hz are on the scale of specific examples in the literature, not universal measured values. Added water mass enters a simplified mass–stiffness relation. Visible shell motion is slowed and enlarged.

Spray markers appear above an illustrative response threshold and fall on gravitational trajectories. The model does not solve free-surface breakup, capillary waves, sound radiation or detailed mode selection. Fountain height, threshold and growth time must not be used to predict a real basin. Decorative engravings do not power the fountains: rubbing supplies the energy.
