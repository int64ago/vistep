## Temperature describes only part of the state

Near atmospheric pressure, pure ice at 0°C can absorb energy without becoming warmer. During solid–liquid coexistence, that energy converts more solid mass into liquid. Once melting finishes, further heating raises liquid temperature again. The plateau is neither a switched-off heater nor missing energy.

The film starts with 100 g of ice at −20°C and divides cumulative net heat into warming ice, phase change and warming water. Tiles represent phase mass fractions, not actual volume or molecular trajectories. Their states, the thermometer and the ledger all use one enthalpy calculation.

## Separate energy from elapsed time

Change heating time, power and mass. At fixed mass, doubling power and halving time gives the same net heat, temperature and phase fraction. Zero power adds no energy. More mass requires more transition energy. The final film chapter reverses transfer and reduces liquid fraction while energy leaves the sample.

## Account separately for sensible and latent heat

<div className="formula">
  Warming energy = mcΔT
  <br />
  Phase-change energy = melted mass × L<br />
  Net supplied heat = power × time
</div>

Rounded teaching constants are 2100 J/(kg·K) for ice, 4186 J/(kg·K) for water and 334000 J/kg for fusion near melting. Warming 100 g of ice from −20°C to0°C takes4.2 kJ. Melting it takes another33.4 kJ, and warming the resulting water to20°C takes8.372 kJ.

This is a uniform, near-equilibrium, constant-pressure enthalpy model for pure water near atmospheric pressure, without heat loss. It omits evaporation, container heat capacity, supercooling, nucleation delays and impurities. The plateau applies to phase coexistence under these conditions; it is not a rule for arbitrary mixtures, pressures or rapid heating. Constant properties are not precision thermophysical data.
