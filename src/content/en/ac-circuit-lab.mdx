<h2 id="understand">From voltage polarity to electric and magnetic energy</h2>

Connect the oscilloscope first to a battery and then to an AC teaching supply. Direct voltage keeps one polarity; sinusoidal alternating voltage crosses zero. Two antiparallel LEDs connected to a teaching generator reveal opposite current directions. Slip rings maintain contact without rectifying the output.

With a coil in series, first observe the instant DC is connected. Current builds, the magnetic field grows, and that change induces an opposing emf. Once current settles, this induced emf approaches zero. A coil opposes changes in current. At the same three-volt RMS supply, AC keeps changing; raising frequency increases reactance and further reduces average lamp power.

With a series capacitor, opposite charges accumulate on its plates. Capacitor voltage rises, leaving less voltage across the lamp, so charging current falls. Blocking steady DC does not erase the initial transient. AC repeatedly changes plate charge and sustains external current: arrows follow the wires, and no electrons cross the insulating gap. Raising frequency reduces capacitive reactance, so this lamp becomes brighter.

Switch a charged capacitor onto a coil and disconnect the supply. Set resistance to zero and pause at a quarter-cycle: capacitor voltage is zero, current is maximal and all the initial energy is magnetic. Self-induction prevents current from stopping abruptly. It recharges the capacitor with opposite polarity, returning energy to the electric field. Add resistance and the heat portion of the energy band grows as oscillation amplitude decays.

<h2 id="try">Preserve the comparison conditions</h2>

- Compare a series coil on DC and AC using the same three-volt RMS supply.
- Hold capacitance fixed and raise frequency, then hold frequency fixed and increase capacitance.
- Pause as the LEDs alternate and inspect the current sign.
- Set LC resistance to zero to preserve total electromagnetic energy, then add loss.
