<h2 id="understand">Compare sounds over the same duration</h2>

The textbook records do and sol, pauses their waveforms and puts both on the same time scale. Frame ten complete do cycles, then count sol cycles within that same duration to obtain their frequency ratio.

The automatic demonstration uses explicitly synthetic examples at 264 Hz and 396 Hz, matching the textbook’s particular tuning. About 37.88 ms contains ten do cycles and fifteen sol cycles, a 3:2 ratio. This does not claim that all tuning systems use identical frequencies.

<h2 id="try">Capture actual sound</h2>

- In exploration, choose “Capture sound A” or “Capture sound B”. Microphone permission is requested only after your click; hold a steady note while a short clip is collected.
- Clips remain in the current page without upload. Capture and listening stop when the scene leaves view, the apparatus changes or the component unmounts.
- Listening starts only after an explicit click and uses a short loop, independently of narration.
- Both waveforms share the same time width. Autocorrelation estimates stable fundamentals from 80–1000 Hz. An uncertain or nonperiodic signal leaves the frequency blank.

Each waveform is vertically normalized to compare periods, so its height cannot compare the original recordings’ loudness. Microphone response, background noise and timbre affect real waveforms.

<h2 id="deeper">A separate apparatus measures propagation</h2>

The lake setup uses two boats, a simultaneous flash and submerged bell strike, and an underwater listening horn. Start timing when the light is seen and stop when the sound is heard.

<div className="formula">v≈distance/delay = 14000 m / 10 s = 1400 m/s</div>

The 14 km and 10 s values belong to the textbook’s simulated exercise, not the historical raw record. Geneva’s Science History Museum dates Colladon’s lake measurement to 1826, giving about 1435 m/s; the related dissertation was presented in 1827. This page keeps that historical background separate from the textbook values.

Including light travel gives a sound time of delay plus distance divided by light speed, or about 1399.993 m/s. The roughly 47 microseconds of light travel are negligible for this exercise. Boat separation is visually compressed and horizontal and vertical scales differ.
