<h2 id="understand">Test the solution before classifying the salt</h2>

The page 71 inquiry lists NaCl, Na₂CO₃, NH₄Cl, KNO₃, CH₃COONa and (NH₄)₂SO₄. Students choose an acidity test, then classify the salts by the strengths of their parent acids and bases. The word “salt” does not establish neutrality.

This demonstration adds equal 0.1 mol/L analytical concentrations at 25 °C. A glass rod transfers a sample onto dry pH paper, which changes only after contact. Ordinary paper reports approximate integer pH. NaCl and KNO₃ are near neutral in this model; carbonate and acetate are basic; the ammonium salts are acidic. Every solution remains electrically neutral overall: acidity does not imply a net excess of positive charge.

Acetate accepts protons, increasing equilibrium hydroxide. Ammonium donates protons to water. Carbonate requires sequential acid–base equilibria and carbon balance. Ammonium sulfate also includes bisulfate equilibrium; sulfate is not universally treated as an inert acid–base spectator. The textbook often writes NH₃·H₂O, while the model uses NH₃(aq) for unprotonated aqueous ammonia.

<h2 id="try">Change ferric-salt conditions while retaining a reference</h2>

The pages 75–76 inquiry supplies 0.01 mol/L ferric sulfate, crystals and concentrated sulfuric acid, asking students to design temperature and concentration comparisons. This visual adds an explicit design starting with 5 mL per sample. The left reference stays unchanged while the right receives one intervention at a time.

- **Temperature:** the treated sample warms from 25 to 70 °C. The thermal response is a teaching model, not a precise calibration of ferric-sulfate solution.
- **Dilution:** add 5 mL water while retaining total iron. The hydrolyzed fraction can rise while the concentration of hydrolyzed species falls. A lighter color does not necessarily mean a smaller hydrolyzed fraction.
- **Crystals:** added crystals increase total iron after entering the liquid. Color depends on both speciation and total concentration; fraction and amount must remain distinct.
- **Acid:** this controlled model uses an added design of 1 mL of 0.1 mol/L dilute sulfuric acid. It demonstrates suppression by increased acidity, separately from the later textbook concentrated-acid procedure.

Ferric calculations retain only the **first aqueous hydrolysis step**, Fe³⁺ + H₂O ⇌ FeOH²⁺ + H⁺, plus bisulfate. Real solutions also contain sulfate complexes, polymeric hydrolysis species and potentially solids. Neither yellow nor brown uniquely identifies one pure species; the first-step fraction is not the fraction of all iron converted to Fe(OH)₃.

<h2 id="deeper">The four applications in Experimental Activity 3</h2>

**Dissolve ferric sulfate, then add acid.** Page 94 dissolves a small amount of crystals in 5 mL water, then adds 2 mL concentrated sulfuric acid. The visual retains crystal dissolution, acid delivery and changing volume. The concentrated-acid stage is qualitative and gives no falsely precise pH. Acid suppression also explains dissolving FeCl₃ in hydrochloric acid before dilution. The concentrated-acid procedure belongs to the teacher-managed textbook laboratory setting.

**Iron and aluminum salts in muddy water.** All three samples begin with 5 mL muddy water. One remains untreated; the others receive 2 mL saturated FeCl₃ or 2 mL 1 mol/L Al₂(SO₄)₃, followed by mixing and five minutes standing. The original untreated reference has a different final volume. Exploration can add 2 mL water to that reference to examine the dilution contribution.

The same particles persist through aggregation and settling. Larger aggregates settle faster; particles are not deleted to make water appear clear. **This illustration explicitly assumes pH 6.5 after dosing**, showing a favorable condition. Lowering that condition can suppress aggregation. The textbook does not specify soil composition or buffering capacity, and its substantial coagulant doses do not independently guarantee a particular pH or removal rate. Actual outcomes require observation. Clearer water does not establish drinking-water safety.

**Prepare Fe(OH)₃ colloid.** The textbook adds five or six drops of saturated FeCl₃ to 40 mL boiling water; this demonstration uses six. Continue boiling until red-brown, then stop heating. Hydrolysis, a finely dispersed phase and acid production remain distinct from instantaneous complete settling of all iron. Because the saturated concentration is unspecified, each drop carries an equal normalized iron-dose unit, without inventing a known molar amount.

**Cold versus heated carbonate cleaning.** Both tubes contain 5 mL saturated Na₂CO₃ and equal vegetable oil. Only one is boiled, then both are shaken. The final textbook observation occurs **after discarding the liquids and rinsing with water**, comparing the inner walls. A separate view therefore follows tilting, outflow, rinsing and residual film. Heating affects hydrolysis, lipid reactions and removal. Displayed film fractions are mechanistic illustrations, not measured cleaning efficiencies.

<summary>Models, conservation and evidence limits</summary>

The six-salt model solves water, acetate, two carbonic equilibria, ammonium and bisulfate with element and charge balance. Textbook 25 °C values are acetic Ka = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵, carbonic Ka₁ = 4.5 × 10⁻⁷ and Ka₂ = 4.7 × 10⁻¹¹, and ammonia Kb = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵. The second sulfuric constant is 0.012. Activities and precise paper color calibration are omitted.

The first ferric hydrolysis constant is 10⁻²·¹⁹ at 25 °C from infinite-dilution research. A chosen 40 kJ/mol thermal parameter illustrates the temperature direction; it is not a measured enthalpy for this sulfate solution. Other constants stay fixed in that temperature demonstration. Its pH, color and FeOH²⁺ fraction belong only to this restricted model. Concentrated sulfuric acid particularly violates dilute-solution activity approximations.

Each muddy-water sample has 36 persistent representative particles. The Stokes settling illustration uses a density difference of 100 kg/m³, water viscosity 0.001 Pa·s and radii increasing from 2 µm to at most 35 µm with aggregation. Aggregation times, pH response and aggregate dimensions are teaching parameters, not textbook measurements. Real shape, interactions and flow may violate these assumptions.

Colloid preparation conserves normalized iron between dissolved and dispersed forms, with three formal acid equivalents per Fe(OH)₃ unit. Drops are illustratively 0.05 mL; evaporation and real polymer distributions are omitted. The cleaning model tracks oil equivalents through reaction, retained film and waste. Thermal rate differences and rinse detachment are illustrative. Pouring uses a two-dimensional equal-depth section matching the drawn vessel: the surface stays horizontal and only liquid exceeding the lower opening flows out. Input volume equals retained liquid plus waste throughout rinsing.
