<h2 id="understand">Build testable evidence for molecules you cannot see</h2>

The microscope shows suspended carbon particles, not water molecules. Random molecular impacts move the particles; smaller particles and warmer water generally make the motion more apparent. Joining positions recorded thirty seconds apart is an observation record, not the actual path between samples.

Mixing fifty millilitres of water with fifty millilitres of ethanol does not simply add their volumes. A representative near-room-temperature result is about ninety-six millilitres while mass remains unchanged. Interactions and the mixed structure determine the contraction; rigid spheres filling fixed gaps are only a rough analogy.

The oil-film experiment estimates microscopic length using macroscopic measurements. Calibrate a diluted drop, multiply by the oleic-acid volume fraction, measure the spread monolayer area, and divide volume by area.

<h2 id="try">Separate observation, calculation and measurement error</h2>

- Preserve the random sequence while changing tracer temperature or effective radius.
- Follow both cylinders through pouring and later contraction.
- Use one millilitre, fifty drops, 500-fold dilution and 200 square centimetres to obtain two nanometres.
- Increase the area reading and explain the smaller estimated thickness; then vary drop-count and dilution readings.

The carbon tracer uses an effective hydrodynamic radius, neglecting convection, particle interactions and focus-depth changes. Water viscosity varies through a room-temperature approximation. Particles intentionally leave the microscope field; sampled connecting lines do not resolve inertial trajectories.

The illustrated oil patch remains 200 square centimetres. Exploration changes measurement readings to examine error; it does not change that patch's physical area. A uniform monolayer, solvent preparation and a visible boundary are necessary assumptions. An oleic-acid molecule is not literally a sphere.
