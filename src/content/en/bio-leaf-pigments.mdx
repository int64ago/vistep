<h2 id="understand">One green extract separates into several bands</h2>

Grinding disrupts leaf tissue, ethanol extracts pigments and filtration produces a mixture. The textbook uses silica to assist grinding and calcium carbonate to protect pigments. A narrow dry origin stays above the solvent reservoir. Different partitioning between stationary and moving phases produces different migration distances.

The main textbook bands, from the front toward the origin, are carotene, xanthophyll, chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b. Migration distance and darkness do not directly measure molecular size or precise abundance.

<h2 id="try">Change a condition and inspect the evidence</h2>

- Submerge the origin and observe pigment dissolving into the reservoir.
- Widen the starting line and compare separation clarity.
- Change wavelength and compare absorption with transmission.
- Double concentration and check the Beer–Lambert relation rather than expecting transmission to simply halve.

Required 1, Chapter 5, Section 4, pp. 98–99. Extraction and separation is the formal inquiry; absorption spectra are a research demonstration.

<h2 id="deeper">Measured data and illustrative parameters</h2>

<details>
<summary>What spectral data are shown?</summary>

Extinction coefficients come from PhotochemCAD 2.1a / OMLC, interpolated at 5 nm intervals over 400–700 nm. Chlorophyll a and b use diethyl ether; beta-carotene uses hexane. Path length is 1 cm and default concentration is 5 µM. The model calculates A = εcl, T = 10⁻ᴬ and absorption fraction 1 − T, ignoring scattering, reflection and fluorescence. Provenance accompanies the model data.

These are solution absorption spectra, not a leaf action spectrum. Screen colors approximate spectral colors. The solvent front follows a capillary square-root time relation; four migration factors are illustrative, not standards for arbitrary solvents. The main pigment categories do not mean all plants contain only four pigment molecules.

</details>
