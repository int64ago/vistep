<h2 id="understand">Understand the principle</h2>

Three positions from PEP Selective Compulsory 3 remain distinct: polymerization mechanisms on pages 132–134, phenolic-resin experiment 5-1 on pages 140–141, and superabsorbent inquiry on pages 147–148. A mechanism drawing is not a performed experiment.

The addition view retains eight carbons and four chlorines from four vinyl chloride units in a PVC chain segment. End groups and initiation, transfer and termination details are omitted. Partly transparent transitional bonds are graphical interpolation, not stable molecules or measured trajectories. Real polymers have chain-length distributions and statistical average molecular masses.

The condensation view magnifies one net ester-forming event and counts an open chain made from n diacids plus n diols. Joining 2n molecules into one acyclic chain needs 2n − 1 ester links and releases the same number of waters. Carboxyl and hydroxyl ends remain. R and R′ stand for the rest of each molecule. Oxygen tracking represents net atom accounting, not all proton transfers or isotope exchange.

<h2 id="try">Try it yourself</h2>

Experiment 5-1 compares acid and base catalysis in a water bath. The subsequent textbook model associates acid catalysis and equimolar phenol/formaldehyde with linear resin; the network explanation additionally requires excess formaldehyde and further reaction under base catalysis. A catalyst switch alone is not sufficient. Bridges occupy ortho/para positions while phenolic hydroxyls persist. Brown crosslinks are covalent. Real resins have distributed structures and additional link types; six rings form an illustrative planar fragment. Colors and curing progress are not measured kinetics.

The inquiry compares 1.0 g dry samples with 70 mL water, followed by stirring, a 5 min rest and removal of unabsorbed water. The model assumes capacities of 50 g/g for resin and 12 g/g for cotton. These invented samples teach measurement; they are neither textbook results nor product specifications. Water density is approximated as 1 g/mL, with no evaporation, dissolved loss or squeezing.

Raise capacity above 70 g/g and finite supply still caps observed uptake at 70 g. Changing beaker tare changes gross balance readings, not correctly calculated uptake. Incomplete draining leaves free water on the balance and inflates apparent absorption.

<h2 id="deeper">Go deeper</h2>

Absorbed water = min(initial water, dry mass × assumed capacity) × teaching uptake progress. Free water is initial minus absorbed water; drained water is free water times draining progress. Gross balance mass includes tare, dry sample, absorbed water and residual free water. The ratio (m₂ − m₁)/m₁ is expressed in g/g without multiplying by 100. A percentage requires an explicitly different presentation.

Hydrophilic segments interact with water while sparse crosslinks preserve an insoluble network. Segment and link identities persist in the drawing. Counterions, electrostatic screening, mixing free energy and elastic equilibrium are not solved. Salt and crosslink density affect actual uptake, but no arbitrary formula is presented as a quantitative material prediction. A highly crosslinked thermoset cannot flow again like an uncrosslinked thermoplastic melt; stronger heating may cause decomposition.
