<h2 id="understand">Is the photograph being printed?</h2>

Follow one sheet of **Polaroid color i-Type integral film**. Before exposure, the sheet already contains photosensitive material, dyes and an image-receiving system. The camera records light, then sends that same sheet through rollers. The image forms inside the sealed film, without inkjet printing or peeling away the negative. Other instant-film systems may use different structures and chemistry; this explanation does not combine Instax with Polaroid.

Opening the shutter projects light onto the film through a folded optical path and the transparent cover sheet. Exposure creates a **latent image** in the silver-halide layers of the negative. That record is not yet a visible finished print, and the chemistry pod remains sealed. [Polaroid's process explanation](https://support.polaroid.com/hc/en-us/articles/115012396647-What-happens-when-I-insert-a-Polaroid-film-pack-into-my-camera) separates exposure from processing.

After the shutter closes, the same sheet advances continuously. The foil pod in its wide border reaches two metal rollers. Compression opens the pod and spreads reagent between the negative and image-receiving parts. The spreading front follows the same sheet coordinate; it cannot pass a region that has not reached the nip. [Polaroid's film construction overview](https://support.polaroid.com/hc/en-us/articles/115012554908-What-s-inside-a-Polaroid-film-box) identifies the negative, transparent cover and lower-border pod.

The paste contains more than developer: white pigment, opacifying dyes, alkali and a thickener also matter. The color negative uses photosensitive layers with cyan, magenta and yellow dye systems. Development controls which dyes can migrate into the receiving layer to make a positive image viewed through the transparent side. The scene reduces this to a conservative CMY teaching model rather than a manufacturer's formula. [Polaroid's account of modern color-film layers](https://www.polaroid.com/en_gb/blog/journal/the-reclaimed-blue-story) describes those systems; [a Polaroid diffusion-transfer patent](https://patents.justia.com/patent/5449586) explains exposure-dependent dye immobilization and migration.

**Ejected does not mean developed.** The current color i-Type specification gives a typical **10–15 minutes**; use the time on your film packaging. The film compresses that wait into tens of seconds. In the gradual-development chapter, a minute clock continuously shows the elapsed chemical time. It is not a real experiment that finishes in a few seconds. [The product specification](https://shop-us.polaroid.com/products/color-itype-instant-film) provides the development range.

<h2 id="try">Control transport and waiting separately</h2>

- In “Rollers and pod”, move the feed distance. Follow the same pod toward the nip, through compression and rupture. Both roller angles come from sheet travel, with opposite signs.
- Pause halfway through “Spread the reagent”. Processed area turns blue; the region not yet through the rollers remains unprocessed.
- In the layer or developing view, change elapsed time. At zero minutes the film shows the opacifying paste. Waiting changes dye distributions and reveals the image.
- Add one roller spot. It returns to the sheet once per turn, producing repeated coverage defects. Clean and dirty processing use identical exposure inputs. Cleaning the roller does not repair an already damaged print.

<h2 id="deeper">Why doesn't shaking help?</h2>

<details>
<summary>Can freshly ejected film face bright light?</summary>

Opacifying chemistry helps protect the still-sensitive negative, but it does not make bright-light exposure harmless. The camera's film shield protects the first moments; continue shielding according to the film packaging, for example by laying the picture flat and face down. Do not shake, bend or squeeze it: wet processing layers can be disturbed. [Polaroid's shielding guidance](https://support.polaroid.com/hc/en-us/articles/115012519828-How-to-get-the-most-out-of-Polaroid-film) and [its explanation of shaking damage](https://support.polaroid.com/hc/en-us/articles/4507821657106-Why-do-my-photos-have-distortions-and-blobs-on-them) give the practical limits.

The translucent cutaway makes the protection visible. It does not suggest opening or dissecting the sheet; the chemical layers stay sealed inside.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why do roller defects repeat?</summary>

In an ideal no-slip model, sheet travel `d` rotates a roller of radius `r` through `d/r` radians:

<div className="formula">θupper = d/r　θlower = −d/r　repeat spacing = 2πr</div>

The teaching radius is 8 mm, so a single returning spot contacts the sheet about every 50.3 mm. That is not a specification for every real camera. Polaroid identifies dirty or uneven rollers as a cause of uneven reagent coverage, spots, streaks and missing areas. This model selects one local spot to explain periodicity rather than simulating every defect. [The roller-cleaning guide](https://support.polaroid.com/hc/en-us/articles/115012564148-How-do-I-clean-my-camera-rollers) covers real maintenance.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Which dimensions and equations are illustrative?</summary>

The sheet uses Polaroid's published dimensions: 88.47 × 107.52 mm overall, with an image area about 76.801 × 78.94 mm. Product pages may round these values. [Polaroid's dimensions article](https://support.polaroid.com/hc/en-us/articles/115012363647-What-are-Polaroid-photo-dimensions) is the geometric reference. Pod placement, cutaway camera, roller radius, paper and paste thickness are disclosed teaching choices, not a replica of one manufactured camera.

The model retains one sheet, tangency at the nip, continuous transport, opposite roller phases, a causal spreading front and illustrative reagent-volume conservation. Each CMY channel has normalized total inventory 1, conserved between immobilized dye, dye received in the positive layer and the remaining pool. Rate constants, the opacifying appearance and subtractive color mixing are teaching approximations. They do not predict real print color, temperature effects, aging, lateral diffusion or a proprietary recipe. Microscopic thickness is enlarged, and the integral sheet remains intact.

</details>
