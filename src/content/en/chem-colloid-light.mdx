<section id="understand">
## Look from the side

Salt solution and iron hydroxide colloid both transmit light. When viewed perpendicular to a red incident beam, however, the colloid reveals a distinct light path. Dispersed particles redirect some of the light toward the observer: the Tyndall effect. The sample is scattering incident light, not emitting its own light.

The textbook first forms a red-brown iron hydroxide colloid and then compares it with sodium chloride solution. Colour growth represents preparation qualitatively; actual preparation conditions and particle distributions affect the observation.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Change one factor at a time

Reduce particle volume fraction and compare the side beam with transmitted light. Then hold the fraction fixed and change particle radius. Larger particles are fewer in number at a fixed fraction, but each scatters much more strongly. Finally compare red and blue illumination.

The controls operate on a transparent-sphere analogy. Their values are not measurements of the textbook iron hydroxide sample.

</section>
<section id="deeper">
<details>
<summary>Model, conservation and limits</summary>

The Rayleigh cross section scales with radius to the sixth power and inverse wavelength to the fourth power. With fixed particle volume fraction, number density decreases with radius cubed, so the scattering coefficient increases with radius cubed. Exponential attenuation determines transmission; transmitted and total scattered fractions add to one.

The model neglects absorption, interactions and multiple scattering. A real red-brown colloid is not a collection of perfectly transparent spheres. Display brightness is amplified, and the observer receives only a small portion of all scattered light. Solutions also scatter weakly: “not obvious” does not mean “zero scattering.”
