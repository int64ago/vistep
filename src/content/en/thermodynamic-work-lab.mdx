<h2 id="understand">Different work paths can produce the same internal-energy change</h2>

Rapid compression raises a fire-piston gas’s internal energy, producing a large temperature increase when heat has little time to escape. In slow isothermal compression, the input work is transferred to the surroundings as heat and temperature can remain nearly constant.

Joule’s mechanical calorimeter transmits falling-weight energy through ropes, a shaft and submerged paddles. Equal mgh gives equal temperature rise in the insulated limit. The electrical arrangement also uses a falling weight, driving a generator and an immersed resistor. It retains the textbook’s mechanical–electrical–internal-energy path.

Rapid escape of moist bottle gas cools the remaining gas and can form fog. Once the stopper leaves, this is an open system with changing gas mass. Rumford’s boring observations show that continuing mechanical work can keep producing internal energy even when the tool becomes blunt.

<h2 id="try">Compare energy across apparatuses</h2>

- Compare adiabatic and isothermal compression to the same volume.
- Halve paddle-driving mass and double its descent, then compare final temperatures.
- Increase generator descent duration: current decreases while delivered energy stays equal.
- Observe bottle pressure, gas amount and temperature decreasing together.
- Continue frictional heating through boiling and account for vaporization energy.

<h2 id="deeper">Each apparatus keeps its own model</h2>

<div className="formula">Adiabatic: TV⁰·⁴ = constant; Joule calorimetry: mgh = I²Rt = CΔT</div>

Compression starts with 30 mL ideal air at 293.15 K and 100 kPa, heat-capacity ratio 1.4 and maximum compression ratio ten. The 500 K visual ignition trigger is illustrative, not a nitrocellulose material constant. Combustion feedback is omitted.

The calorimeter contains 0.05 kg water with specific heat 4180 J/(kg·K), cup heat capacity 20 J/K, an ideal generator and a 4 Ω resistor. Bearing and external circuit losses are omitted. Shaft angle comes from descent and a 4 cm drum radius; the same mgh determines temperature rise.

Bottle blowdown assumes an adiabatic well-mixed rigid tank: T/T₀ = (p/p₀)²⁄⁷ and remaining mass fraction is (p/p₀)⁵⁄⁷. Pressure falls from 120 to 101.325 kPa. A near-room-temperature vapor-pressure relation indicates condensation. Latent-heat feedback is omitted, so displayed temperatures are ideal-model values rather than predictions for a real bottle.

The Rumford illustration uses torque 10 N·m, angular speed 12 rad/s and combined heat capacity 4300 J/K. After 100 °C, additional work contributes to vaporization. These are teaching parameters, not original measurements. Barrel rotation is slowed and a cutaway exposes contact.
