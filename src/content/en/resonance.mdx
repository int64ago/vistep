<h2 id="understand">How can a small push build a large motion?</h2>

Give a spring-mounted mass a displacement and release it. Its free motion reveals a natural rhythm. With a repeating applied force, the eventual motion follows the driving frequency, while its amplitude depends on frequency and damping.

The gold segment is the applied force. The power strip is calculated from force times velocity. A force along the motion adds energy; a force against it removes energy. Near the natural frequency, successive cycles can supply energy efficiently. Damping removes energy until the input and loss balance over a cycle.

<h2 id="try">Keep the push and change its rhythm</h2>

After the film, use exploration to vary frequency and damping. The time control reconstructs the exact motion from rest, including the growing transient.

- Compare frequency ratios of 0.5 and 1 with the same applied force.
- Keep the ratio at 1 and increase damping. The steady response falls.
- Raise the ratio to 1.7. The displacement increasingly opposes the applied force.

<h2 id="deeper">The displacement peak is slightly shifted</h2>

The model solves m x″ + b x′ + k x = F₀ cos(ωt), with m = 1 kg and f₀ = 1 Hz. The damping ratio is ζ = b/(2√(mk)). The fixed force satisfies F₀/k = 0.2 m. All shots use the same displacement scale.

The steady amplitude is A = (F₀/k) / √[(1−r²)²+(2ζr)²], where r = f/f₀. For light damping, its maximum occurs at r = √(1−2ζ²), slightly below 1. The demonstration therefore distinguishes a peak near the natural frequency from an exact universal equality.

Instantaneous input power is Fv and dissipated power is b v². The frequency scan compares independent steady solutions rather than pretending to simulate a fast sweep. Large-deformation nonlinearities, dry friction and structural failure are outside this linear, single-mode teaching model.
