<h2 id="understand">Removing one cue does not remove every alternative</h2>

Mesh prevents direct insect contact while allowing airborne chemicals through, and may leave visual cues intact. To test chemical information, compare an occupied opaque chamber with an empty chamber while matching airflow, containers, temperature, humidity and handling. Remove residues, swap positions and repeat independently. The bent duct blocks direct sight while retaining an air pathway; its cross-section and odor dots are explanatory diagrams.

Silkmoths provide a specific example: female-released bombykol can elicit male orientation. The domesticated male silkmoth is shown walking toward the source. Original research also tests receptor specificity and behavior, but this student design does not reproduce that genetic study or establish one universal signal for all insects. [Sakurai and colleagues’ silkmoth research](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pgen.1002115)

The 70% and 20% values are labeled hypothetical practice results. Personal records begin blank. Define orientation before testing and record responding individuals, totals and trial conditions. A single non-response does not by itself mean that no information was detected.

<h2 id="try">Separate resistance, recovery and feedback</h2>

An increase in herbivores can increase grazing pressure and predator responses, which constrain the original increase. Symbol size represents normalized population indices, not growth of individual animals. The model’s delays and coefficients explain feedback rather than predict real populations.

Resistance concerns initial displacement under a disturbance; recovery concerns return afterward. Separate controls change the initial drop and recovery rate. A third view introduces an illustrative threshold: strong disturbance can move the system toward a different state. The value 0.25 is not a real-world safety boundary. Species richness or food-web complexity alone does not guarantee repair after any disturbance.

<h2 id="deeper">Tank stability requires continuing real observations</h2>

The textbook example combines a sloping substrate of stones, sand and humus-rich soil with water, terrestrial and aquatic plants and an appropriate consumer component. Suggested organisms are choices, not a list to stock in full. Explain composition and proportions, add the cover, use indirect light and record organism types and numbers at least weekly. The animation assembles the components, with water and terrain meeting at a shared geometric intersection.

Dissolved oxygen reflects photosynthesis, respiration and gas exchange. Plants continue to respire at night. Flow, temperature and other conditions also affect natural waters, so one daytime measurement cannot represent the entire day. [National Park Service aquatic-ecology guide](https://www.nps.gov/romo/learn/education/upload/Aquatic-Ecology-Teacher-Guide-Final.pdf) [USGS explanation of daily oxygen variation](https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/FS-060-99/)

The short-term model uses 1 L water and 0.3 L internal air, initially containing 8 mg and 84 mg oxygen respectively. Gas exchange redistributes oxygen internally; photosynthesis adds and respiration removes oxygen. Each milligram of oxygen corresponds to 0.375 mg carbon transferred in the simplified reaction, preserving a combined 132 mg of inorganic and organic carbon. External oxygen does not cross the sealed lid, while light can enter: closure to matter is not closure to energy.

Consumer icons represent a load index and producer amount is specified. The 72-hour illustration omits long-term reproduction, death, nutrient limitation and community succession. **It is neither a stocking prescription nor proof of permanent balance.** Higher load or reduced light changes oxygen budgets, but long-term stability still requires weekly journals, comparable photographs, environmental records and attention to unusual changes. Stable systems need not preserve every individual or every reading unchanged.
