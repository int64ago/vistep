title: 'How can impurities be removed without leaving new ones?'

<section id="understand">

Experimental Activity4 removes Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ from crude salt. Dissolving transfers soluble material into water; it does not separate those components. Chemical precipitation changes selected impurities into solids, which filtration can then remove.

BaCl₂ transfers sulfate into BaSO₄. NaOH transfers magnesium into Mg(OH)₂. Na₂CO₃ addresses both calcium and excess barium. Once the solids are removed, acid treats excess hydroxide and carbonate in the filtrate. Evaporation then removes water from the treated solution.

</section>
<section id="try">

Follow the textbook sequence and its dissolved-ion inventory. Too little barium leaves sulfate that produces new cloudiness in a supernatant test. Omitting carbonate leaves calcium and excess barium. Acidifying before filtration toward a neutral endpoint redissolves some precipitates, returning impurity ions to the liquid that passes through the paper.

Displayed amounts are arbitrary molar units, not experimental quantities. They track the same material through different routes. Independent inventories check calcium, magnesium, barium, sulfur, carbon, sodium, chlorine and total charge.

</section>
<section id="deeper">
<details>
<summary>Order, excess and completion tests</summary>

A slight reagent excess helps complete the targeted precipitation but creates later cleanup tasks. Carbonate removes excess barium; acid after filtration removes excess hydroxide and carbonate. This film follows the textbook sequence without claiming that every pair of reagents has only one valid order.

Completion tests use the clear supernatant after settling. A separate small sample receives a probe dose; new solid equals the smaller of remaining target-ion amount and probe amount. In reality, no visible new cloudiness means below the observation threshold, not mathematically zero concentration. The ideal model represents complete reaction as zero for clarity.

Carbonate and acid produce bubbles; hydroxide neutralisation does not. The textbook therefore checks pH paper as well as bubbling, seeking neutral or slightly acidic filtrate. Bubble number is not converted into an actual pH.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Inventory model and boundaries</summary>

Reference amounts are Na⁺21.6, Cl⁻24, Ca²⁺1, Mg²⁺1 and SO₄²⁻0.8. Add Ba²⁺1, OH⁻2.4 and CO₃²⁻1.6, with their accompanying sodium and chloride. Complete precipitation gives BaSO₄0.8, Mg(OH)₂1, CaCO₃1 and BaCO₃0.2. Correct filtration leaves OH⁻0.4 and CO₃²⁻0.4, requiring H⁺1.2 at the ideal neutralisation endpoint. Final sodium and chloride amounts are both27.2. These arbitrary quantities form an independent arithmetic benchmark.

The acid-first comparison acidifies the unfiltered suspension toward the same ideal endpoint and therefore consumes more acid. BaSO₄ stays solid; magnesium hydroxide and the two carbonates redissolve. Calcium1, magnesium1 and barium0.2 remain in the filtrate. This does not claim that an identical tiny acid dose dissolves every precipitate.

Precipitation is idealised as complete. The model does not solve solubility products, ion activities, coprecipitation, colloidal filtration, solution volumes, crystal-solubility curves or recovery yield. Allocating carbonate first to calcium and then to barium only directs the teaching animation; the excess endpoint removes both and the animated order is not measured selectivity. The film does not extend to complex magnesium carbonate formation if hydroxide is omitted.

Filtration is an ideal phase separation. Evaporation depicts decreasing water and retained nonvolatile material, without predicting real crystallisation kinetics or a purity percentage. Actual recovered salt mass also depends on reagent-derived sodium and chloride, handling losses and residual impurities.

</details>
