<h2 id="understand">Heating and enzymes both speed reactions. What changes?</h2>

The four tubes begin with equal peroxide. Heating makes higher-energy states more accessible. At the same temperature, catalysis offers a lower-barrier pathway. An enzyme participates and is regenerated after turnover; it is not extra fuel. For a fixed initial substrate amount, complete conversion still obeys 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂. Faster initial bubbling does not create a larger theoretical oxygen yield.

This explanation predicts that more active enzyme can speed the approach to the endpoint. Severe heat damage can remove usable catalytic arrangements. Actual collected yield may also depend on incomplete conversion, inactivation and collection conditions.

<h2 id="try">How can cold slowing be distinguished from damage?</h2>

Pretreat the enzyme, bring every sample to the same temperature, then add equal fresh substrate. A cold sample that retained its catalytic structure can recover. A sample with persistent heat damage may not. Matching the current conditions makes the remaining difference evidence about prior state. Exploration integrates exposure duration rather than remembering only the highest temperature reached.

pH can change the protonation and charge of groups needed for catalysis. Extreme conditions can additionally damage the fold. Each pH condition needs its own no-enzyme blank; low oxygen release alone does not distinguish these causes. The two-group diagram is a generic explanatory model, not a claimed molecular description of liver catalase.

<h2 id="deeper">What does each line of evidence establish?</h2>

A cage blocks direct grinding but admits gastric fluid. Digestion inside rules out grinding as the only cause. Cell-free yeast extract can still ferment, showing that intact living cells need not remain present, but the extract is still a mixture. Protein properties and urea-breakdown activity provide complementary evidence about urease crystals. Neither substitutes for the other. Product speciation depends on solution conditions; the scene does not invent a bubbling urease assay.

Amylase requires suitable linkage chemistry and several contacts that position a glucan segment productively. Water participates in net hydrolysis, shorter fragments leave, and the enzyme can be reused. The glucose–fructose linkage in sucrose does not form the same productive arrangement. Specificity involves chemical groups and dynamic structure, not shape alone. Starch itself has reducing ends; an operationally negative textbook assay is not a claim that starch has no reducing character whatsoever.
