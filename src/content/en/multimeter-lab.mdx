<h2 id="understand">The head measures its own current</h2>

How can one pointer measure voltage, current and resistance? Follow the quantity it actually responds to: **current through the moving coil**. A different internal circuit converts each external quantity into head current.

- **Voltage mode:** a large series resistor takes most of the voltage. The complete meter connects across the target, and its current depends on terminal voltage.
- **Current mode:** a low-resistance shunt bypasses the sensitive head. The two branch currents recombine to give the external current.
- **Resistance mode:** an internal cell supplies test current through an isolated specimen. A larger specimen resistance produces less head current.

This teaching head has a **50 µA full-scale current** and **2 kΩ resistance**. Its 5 V range adds 98 kΩ in series; its 0.5 A range adds about 0.20002 Ω in parallel. These are explanatory circuit choices, not a complete manufacturer schematic. Moving wire marks indicate conventional current direction, not carrier speed or a count of charges.

<h2 id="try">Predict before changing the range</h2>

With almost unchanged terminal voltage, switching from the 5 V range to 10 V increases resistance and roughly halves deflection. The new range still indicates almost the same voltage. The qualification matters: finite meter resistance can also change the circuit being measured.

The ohm scale cannot be divided uniformly. Let calibrated internal resistance be R₀. Shorted probes produce maximum current. Adding R₀ makes total resistance 2R₀ and deflection one half. Adding 2R₀ makes the total 3R₀, so deflection is one third, not one quarter.

Changing the multiplier changes the internal circuit, so short-circuit zero needs checking again. The resistor, cold filament, hand contacts and diode apply the same principle under different conditions. A cold filament differs from a hot one; contact resistance is not a personal constant; a diode’s apparent resistance depends on test conditions.

**The black lead supplies positive potential in this modeled analog ohmmeter.** This convention is supported by the cited HIOKI 3007 resistance instructions. It is not a universal rule for analog or digital meters.

<h2 id="deeper">Voltage can exist without a working lamp current</h2>

<details>
<summary>The meter becomes part of the fault circuit</summary>

A nearly open contact has high resistance and may carry most of the circuit voltage. A voltmeter connected across it adds a small parallel current path. The diagram reports meter current and filament current separately. Voltage alone does not establish that the filament has enough current to light normally.

Textbook page 74 compares a depleted cell, bad switch contact and bad socket contact. The retained broken-wire example also relates to the exercise on page 76. Those candidates narrow a diagnosis; they do not exclude a broken filament or every other real fault.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Equivalent circuits and limits</summary>

Voltage sensitivity is 20 kΩ/V, corresponding to 50 µA at full scale. Ammeter input resistance comes from the actual parallel head-and-shunt circuit. The lamp example uses a 3 V source with 0.5 Ω internal resistance and a fixed 20 Ω filament equivalent; it is not a thermal simulation.

Ohms mode has a 1.5 V cell. Multiplier m sets R₀ = 20m Ω. The head and shunt have parallel equivalent Rₚ = R₀/15, leaving series resistance R₀ − Rₚ. All four multipliers therefore use the same physical head. Zero adjustment changes the series part. Supported multipliers stop at 1000; this passive topology cannot be extended without limit.

The diode uses a forward exponential model and small reverse leakage, excluding breakdown. Hands use an equivalent contact resistance, without health inference. Bad contacts use a large but finite 1 GΩ resistance. Every probe change recalculates the complete loaded network.

The 20 kΩ/V reference concerns HIOKI 3030-10/3008. The 3007 has a different sensitivity; its manual is cited only for the specified ohms polarity and semiconductor-test conventions. All readings here are model calculations under declared conditions, not independent observations.

</details>
