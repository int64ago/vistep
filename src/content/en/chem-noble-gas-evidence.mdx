<h2 id="understand">Understand the principle</h2>

Three historical-research positions from PEP Selective Compulsory 2 pages 26–27 are retained: helium spectroscopy, argon density/separation evidence and the first noble-gas compound. These are reconstructions of evidence, not student experiments presented as completed work.

The representative He I line uses the NIST value 587.56148 nm; sodium references are 588.995 and 589.5924 nm. A narrow 584–592 nm window uses Gaussian instrumental broadening and normalized intensities, omitting helium fine-structure splitting. It is not a full solar absorption spectrum, radiative-transfer calculation or reconstruction of the original instrument. The solar-edge row represents the emission-line clue relevant to discovery. One matching line is significant but full identification also depends on other lines, calibration, sample provenance and alternative explanations.

The textbook densities are 1.2508 g/L for chemically prepared nitrogen and 1.2572 g/L for an air-derived sample. At equal conditions, 1 L differs by 0.0064 g, or 6.4 mg. These historical textbook values are not refitted as a modern atmospheric composition. Markers in the bulbs denote component types, not molecule ratios.

<h2 id="try">Try it yourself</h2>

Width changes actually broaden the calculated spectrum; a sample calibration offset breaks alignment of otherwise identical helium lines. Changing both sample volumes scales the mass difference. The temperature counterexample assumes a common 273.15 K reference and ideal-gas constant-pressure density, ρ(T)=ρ₀T₀/T. This is a teaching extension, not the textbook's documented measurement temperature. A small mismatch can reverse the difference.

The separation drawing assumes water and carbon dioxide have already been removed. An illustrative 100 parts consist of N₂ 78, O₂ 21 and Ar 1, not precision modern air data. Gas remaining plus captured products stays at 100 each frame; argon remains 1. The closed loop and two beds express selective-removal logic, not a reconstruction of all original reagents, heating controls or pumping. A residual gas still requires independent identification evidence.

Xenon markers pass through the joining neck only after the seal opens. Colors and solid growth follow teaching progress, not a kinetic or yield model. Markers preserve the destination of xenon without specifying the historical product stoichiometry.

<h2 id="deeper">Go deeper</h2>

Bartlett's comparison involved **molecular O₂** and **atomic Xe**, not atomic oxygen. Modern NIST reference ionization energies are approximately 12.0697 eV and 12.12984 eV. Their proximity motivates a hypothesis but does not alone determine free energy, rate or final composition.

The original material was formulated as XePtF₆; later research showed greater complexity in the Xe/PtF₆ system. The scene therefore shows the formation of orange-yellow xenon-containing material without presenting a definitive single Xe⁺[PtF₆]⁻ lattice. Later-confirmed linear XeF₂ is a different compound, not another name for that first material. Lone pairs and detailed bonding are omitted.

A stable electron shell does not mean absolute inertness under every condition. Broad textbook statements about the noble-gas family need specific modern research conditions. This scene does not claim that every noble gas readily forms ordinary stable compounds at room temperature and pressure, and it does not assign helium eight outer-shell electrons.
