<section id="understand">
## Build the conclusion from two observations

The chloride comparison follows the textbook samples: dilute hydrochloric acid, sodium chloride and sodium carbonate. Silver nitrate precipitates solids in all three. Subsequent dilute nitric acid leaves AgCl but removes Ag₂CO₃, without introducing extra chloride.

The sulfate comparison uses dilute sulfuric acid, sodium sulfate and sodium carbonate. Barium chloride produces white precipitates; dilute hydrochloric acid leaves BaSO₄ but dissolves BaCO₃ with carbon dioxide evolution. Both experiments connect a first observation to a discriminating second response.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Keep the carbonate control

Pause while every tube contains a precipitate, then observe what acid changes. Exploration varies the precipitating ion and subsequent hydrogen-ion amounts. Too little acid can leave carbonate solid behind. Each tube begins with 1 mmol of its relevant anion, and displayed solid amounts follow stoichiometry.

Pale colour is only a clue. The textbook describes the chloride-series precipitates as white, while silver carbonate may appear pale yellow. The display does not use this subtle colour difference as the decisive test. These are supervised laboratory comparisons, including the handling of soluble barium compounds.

</section>
<section id="deeper">
<details>
<summary>Stoichiometry and limits of identification</summary>

AgCl and BaSO₄ form with one precipitating ion per anion; Ag₂CO₃ needs two silver ions, while BaCO₃ needs one barium ion. Converting 1 mmol of carbonate consumes 2 mmol of hydrogen ions and produces 1 mmol of carbon dioxide. Carbon and precipitating-metal totals remain conserved. Small equilibrium solubilities, nucleation rates and intermediate acid–base species are omitted; this model does not calculate detection limits.

These known-sample comparisons are not complete procedures for arbitrary mixtures. Other precipitates and reagent contamination can require further separation. [Truman State University](https://chemlab.truman.edu/chemical-principles/inorganic-quantitative-analysis/) explains selective precipitation and acidification; the [RSC experiment](https://edu.rsc.org/experiments/testing-salts-for-anions-and-cations/464.article) gives corresponding anion tests. Textbook positions: Required Book 1, page 49 (PDF page 54), and Required Book 2, page 6 (PDF page 11).

</details>
