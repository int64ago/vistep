<section id="understand">
## Build the conclusion from two observations

The chloride comparison follows the textbook samples: dilute hydrochloric acid, sodium chloride and sodium carbonate. Silver nitrate precipitates solids in all three. Subsequent dilute nitric acid leaves AgCl but removes Ag₂CO₃, without introducing extra chloride.

The sulfate comparison uses dilute sulfuric acid, sodium sulfate and sodium carbonate. Barium chloride produces white precipitates; dilute hydrochloric acid leaves BaSO₄ but dissolves BaCO₃ with carbon dioxide evolution. Both experiments connect a first observation to a discriminating second response.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Keep the carbonate control

Pause while every tube contains a precipitate, then observe what acid changes. Exploration varies the precipitating-ion amount and a net hydrogen-ion budget. Each tube begins with 1 mmol of its relevant anion. The display allocates the budget to complete net conversions; an insufficient budget cannot support conversion of all the carbonate.

Pale colour is only a clue. The textbook describes the chloride-series precipitates as white, while silver carbonate may appear pale yellow. The display does not use this subtle colour difference as the decisive test. These are supervised laboratory comparisons, including the handling of soluble barium compounds.

</section>
<section id="deeper">
<details>
<summary>Stoichiometry and limits of identification</summary>

AgCl and BaSO₄ form with one precipitating ion per anion; Ag₂CO₃ needs two silver ions, while BaCO₃ needs one barium ion. Converting 1 mmol of carbonate consumes 2 mmol of hydrogen ions and produces 1 mmol of carbon dioxide. Carbon and precipitating-metal totals remain conserved. This is an endpoint net-stoichiometric allocation, not the species distribution during gradual acid addition. Bicarbonate, pH, small equilibrium solubilities and nucleation rates are not solved. Partial states must not be read as a titration curve. Gas markers make one finite exit and remain outside; they do not imply ongoing gas production.

These known-sample comparisons are not complete procedures for arbitrary mixtures. Other precipitates and reagent contamination can require further separation. The [Pearson practical sheet](https://www.pearson.com/content/dam/one-dot-com/one-dot-com/uk/documents/subjects/science/GCSE-core-practical-sheets/Chemistry/cp7b-identifying-ions-negative.pdf) explicitly uses acid before the precipitating reagent for unknown-sample tests and asks why carbonate interference matters. The textbook demonstration deliberately precipitates first to expose that interference; its following guidance normally acidifies first. Neither order constitutes a complete analysis of arbitrary mixtures. Textbook positions: Required Book 1, page 49 (PDF page 54), and Required Book 2, page 6 (PDF page 11).

</details>
