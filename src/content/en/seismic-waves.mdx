<h2 id="understand">Does rock travel all the way with the wave?</h2>

Seismic body waves transmit a disturbance without carrying a block of rock to the receiving station. P waves move material along the propagation direction, creating compression and dilation. S waves move it across that direction, creating shear. The gold marker always identifies the same material point; its dashed ring shows the undisturbed position.

For the same event, P commonly arrives before S. Both synthetic traces use the same source time and distance, calculated from the same packets driving the particle fields.

<h2 id="try">Move the station farther away</h2>

Vary distance, elapsed time and solid versus ideal-fluid behavior. Greater distance delays both arrivals and widens their time difference. Setting shear modulus to zero removes the propagating S wave while retaining compressional P propagation.

This is not an earthquake prediction tool. Arrival differences must be combined with a velocity structure and multiple stations to constrain a source. One P–S interval cannot identify a unique point on a map.

<h2 id="deeper">Material properties determine the speeds</h2>

In a homogeneous isotropic linear elastic medium, vP = √[(K + 4G/3)/ρ] and vS = √(G/ρ). K is bulk modulus, G shear modulus and ρ density. Defaults are K = 50 GPa, G = 30 GPa and ρ = 2700 kg/m³, a teaching example rather than a fitted local Earth model.

Straight-path arrivals are tP = d/vP and tS = d/vS. Their difference d(1/vS−1/vP) increases with distance. An ideal fluid has G = 0 and cannot sustain an elastic shear body wave; that does not rule out fluid motion or surface waves.

The packet is a smoothly windowed six-second sinusoid, with greatly exaggerated displacement. This demonstration excludes rupture, Earth curvature, reflection, refraction, attenuation, surface waves, magnitude and damage. Synthetic trace amplitude should not be interpreted as real hazard.
