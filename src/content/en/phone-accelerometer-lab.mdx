<h2 id="understand">Still reads 9.8; why does falling read zero?</h2>

Follow **one proof mass inside the sensor**. An elastic suspension connects it to the case, beside fixed sensing electrodes. When a hand supports the phone, gravity displaces the mass downward relative to the supported case. Elastic force acts upward. Unequal electrode gaps produce unequal capacitances, encoding relative displacement. The sensor does not read distance travelled or speed.

After support is removed, the case begins falling and the internal deformation relaxes. Once settled, the mass is near its unstrained center and the raw signal is approximately zero. Gravity remains, and downward speed keeps increasing. **Zero signal means neither zero gravity nor zero coordinate acceleration.**

A catch reverses the change. The hand contacts the phone before slowing its case. The proof mass lags, moving farther down relative to the case, and the signal increases. Velocity can still point down while acceleration points up: downward speed is decreasing.

This is a **single-axis mechanical equivalent** with an elastic suspension and differential capacitance. Real MEMS beams, combs, electrodes and electronics vary. Micrometre displacement is magnified; this is not the exact interior of every phone. [Analog Devices explains the sensing mechanism](https://www.analog.com/en/resources/technical-articles/accelerometer-and-gyroscopes-sensors-operation-sensing-and-applications.html), including suspended mass, fixed electrodes and differential capacitance.

<h2 id="try">Use counterexamples to interpret the signal</h2>

**Rest and free fall.** Choose “Inside the sensor” in exploration. At 0.20 s, the modeled raw signal is 9.8 m/s²; at 0.55 s it is near zero. Inspect 0, 0.64 and 3.50 ms after release. Both mass and signal change continuously; changing a chapter label does not teleport the mass to its center.

**Catching a downward-moving phone.** At 0.78 s, velocity is −1.47 m/s while acceleration is +18.375 m/s². Reducing downward speed requires upward acceleration. The green raw signal and gold corrected signal in “Same-time traces” share one clock.

**A lift that keeps rising.** During startup, cruising and braking, the phone may move upward throughout, yet settled raw signals are 10.8, 9.8 and 8.8 m/s². With orientation fixed, subtracting 9.8 gives +1, 0 and −1 m/s². Steady upward travel still needs support; slowing that upward travel requires downward acceleration.

The falling-phone graph on textbook pages 49–50 has the meaning of coordinate acceleration, rather than the values expected from every raw accelerometer mode. This does not identify the app’s particular filtering pipeline. [Android’s official documentation](https://developer.android.com/develop/sensors-and-location/sensors/sensors_motion) distinguishes accelerometer output with a gravity reference from linear acceleration without that reference.

<h2 id="deeper">From structure to calculation and an independent instrument</h2>

<details>
<summary>Why does the millisecond response differ from case acceleration?</summary>

Take upward as positive. Let q be proof-mass displacement relative to the case and a the case acceleration. The equivalent dynamics are:

<div className="formula">q″ + 2ωq′ + ω²q = −(a + g)</div>

The response is critically damped. The value ω = 2π × 500 s⁻¹ is illustrative, not a phone specification. Position q and relative velocity q′ remain continuous across changes in case acceleration. At release, the case acceleration becomes −g while the mass retains its earlier elastic deformation; they do not instantly acquire the same acceleration.

The displacement-calibrated signal is f = −ω²q. Actual suspension force per unit mass is −ω²q − 2ωq′. The green arrow shows **elastic force only**, excluding damping. Damping can briefly reverse total suspension force; exploration details expose its calculated value.

The corrected signal is always the same-time value f − g. It approaches case acceleration after settling. A moving mass is never paired with an instantly idealized zero signal. The slowed internal response is explicitly timed in milliseconds; external position, internal displacement and outputs use one physical clock.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How do electrode gaps encode displacement?</summary>

At the neutral position, each clear surface gap is d₀ = 4 μm. With upward q positive, the upper gap is d₀ − q and the lower gap d₀ + q. Ideal parallel-plate capacitances are:

<div className="formula">Ctop = εA/(d₀−q), Cbottom = εA/(d₀+q)</div>

Their normalized difference is (Ctop − Cbottom)/(Ctop + Cbottom) = q/d₀. One q determines the drawn gaps, finite plate edges and output, with contact boundaries checked. Settled catch displacement is approximately −2.855 μm, smaller than the 4 μm clear gap. [Analog Devices describes differential sensing and below-resonance operation](https://www.analog.com/en/resources/analog-dialogue/articles/dual-axis-low-g-fully-integrated-accelerometers.html); the dimensions and response parameters here are teaching choices.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Can force readings determine acceleration without knowing the mass?</summary>

The exercise on textbook page 112 specifies **equal startup and braking acceleration magnitudes**. Compare settled readings for the same mass, omitting initial spring vibration:

<div className="formula">G₁ = m(g+a), G₂ = m(g−a)</div>

Adding gives 2mg; subtracting gives 2ma. Taking their ratio eliminates mass:

<div className="formula">a = g(G₁−G₂)/(G₁+G₂)</div>

Readings of 2.16 N and 1.76 N yield a = 1.00 m/s². With a known mass, each settled stage also permits a = F/m − g. This model uses 0.20 kg to obtain benchmarks of 2.16, 1.96 and 1.76 N. If startup and braking magnitudes differ, the two-reading shortcut does not recover either stage acceleration.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What lies outside this model?</summary>

Orientation stays fixed. Rotation changes gravity components, so subtracting a constant 9.8 from an arbitrary axis is insufficient. Real systems also involve orientation estimation, filtering, noise, range limits and response delay.

The prescribed trajectory holds the phone for 0.4 s, releases it for 0.3 s and catches it with 0.16 s of constant upward acceleration. A real catch is smoother. The microscopic proof mass’s back-reaction on the case is neglected. The lift rises 8 m through acceleration at +1 m/s², cruising at 2 m/s and braking at −1 m/s². The force meter shows settled-stage snapshots, not an instantaneous real spring response.

These are testable model predictions, not newly collected measurements. Understanding the principle does not require dropping a phone.

</details>
