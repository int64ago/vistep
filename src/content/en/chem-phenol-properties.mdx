<h2 id="understand">Understand the principle</h2>

This family covers PEP Selective Compulsory 3, printed pages 64–66 and the carboxylic-acid inquiry on page 75. Neutralization, substitution, complex formation and historical interventions provide different kinds of evidence.

Experiment 3-4 uses more phenol than the water dissolves at room temperature. NaOH forms phenolate and clears the mixture; hydrochloric acid restores neutral phenol. The model uses total concentration approximately 1.594 mol/L and neutral solubility approximately 0.9776 mol/L, simplified from the textbook 0.3 g and 2 mL water. It solves charge and phenol balances with fixed volume, temperature, ideal activities and pKₐ approximately 10. Actual dilution and concentrated-solution activity corrections are omitted.

CO₂ converts sodium phenolate to phenol and NaHCO₃, supporting the first acidity of carbonic acid over phenol. The product is not Na₂CO₃. That chapter uses a conditional net balance and illustrative solubility capacity rather than a full open gas–liquid carbonate equilibrium.

Experiment 3-5 is ortho/para bromination: C₆H₅OH + 3 Br₂ → C₆H₂Br₃OH↓ + 3 HBr. The white solid is 2,4,6-tribromophenol. Experiment 3-6 gives a violet iron–phenolic complex response in suitable media. It is neither the same precipitate nor a universal violet color for every phenol.

<h2 id="try">Try it yourself</h2>

Adjust base, acid and bromine progress separately. Excess bromine can retain color. Ring labels 2, 4 and 6 indicate substitution positions; fading-in bonds show net products, not simultaneous elementary reactions.

Compare formic, benzoic and oxalic acids as ideal 0.010 mol/L solutions. The pH solver includes both oxalic-acid dissociations. Approximate Kₐ values are 1.8×10⁻⁴, 6.3×10⁻⁵, and 5.9×10⁻² / 6.4×10⁻⁵, respectively. Indicator-paper colors show direction only; these are not textbook measurements. Oxalic acid has two protons neutralizable by bicarbonate. Phenol does not give similarly clear gas evolution in this comparison.

Keep or remove the saturated NaHCO₃ wash, then isolate acetic-acid vapor as a counterexample. Acetic acid with Na₂CO₃ produces CO₂; the middle bottle removes carried acetic acid before the final phenolate solution. Without it, acetic acid could directly cause cloudiness. Trapping assumes sufficient wash capacity and omits finite CO₂ dissolution losses.

<h2 id="deeper">Go deeper</h2>

The familiar acidity order concerns acetic acid, the first carbonic-acid dissociation and phenol under specified solvent conditions. Gas escape, phase separation and concentration affect the observations. Carbonic acid’s second dissociation is not folded into the same comparison.

<summary>History and model limits</summary>

The textbook introduces Lister’s antiseptic use of phenol. Museum records add his engagement with Pasteur’s work, interventions involving dressings and instruments, and clinical publications in 1867. The instrument, dressing and casebook drawings are interpretive. No patient counts, infection rates or randomized trial are fabricated. A textbook discovery narrative and documented clinical publication are not equally strong historical sources.

This is not a prescription for applying phenol to people. Its toxicity, corrosivity and wastewater hazards are part of the textbook context. Classroom experiments follow school laboratory procedures. Animated cloudiness, precipitation, color and markers are not measured kinetics or molecular images.
