<h2 id="understand">A full turn produces a small lift</h2>

A fixed nut constrains the screw through its mating thread surfaces. In this single-start square-thread model, one revolution advances the screw by one **lead**. The default lead is 4 mm, so a full turn raises the 1000 N load by only 4 mm.

Unwrap one helical turn and it becomes a shallow ramp. A long circumferential motion produces a short axial displacement. Without friction, rotational input work equals the load's potential-energy gain; the small torque is exchanged for a large turning distance.

Sliding-thread friction increases the lifting torque. It can also prevent the load from driving the screw backward. **Self-locking depends on the lead angle and friction angle**, rather than being guaranteed by the presence of a screw.

<h2 id="try">Check geometry and work together</h2>

- With a 4 mm lead, scrub from zero to one turn and then two. The lifts should be 4 mm and 8 mm.
- Remove friction and compare 4 mm with 8 mm lead. The same number of turns gives more lift but requires more raising torque.
- Keep the lead fixed and increase friction. Compare the extra input work with the load's lifting work.
- Reduce friction and watch the lowering-torque sign. A negative value means the load can tend to back-drive this ideal screw; controlled slow lowering would require a braking torque.

The 3D view has an optional side view. The nut is deliberately cut away to expose real square-thread surfaces rather than replacing the mating region with overlapping smooth cylinders.

<h2 id="deeper">Lead, pitch and friction are different quantities</h2>

<details>
<summary>Lead is not always pitch</summary>

Pitch is the axial spacing of neighboring threads; lead is axial advance per revolution. An n-start thread has lead equal to n times its pitch. This scene uses one start, so the two coincide. For mean radius r and lead L:

<div className="formula">tan α = L / (2πr); lift = L × turns</div>

The geometry uses the same helix for rotation and translation, preserving the mating phase in the fixed nut.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Square-thread torque and the self-locking boundary</summary>

For axial load W, thread friction coefficient μ and friction angle φ = arctan μ, a quasi-static model with an ideal thrust bearing gives:

<div className="formula">raising torque = Wr tan(α + φ)</div>

<div className="formula">lowering torque = Wr tan(φ − α)</div>

When φ exceeds α, a positive lowering-direction torque is needed and the model is self-locking. Equality is the boundary; below it, back-driving is possible. Other thread profiles change the contact forces and friction relationship.

The scene does not model strength, buckling, impact, wear, lubrication changes, real bearings or support failure. Its self-locking criterion is not a safety guarantee for lifting equipment.

</details>
