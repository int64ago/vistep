<h2 id="understand">Magnify the image, not the cell</h2>

With a fixed eyepiece field number, quadrupling objective magnification reduces the specimen field diameter to one quarter. Locate at low power, center the target, then switch objectives and use fine focus to retain the same cell.

Hooke's cork observations mainly revealed the walls left by dead cells. Cell theory emerged from comparisons across animal and plant tissues; observations of division later corrected explanations of cell formation. The historical shots are evidence reconstructions, not historical photographs.

<h2 id="try">Keep track of one chloroplast</h2>

- Move a cell to the edge under low power, then change objectives. Does it remain visible?
- Adjust fine focus without changing magnification to distinguish blur from a change in size.
- Scrub tracking time in the Hydrilla leaf and compare the ringed chloroplast with the fixed cell wall.

<h2 id="deeper">Scale and observational limits</h2>

<details>
<summary>How is field size calculated?</summary>

An 18 mm eyepiece field number gives specimen field diameters of 1800 μm and 450 μm with 10× and 40× objectives. Representative specimen dimensions and the scale bar use the same conversion. Reconstructed shapes do not replace measurements of actual specimens.

Chloroplasts follow a reproducible peripheral path at an illustrative speed. The model does not depict molecular motors or place chloroplasts inside the central vacuole. Greater magnification alone cannot overcome optical resolution limits.

</details>
