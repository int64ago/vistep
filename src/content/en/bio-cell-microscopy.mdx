<h2 id="understand">What part of a cell did you actually observe?</h2>

Cork partitions and cavities provide evidence, but one cork section cannot establish the whole of cell theory. Hooke mainly saw walls left by dead cells. Comparisons across plant and animal tissues supported a shared cellular basis; tracking division supplied a different kind of evidence about where new cells originate.

The film separates these inferences. Leaf tissue and animal epithelium are independent samples with different shapes and visible structures. The later division shot is a modern explanatory schematic. It starts after nuclear division, with two daughter nuclei already separated. A cell plate grows from the center toward the original wall, partitioning the same parent cell. This is not historical footage, nor a claim that every cell has identical visible structures.

<h2 id="try">Predict, then follow the same object</h2>

- **Will the changing field lose the target?** Leave it 480 μm from the center and change from a 10× to a 40× objective. Predict whether it remains inside the field, then center that same target and test again.
- **Does enlargement add information?** Enlarge the fixed pixel image. Its display grows while its recorded pixels stay the same. Resizing an old image differs from acquiring a new image with a higher numerical aperture.
- **Is the slide moving, or the cell interior?** Keep tracking time fixed and switch between internal motion alone and internal motion with drift. Screen positions change, but wall-relative displacement agrees.

Textbook positions: PEP Biology Required 1, Chapter 1, Sections 1 and 2, pp. 3–4 and 9–10; Chapter 3, Section 2, p. 50. The cell-plate geometry also follows the plant-division schematic on p. 113. It is a modern explanation of lineage continuity, not an additional historical experiment.

<h2 id="deeper">How far does the evidence go?</h2>

<details>
<summary>Why locate the target at low power?</summary>

With an 18 mm eyepiece field number, specimen field diameter equals field number divided by objective magnification: 1800 μm at 10× and 450 μm at 40×. Diameter falls to one quarter and area to one sixteenth. At 480 μm from the center, the whole representative target lies outside the 225 μm high-power radius. It remains in the specimen, outside the imaged circle. Centering recovers the same cell identity.

Scale bars, cells and fields share micrometer coordinates. Cell sizes are representative, not measurements of a particular specimen. The smooth lens-change presentation does not imply continuously variable objective lenses. [Nikon's field-of-view explanation](https://www.microscopyu.com/microscopy-basics/field-of-view) gives the field-number relationship.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does a larger image not guarantee more detail?</summary>

The final enlargement reuses a fixed 24 × 18 pixel image after one blur-and-sampling step. Two close features were not separated in this record. Showing the same pixels at a larger size cannot recover the missing detail. Its Gaussian low-pass example is instructional, not a calibrated microscope point-spread function.

Changing objectives can still help. Numerical aperture, illumination and wavelength affect the detail in a newly acquired image. The counterexample concerns enlarging an existing record. See Nikon's explanations of [resolution](https://www.microscopyu.com/microscopy-basics/resolution) and [useful magnification](https://www.microscopyu.com/microscopy-basics/useful-magnification-range).

</details>

<details>
<summary>How does motion provide evidence of streaming?</summary>

Use the same wall landmark as a reference. A shared slide displacement D is added to both chloroplast and wall positions; subtraction cancels it. A remaining relative displacement provides evidence of internal motion. The model retains 18 chloroplast identities, including the ringed marker, on a peripheral cytoplasmic path outside the central vacuole.

The classroom Hydrilla observation uses chloroplast movement as a tracer of cytoplasmic streaming. This is a kinematic teaching model, without motor or fluid-dynamic calculations; its speed is illustrative. Chloroplasts can also respond to light and exhibit other movement, so this explanation does not cover every chloroplast motion in every species. [Nedo and colleagues' primary study](https://nph.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/nph.20147) illustrates distinctions in its particular research material. The structural close-up clarifies boundaries that may be difficult to see in bright-field microscopy; it is not a newly resolved photograph.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How are historical observations separated from later explanations?</summary>

[Hooke's Micrographia, Observation XVIII](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/sts-003-the-rise-of-modern-science-fall-2010/aace2313aa705710ac016d583ef50dce_MITSTS_003F10_read14_hooke.pdf), describes cork chambers. [Selected passages from the 1847 English translation of Schwann's work](https://mechanism.ucsd.edu/bill/teaching/w10/schwann.Microscopical_Researches.selectedpassages.pdf) preserve the context of early formation theories. A common cellular basis must be distinguished from the former crystal-like explanation of cell formation, which was later corrected.

Division evidence requires continuity of the observed object. Two still pictures with different cell counts cannot establish lineage if their samples or fields differ. This film begins after the nuclei have separated and illustrates how the plate connects to the parent boundary. It neither models complete mitosis nor addresses the origin of the first cells.

</details>
