<h2 id="understand">Three different levels of achievement</h2>

Chemical DNA synthesis, chromosome rearrangement and a cell capable of sustained growth are distinct achievements. The 2010 bacterial-genome study used an existing recipient cell and explicitly noted that its cytoplasm was not synthetic.

The 2018 single-chromosome yeast study successively fused chromosome ends and deleted redundant centromeres, reorganizing sixteen chromosomes into one. Following the original paper clarifies the textbook’s brief account: this was not transplantation of a newly synthesized chromosome into an enucleated yeast cell.

<h2 id="try">Change packaging and inspect what remains</h2>

- Increase fusion count and compare chromosome number, centromere number and retained representative gene blocks.
- Remove the model’s recipient environment: DNA remains, but a necessary condition is absent.
- Disrupt a representative essential function to distinguish structural assembly from demonstrated activity.

<h2 id="deeper">The blocks do not encode real sequences</h2>

<details>
<summary>How does the model relate to research?</summary>

Twelve assembly pieces and sixteen colored yeast gene blocks are explanatory symbols, without actual DNA sequences or experimental parameters. Fusion retains major genetic content, but real engineering also changes centromeres, telomeres and some sequence regions; it does not preserve every base unchanged.

The logical model shows that complete assembly, a suitable cellular environment and essential functions are necessary. It predicts no success rate and replaces no growth, expression or reproduction experiment. These necessary conditions do not guarantee that any particular design will survive.
