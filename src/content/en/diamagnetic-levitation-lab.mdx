<h2 id="understand">Water is weakly diamagnetic, not nonmagnetic</h2>

Diamagnetic material in a nonuniform field tends toward weaker field. A large field magnitude alone is insufficient: an ideal uniform field produces no net translational lift in this model. With a sufficient field–gradient product, magnetic force can balance weight.

Berry and Geim discussed the levitation of water drops, frogs and other objects in fields of about 16 T in 1997. This differs from the nearly complete magnetic exclusion of a superconductor. The film tracks water, then uses a frog outline to connect the mechanism to the textbook’s historical example.

<h2 id="try">Separate strength from gradient</h2>

- Compare no field, a uniform 16 T field and a nonuniform field.
- Find the height where magnetic lift and weight agree.
- Observe recovery after a small perturbation, then reduce peak field.
- Distinguish existing gravity from zero resultant force.

<h2 id="deeper">Limits of the vertical model</h2>

<div className="formula">Fz/V = (χ/μ₀) B(dB/dz); equilibrium: B(dB/dz) = ρgμ₀/χ</div>

For water, χ = −9.05×10⁻⁶ and ρ = 1000 kg/m³ give a required product of about −1361 T²/m. Both magnetic force and weight scale with volume, so equilibrium for a small sample of the same material does not depend on total mass.

The model uses an axial Gaussian field of width 5 cm, linear damping and fourth-order integration. It analyzes only the vertically stable branch, not full three-dimensional stability; transverse stability requires the real spatial field. The frog uses water-like properties without modeling tissue differences. Magnet lines are an apparatus illustration, not a complete winding or cooling design.

Source: [Berry and Geim, Of flying frogs and levitrons (1997)](https://www.physics.umd.edu/grt/taj/411c/FlyingFrogs.pdf). Textbook: PEP Elective 3, p. 34.
