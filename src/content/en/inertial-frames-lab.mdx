<h2 id="understand">One ball, two descriptions of motion</h2>

Place a steel ball on paper and pull the paper quickly. From the table, the paper travels far while the ball may move only slightly. In a view following the paper, the ball moves backward relative to it. Both views come from the same physical calculation.

Over this short experiment the table is approximately an inertial frame. The accelerating paper is not. Even if horizontal contact force is ideally removed, a ball at rest relative to the table has a changing relative velocity in the accelerating paper frame. A coordinate change does not create a new real interaction.

Real paper exerts friction, so the ball is not exactly stationary. That small motion is retained here, with a separate zero-friction comparison isolating the reference-frame effect.

<h2 id="try">Change friction and pulling time separately</h2>

- Set paper friction to zero. The ball stays at its table position while its paper coordinate changes.
- Keep friction and compare fast and slow pulls. Measure the actual ball displacement instead of assuming every pull leaves it unmoved.
- Pause and compare the two positions with paper displacement. Paper coordinates equal table coordinates minus the paper position.
- Watch the contact status. When the paper edge passes the ball, contact changes to the bare table and so does its friction law.

<h2 id="deeper">One contact model, two coordinate views</h2>

<details>

<summary>Coordinate and acceleration relations</summary>

If the ball has table coordinate x and paper displacement X, then x′ = x − X. Similarly v′ = v − V and a′ = a − A. When the paper accelerates, a′ can be nonzero even with zero true horizontal acceleration a.

Applying Newton’s law in the inertial frame does not require inventing an extra physical interaction when the camera changes. Equations written in an accelerating frame need explicitly identified frame-dependent inertial terms, distinct from contact forces.

</details>

<details>

<summary>The steel sphere can rotate</summary>

A smooth half-cosine pull carries the paper 0.50 m from rest back to rest. The uniform solid sphere has I = 2mr²/5 and radius0.03 m. Friction changes translation and rotation together. Each small time step checks whether contact can stick to the moving paper; if the required impulse exceeds the static threshold, sliding friction is used.

The default paper kinetic coefficient is0.03, with a static limit1.3 times larger. Bare-table kinetic friction is0.10. Paper thickness is ignored, so the edge does not cause a vertical fall. These are teaching parameters, not measured material data. A slowly pulled sphere may roll on the paper; it is not automatically glued to it.

</details>
