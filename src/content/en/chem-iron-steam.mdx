<section id="understand">
## Follow oxygen from water

Under the textbook's red-hot conditions, iron reacts with steam to form magnetite and hydrogen. Gas leaves the reaction region, but the remaining solid need not become lighter. Oxygen from water joins the solid, while hydrogen forms gas. The film tracks the same three iron, four oxygen and eight hydrogen atoms through the net account.

<div className="formula">3Fe + 4H₂O(g) → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ (textbook high-temperature conditions)</div>

Fe₃O₄ describes the solid's composition. Its grouping frame is neither an isolated molecule nor a simple mixture of separate FeO and Fe₂O₃ phases. Moving atoms illustrate net allocation, not each elementary step occurring at an iron surface.

The added solid mass comes from oxygen in water. A complete mass account also includes hydrogen, unreacted water and remaining iron. Weighing only the solid and counting the entire system answer different questions. During the mass-transfer illustration, a batch temporarily belongs to an explicit transfer account; it joins the solid and gas accounts only on arrival, so no material is counted twice.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Twice the water, twice the hydrogen?

Both ideal examples start with 1 g of iron. A supplies 0.3 g of water; B supplies 0.6 g. Hide the hydrogen answers and inspect which reactant runs out. A exhausts its water and leaves iron. B exhausts its iron and leaves water. Hydrogen yields are about 16.65 mmol and 23.88 mmol, so doubling water has not doubled hydrogen.

In free exploration, switch among apparatus, atoms, mass, evidence and prediction. Change B's water mass, make a new prediction and reveal it. The mass view also offers an unheated comparison and zero available water. These controls change the actual model while leaving the film's comparison conditions intact.

The amounts are ideal stoichiometric calculations, not textbook measurements. Progress means teaching sequence or conversion, not reaction time. The unheated comparison represents no similarly appreciable reaction on this demonstration's timescale; it does not exclude slow corrosion in other environments.

<summary>Why is one bubble or one colour insufficient?</summary>

Bubbles show gas passage; air also makes bubbles. Textbook Figure 3-5 includes a combustibility test. Taken together with the reactants, products and material account, that supports hydrogen formation. Black is also non-unique: the following textbook page describes black FeO. The air and solid-colour comparisons are conceptual aids, not additional textbook experiments.

The apparatus preserves damp cotton, the heated iron region, both heat sources and connected tubing to soap solution. Selected product markers do not imply that the whole gas stream is pure hydrogen. Initial air, steam condensation, mixing and purity are not calculated. The combustibility view explains textbook evidence rather than providing a procedure for igniting gas mixtures.
