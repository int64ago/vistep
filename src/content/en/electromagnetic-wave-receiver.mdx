<h2 id="understand">Reception waits for propagation</h2>

PEP Required Book3 page120 uses two pairs of copper tubes as parallel transmitting and receiving antennas, changing distance while observing a receiving indication. This scene retains that modern classroom arrangement: a high-voltage source excites the transmitter gap, and the receiver connects to an RF indicator. It is not a reconstruction of Hertz’s original apparatus.

Rapid electrical changes produce an outward-propagating electromagnetic wave. The receiving antenna responds after arrival; greater distance also means longer delay. Stopping the source does not erase waves already traveling through space.

<h2 id="try">Compare the same packet phase</h2>

Change distance or scrub time after emission. The distance chapter aligns observations to the same retarded-time point within the packet, avoiding confusion between attenuation and different packet phases.

An ideal radiative far field has electric amplitude proportional to1/r. This square-law detector’s response therefore scales as1/r². This is not an exact fit for every classroom distance: near fields, reflections, multipath, resonance and instrument response affect actual measurements.

<h2 id="deeper">The instrument needs a model too</h2>

<details>
<summary>Why an RF detector is included</summary>

The textbook does not specify the meter’s internal circuit. High-frequency alternating current can have zero average; a bare DC ammeter cannot directly give the displayed stable RF-strength indication. This scene explicitly assumes square-law detection and smoothing, without claiming the textbook uses a particular circuit.

The illustrative spark packet has a120 ns sine-squared envelope and50 MHz carrier, not a measured spark spectrum. The field uses retarded time t−r/c, with magnetic field in phase and B=E/c. Detector drive is proportional to squared envelope, followed by a40 ns response. The needle directly shows that response, not mechanical meter inertia. Readings are relative units.

Quantitative amplitudes are limited to an ideal4–12 m far-field comparison. Spatial strokes illustrate polarity, envelope and arrival, not field strength to scale. Antennas and separation are magnified separately. Motion is greatly slowed; actual delays are nanoseconds.
