<h2 id="understand">Understand it</h2>

**A colony is growth made visible.** Follow starting sites A, B and C. With suitable nutrients, temperature and time, cells multiply at their deposited positions. The illustration preserves each site's identity; colours aid tracking rather than identify species. A starting site may be one cell or an attached cluster.

The yeast pure-culture activity on pages 12–13 of Elective 3 asks two different questions. Streaking carries fewer cells into successive regions, separating starting sites. A matched uninoculated plate asks whether growth can occur without the intended inoculum. In Watch, this blank is initially clear and later develops one colony. That failure invalidates this batch's purity interpretation: investigate contamination and repeat the check. Subtracting one colony is not a valid correction. Even a clear blank and similar-looking isolated colonies would not establish organism identity.

The high-throughput screening discussion on page 15 asks why the darkest well need not be the best candidate. B3 leads in total output, but its output per biomass is 3.4 / 3.0≈1.13, compared with A4's 2.1 / 0.8≈2.63. Positions, raw output and colour stay fixed; the denominator changes. Independent retesting then removes B3's initial advantage while A4 remains higher. The 24-well arrangement and all readings are synthetic teaching examples.

The mechanism on page 16 connects to the urea-decomposing soil-bacteria activity on pages 18–19. **Urea is not a switch that makes a desired organism appear.** Follow nitrogen markers N1 and N2: urease hydrolysis releases ammonia/ammonium, and uptake and assimilation incorporate usable nitrogen into biomass. This can support continued growth only when carbon, energy and other requirements are also met. The diagram shows net nitrogen flow, not a universal intracellular or extracellular location for urease.

Use that mechanism to predict: B cannot hydrolyze urea, so what happens if usable nitrogen is supplied directly? The matched cultures preserve candidate identity and inoculum while changing the nitrogen source. Both candidates can use the other supplied nitrogen; only A has the modelled urea-hydrolysis ability. Without nitrogen neither continues multiplying. A remaining small cell represents the original inoculum, not a colony grown on nitrogen-free medium. Actual cultures require checks for nutrient carryover, cross-feeding and contamination. Growth alone does not establish urease activity or species identity.

<h2 id="deeper">Go deeper</h2>

**Nitrogen accounting and limits.** Pool values are relative nitrogen equivalents with an initial total of 2, not measured concentrations, cell counts or culture hours. A sequential first-order model conserves nitrogen through urea, available nitrogen and newly assimilated biomass nitrogen. N1 and N2 are representative pathway markers, not a count of every molecule. The alternative nitrogen source starts in the available pool; the no-nitrogen culture starts with zero supplied nitrogen. Storage, transport details, enzyme location and regulation, other urea-utilization pathways and cross-feeding are omitted. This explains the textbook pathway rather than predicting a real strain's growth.

**Separation and growth.** Five regions contain 64,24,9,4 and 2 illustrative inoculation sites. These are not measured transfer counts. The first pickup comes from the culture tube; later pickups come from the end of the preceding streak after loop treatment and cooling. Deposited sites retain their positions during growth. Cells and visible colonies are drawn at different scales. Medium preparation, sterilization, cooling and material disposal remain actual laboratory conditions to follow through the school's procedures.

**From colonies to CFU.** In the textbook's nominal chain,10 g of soil with 90 mL water already establishes the initial 1:10 suspension. Each subsequent 1 mL transfer into 9 mL yields a further tenfold dilution after mixing, while sample material remains accounted for. Here g/mL means original-soil equivalent, not a precise measurement obtained by adding solid and liquid volumes.

At 10⁻⁵ g/mL and 0.1 mL per plate, synthetic counts of 96,103 and 101 average 100, giving 100/(0.1×10⁻⁵)=10⁸ CFU/g. The initial suspension is already included in the exponent. This textbook exercise uses 30–300 colonies and at least three replicates at the same dilution; other analytical methods can use different rules. Out-of-range values remain visible and prompt review rather than disappearing to make an acceptable result.

This calculation assumes a separate teaching batch with satisfactory controls, not the failed yeast batch shown earlier. One CFU can arise from a cell or cluster. Organisms unable to form colonies under the specified conditions are absent from the count. CFU is neither a direct cell count, pathogen identification nor complete quality assessment.

**Sampling defines the denominator.** Page 20 offers investigations of air, water, milk and soil. An illustrative active sample of 100 L yielding 12 CFU converts to 120 CFU/m³. A settle plate with 12 CFU has no measured air volume, so that conversion cannot be copied; exposed area and time must be retained. Collection efficiency and sampler correction are not modelled.

The water and milk examples both yield 80 CFU from 0.1 mL, but water is undiluted and milk has a 100-fold dilution. Their inferred original concentrations are 800 and 80000 CFU/mL. This compares denominators, not sample safety. Soil results use mass and require a dry/wet basis, location, depth and culture conditions.
