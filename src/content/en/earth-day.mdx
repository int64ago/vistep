<h2 id="understand">A day needs a reference</h2>

Start at local noon. One full rotation restores a direction relative to distant stars. During that rotation, orbital motion changes the direction of the Sun. A prograde planet must therefore rotate farther to face the Sun again.

The film first uses a circular orbit lasting 12×24 h to make the difference visible. This is explicitly not Earth's actual orbital rate. Its sidereal rotation takes about 22:09:14, while successive model noons are 24:00:00 apart. The orbital inset and close view share one orbital angle; sizes and distances are not drawn on one common scale.

<h2 id="try">Subtract orbital from spin angular velocity</h2>

With signed rates, `ω_solar=ω_spin−ω_orbit`. For faster prograde spin in the same direction as the orbit, the solar day exceeds the sidereal day. The star reference stays fixed, the Sun direction follows orbital motion, and the radius through observer P follows spin.

Switch off orbital motion while holding spin rate fixed and both periods become equal. Reverse spin without changing its speed and the magnitude of relative angular velocity increases, making the solar day shorter than the sidereal day. This ideal retrograde counterexample does not claim to use Venus's actual parameters.

<h2 id="deeper">Earth's difference is about four minutes</h2>

<details>
<summary>Earth-like scaling and the limits of mean solar time</summary>

A model year of 365.256×24 h gives a sidereal period of about 23:56:04 and a mean solar period of 24 h. The orbital offset at a full spin is about 0.98°. That angle is genuinely small in the drawing: clocks and measurements preserve the model ratio without silently exaggerating it again.

Assumptions are circular uniform orbital motion, constant spin, an axis perpendicular to the orbital plane and a fixed distant-star direction. Earth's actual tilt and elliptical orbit separate true solar time from the uniform mean reference. Precession, nutation and variable Earth rotation also lie outside scope. This is not a high-precision UT1 or sidereal-time algorithm, and the rounded clocks do not predict actual daily noon intervals.

Explore changes the orbital period, elapsed time, orbit switch and spin direction, or seeks to one sidereal or solar period. Switching off orbit holds the original spin speed fixed so the causal intervention remains explicit.

</details>
