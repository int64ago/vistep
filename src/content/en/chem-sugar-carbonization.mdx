<section id="understand">

Concentrated sulfuric acid blackens sucrose and forms expanded porous material. Dehydration removes hydrogen and oxygen in the proportion that forms water; it differs from absorbing water already present. Heat release and oxidation side reactions also occur, so the residue cannot be described simply as pure carbon.

The textbook introduces the phenomenon in body Figure 5-5, with exercise 4 discussing darkening, expansion and gas evolution. It remains a textbook demonstration rather than a newly numbered student experiment.

</section>
<section id="try">

Observe colour first, then vary pores independently. At fixed dehydration, increasing porosity lets the same solid amount occupy more bulk volume. Increasing the fraction of carbon that is further oxidized reduces the carbon left behind rather than creating extra solid.

The dehydration inventory and oxidation pathway check C, H, O and S separately. Equations balance selected paths; the dots are not molecular structures, and animation speed does not establish reaction time.

</section>
<section id="deeper">
<details>
<summary>Two reaction inventories</summary>

The simplified dehydration equation is C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁→12C+11H₂O. The selected carbon-oxidation path is C+2H₂SO₄→CO₂+2SO₂+2H₂O. For one initial sucrose unit, dehydration extent d and fraction f of formed carbon further oxidized, oxidized carbon is 12df and remaining carbon is 12d(1−f). Unreacted sucrose, water and selected gases are all included in the atom balance.

This is not a measured product distribution. The real mixture may contain other carbon products, residual acid and complex intermediates. No exact purity, yield, temperature or gas-composition ratio is claimed.

</details>
<details>
<summary>How pores affect volume</summary>

The geometric relation is V=Vₛ/(1−φ), with porosity φ and solid-framework volume Vₛ. Fully dehydrated carbon receives an illustrative framework volume of 0.45 relative to the starting sucrose reference; this is not a fit using measured densities. Pores are assigned only to the formed carbon phase; unreacted sucrose retains compact volume. Displayed overall porosity is void volume divided by total volume. Pore texture and column height follow the same state, not microscopy or a bubble-flow simulation.

Porosity is limited to 0.88 to avoid the divergence as φ approaches one. Zero porosity gives compact volume. If the selected pathway oxidizes all carbon and no unreacted sucrose remains, solid volume is zero. Expansion illustrates how growing gas-filled space is compatible with conservation of matter.
