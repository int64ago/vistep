<section id="understand">

A calorimeter turns energy transfer into an observable temperature rise or mass of melted ice. In either case, the apparatus must distinguish heat from the studied source from heat supplied by the surroundings.

## Temperature rise: measure first, calculate second

Pages 5–6 of Selective Compulsory 1 use 50 mL of 0.50 mol/L hydrochloric acid and 50 mL of 0.55 mol/L sodium hydroxide. Slight excess base ensures the acid reacts completely. Approximating both dilute solutions by water, equal volumes have equal heat capacities, so their initial temperatures are averaged. The highest mixed temperature is recorded, and the experiment is repeated three times.

The limiting acid forms 0.025 mol of water. The combined mass is approximately 100 g, with specific heat capacity 4.18 J/(g·K). Multiply these by the mean temperature rise to estimate absorbed heat, then divide by water formed. The reaction enthalpy is negative. The textbook gives approximately 57.3 kJ released per mole of water for dilute strong-acid/strong-base neutralisation at 25 °C and 101 kPa.

All displayed trials are synthetic calculation examples. They are not experimental records. Heat absorbed by the vessel or lost to the surroundings reduces the apparent magnitude when only solution heating is counted. Repetition does not eliminate that systematic error. The Royal Society of Chemistry discusses insulation, lids and measurement practice in its [calorimetry teaching resource](https://edu.rsc.org/cpd/measuring-energy-changes-teaching-practical-science/4010499.article).

## Melting ice: keep the drains separate

Page 16 introduces Lavoisier and Laplace’s ice calorimeter as history of science. The original description distinguishes a central sample chamber, measurement ice and external guard ice, with separate drains. The combustion version includes inlet and outlet gas tubes conditioned by the ice. [Lavoisier, Elements of Chemistry, calorimeter chapter](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/30775/30775-h/30775-h.htm)

Ice at its melting point provides a heat scale through mass melted times latent heat. This model uses a modern approximation of 333.5 J/g ([NBS thermophysical study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5295832/)), complete collection, effective guarding and complete graphite combustion. Its numbers are modern teaching calculations, not historical measurements. See the [Science Museum collection record](https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co2543/replica-of-lavoisiers-calorimeter) for a historical replica.

<summary>Model and limits</summary>

The temperature model integrates reaction heat and environmental exchange into solution-plus-vessel heat capacity. Reaction progress follows an illustrative 20-second smooth function, not measured kinetics. Default vessel capacity is 25 J/K and environmental conductance 1.5 W/K, selected to explain error. Vessel and surroundings start at the mean solution temperature; mixing heat and temperature dependence are omitted. All three energy destinations use the same integrated state.

Synthetic repeats vary conductance slightly and add zero-mean reading offsets. Calculations use unrounded values, so calculations from the displayed two-decimal table can differ in the last digit.

The ice model uses 12.01 g/mol and −393.5 kJ/mol for modern graphite combustion. Central heat melts measurement ice; external heat melts guard ice independently. Real gas flow, combustion rate, retained water and historical calibration are not predicted. Dimensions are rearranged for teaching, and colours distinguish structure and water paths rather than actual water colour. Exhausting the guard allows environmental contamination; normal exploration retains guard capacity.
