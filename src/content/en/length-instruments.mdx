<h2 id="understand">Fine readings come from geometry, not extra decimal places</h2>

A vernier amplifies displacement through a small difference between two scales. Ten vernier divisions span 9 mm, so each is 0.9 mm, or 0.1 mm shorter than a main-scale division. Read the main scale immediately left of the vernier zero, then identify the aligned vernier line. The textbook example adds 23 mm and the seventh division's 0.7 mm to give 23.7 mm. Twenty- and fifty-division verniers resolve 0.05 and 0.02 mm respectively.

External jaws measure outside dimensions; internal jaws measure bores or slot widths. Before correct contact, a finely read number is not the specimen dimension. Jaws and object use the same length coordinates, followed by a separate magnified scale.

A micrometer converts rotation into translation: one turn advances 0.5 mm, and fifty thimble divisions give 0.01 mm each. Check whether the sleeve's half-millimetre mark is exposed, add the thimble reading and retain one estimated digit. The textbook example is 6.5 + 22.5 × 0.01 = 6.725 mm.

<h2 id="try">Establish contact and check zero before reading</h2>

- Choose outside diameter or slot width, then change the vernier division count. Scale positions and the aligned line derive directly from size.
- Compare paper, hair and the textbook reading example in the micrometer. Near contact, use the fine mechanism to control force. The model stops the spindle at the specimen surface.
- Add a positive zero reading and subtract it from the indicated value. A magnified contact view makes thin specimens visible without inventing extra thickness.
- Add wire turns, then deliberately introduce gaps. More turns reduce the contribution from a fixed total-length reading error, but do not remove loose-winding bias.

<h2 id="deeper">Estimated digits, error and idealized contact</h2>

<details>
<summary>What does each method approximate?</summary>

Vernier indications use the nearest alignment at the selected least count, without invented finer digits. The micrometer's third decimal is estimated; it does not guarantee 0.001 mm instrument accuracy. Zero, temperature, alignment, force and specimen deformation all affect actual results.

The contact model treats specimens as rigid. At contact the spindle stops advancing and further fine-control motion represents slip, not compression. Real paper and hair can deform, making consistent force and repeated readings necessary.

Accumulation assumes tightly packed circular cross-sections and neglects helix angle and deformation. If L = Nd and the total-length reading bound remains ±0.1 mm, this contribution to diameter error is ±0.1/N mm. Gaps add empty space, so dividing the span by turn count overestimates diameter. Systematic error does not automatically vanish.

</details>
