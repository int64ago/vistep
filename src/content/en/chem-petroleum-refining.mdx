<h2 id="understand">Understand the principle</h2>

This film follows petroleum fractionation, cracking and pyrolysis on pages 103–104 of the second required PEP chemistry textbook. It is an industrial-process and molecular-structure demonstration, not a student operating procedure.

Crude oil is already a complex mixture. Heating feeds vapour and liquid into a fractionating system, where repeated contact enriches components of different volatility. Lighter fractions are generally collected higher in the tower, heavier fractions lower down, and insufficiently vaporized material remains below. Fractions are mixtures: gasoline is not a single alkane. Distillation preserves molecular identities. See the [US Energy Information Administration's refining overview](https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/oil-and-petroleum-products/refining-crude-oil-the-refining-process.php).

The drawing selects normal butane, hexane, decane, hexadecane and tetracosane as representatives. Forty fixed markers, eight per species, provide a conserved population to follow. They do not represent a real crude composition or product yield. Outlet numbers 4, 6, 10, 16 and 24 are representative carbon counts, not commercial fraction names. A ring follows a hexadecane parcel. The molecular close-up shows that all sixteen carbons retain their connections.

<h2 id="try">Try it yourself</h2>

Choose a representative and vary the illustrative column temperatures. In this model, a normal boiling point above the bottom temperature gives a residual liquid route; one below the top temperature gives a gaseous top route. Intermediate values map onto a condensation waypoint. Lower the bottom to 250°C and follow hexadecane: its route changes to the residue outlet.

This is a normal-boiling-point sorting model, not a quantitative vapour–liquid-equilibrium calculation. A linear temperature gradient sets the waypoint. Partial pressures, tray efficiency, reflux, pressure loss, residence times and heat transfer are omitted. In a real mixture, individual molecules do not suddenly condense only at their pure-substance boiling points. Connected paths and outlet counts use the same conserved markers, so seeking reconstructs a deterministic state.

In the cracking shots, changing the shorter alkane's carbon count changes connectivity and the length of the accompanying alkene. Product hydrogen counts follow carbon valence and the displayed bond orders. Move to the final state, count atoms, and then try another cut position.

<h2 id="deeper">Go deeper</h2>

The four textbook net reactions are C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + C₈H₁₆; C₈H₁₈ → C₄H₁₀ + C₄H₈; and two alternative butane routes, C₄H₁₀ → CH₄ + C₃H₆ or C₄H₁₀ → C₂H₆ + C₂H₄. The last two shots each begin with a fresh butane molecule. One molecule is not assigned both product sets.

Lines express initial and final connectivity. Transition frames are not radicals, carbocations or other chemical intermediates, and the film does not claim that one bond cleavage alone yields the products. Carbon identities remain fixed; hydrogen is accounted for in formulas and valence rather than drawn individually. Real cracking involves competing reactions, multiple products and process-dependent distributions. No conversion, selectivity or industrial yield is predicted.

Catalytic cracking and pyrolytic routes to low-carbon olefins should not be assigned one universal operating condition. Modern steam cracking can be a high-temperature, noncatalytic process with conditions different from catalytic cracking. This film retains the textbook's net-reaction learning task without generalizing one catalyst, temperature or pressure to all industrial routes. See [OpenStax on industrial alkene preparation](https://openstax.org/books/organic-chemistry/pages/7-1-industrial-preparation-and-use-of-alkenes).

Approximate normal boiling points are drawn from NIST: butane −0.5°C, hexane 68.8°C, decane 174.2°C, hexadecane 287°C and tetracosane 391.4°C. Consult [butane](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?ID=C106978&Type=TBOIL), [hexane](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?ID=C110543&Type=TBOIL), [decane](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/inchi?ID=C124185&Type=TBOIL), the [NIST hexadecane compilation](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nistir6659.pdf) and [tetracosane](https://webbook.nist.gov/cgi/cbook.cgi?ID=C646311&Mask=4). For tetracosane, the selected entry is Weast and Grasselli's 664.5 K; historical entries are not equally precise. These numbers support an ordering illustration, not refinery equipment design.
