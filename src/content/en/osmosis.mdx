<h2 id="understand">Why does water rise behind a solute-blocking membrane?</h2>

The membrane admits water while retaining the modeled non-electrolyte solute. Water can exchange in both directions. A difference in water chemical potential produces a net transfer, initially toward the more concentrated side when no opposing pressure is applied.

Incoming water dilutes the solution without carrying its solute across. Conserved volume raises one surface and lowers the other. The resulting hydrostatic pressure opposes the transfer. Net flow can stop even while concentrations remain unequal.

<h2 id="deeper">Derive flux from pressure differences</h2>

For a dilute ideal non-electrolyte, Π = cRT. Each chamber starts with 200 mL and a 50 cm² cross section at 298 K. A net transfer q leaves V₀−q on the left and V₀+q on the right, with each solute amount fixed.

The driving pressure is RT(nR/VR−nL/VL) − ρg(hR−hL) − Pexternal. Volume flux is a hydraulic permeability times that difference. Small fixed steps reconstruct the history at any requested film time. Levels, concentrations, pressures and tracer ownership all use the same transferred volume.

The model excludes nonideal activity, electrolyte dissociation, concentration polarization, membrane deformation and solute leakage. Area is fixed and density is approximated by water. Very dilute concentrations and visible columns are classroom choices, not operating specifications for seawater desalination.
