## Rays and wavefronts describe one wave

A monochromatic plane wave crossing a stationary boundary between isotropic media preserves its frequency. Phase speed is vacuum light speed divided by index, and the in-medium wavelength changes accordingly. Wavefronts must join compatibly along the interface; rays are normal to their respective fronts.

At oblique incidence from lower to higher index, the ray bends toward the normal. In the reverse direction it bends away. Normal incidence provides the essential counterexample: speed and wavelength change without a change in direction.

## Hold one condition fixed

Exploration keeps the first index at1 and varies incident angle and the second index. Begin with equal indices, then increase the second. Compare normal with oblique incidence. Angles are measured from the dashed normal, rather than the interface itself.

## Tangential phase must match

<div className="formula">
  n₁ sinθ₁ = n₂ sinθ₂
  <br />
  Phase speed = c/n
  <br />
  In-medium wavelength = vacuum wavelength/n
</div>

Rays and wavefronts share one Snell-law state and the film's common phase clock. The scene uses550 nm vacuum-wavelength light. Front spacing is proportional and motion is slowed for explanation. Media are homogeneous, isotropic, transparent and lossless; the weaker reflected contribution is omitted.

For higher-to-lower index incidence, sufficiently large angles have no real propagating refraction angle and require total internal reflection instead. Absorption, anisotropy, nonlinearity and frequency changes at moving boundaries are outside the model. Phase speed is not asserted to equal information speed under every condition.
