<h2 id="understand">A recorded graph is not a spatial path</h2>

A swinging syringe drops ink onto a board moving uniformly perpendicular to its swing. Board motion converts landing times into distances. Drops retain their tangential velocity at release and take finite time to fall, so the ink pattern is not exactly the needle’s instantaneous position.

The length experiment changes the pivot-to-centre distance and times several full oscillations. Plotting period squared T² against length L tests proportionality across trials. A single period calculated from a formula is not an experimental record.

<h2 id="try">Infer gravity from length and time</h2>

Measure to the bob centre, rather than recording only string length. Add the radius measured by caliper, or align the ruler directly with the centre. Start timing at equilibrium; the next crossing in the same direction completes one full oscillation. After twenty cycles, calculate T = t/20 and g = 4π²L/T².

Exploration allows omitted radius, length and timing errors, or larger release angles. Omitting the radius biases inferred gravity downward. Larger amplitudes increase the actual period, also producing a low estimate when the small-angle formula is used. The textbook mentions Huygens measuring gravity in Paris; this scene demonstrates the method without presenting model output as historical measurements.

<h2 id="deeper">Change effective gravity on Earth</h2>

A light rod and a string connect the bob to two points on one rotation axis, constraining motion to a plane perpendicular to that axis. Tilting the support tilts the plane. For plane inclination θ above horizontal, gravity projected along the plane corresponds to a = g sin θ.

Measure angle β between the resting rod and a plumb line, giving θ = 90° − β. Keep the rod length fixed, time twenty full oscillations and compare T against 1/√a. A horizontal plane has a = 0 and no gravitational restoring oscillation of this kind.
