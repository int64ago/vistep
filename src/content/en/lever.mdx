<h2 id="understand">The direction of a push matters</h2>

Keep your eye on the blue **120 N load**, one metre from the pivot. A downward push on the other side tends to turn the bar the opposite way. At the same radius it takes 120 N to balance; at twice the radius it takes 60 N.

Now tilt the push without moving its attachment. The violet segment becomes shorter. It is the shortest distance from the pivot to the force's line of action. That **perpendicular distance** is the moment arm. A long bar alone does not guarantee a large turning effect.

When the push points along the bar, its line passes through the pivot. Its moment is zero. The pivot can carry a reaction force, so balanced moments do not require equal forces on the two ends.

In the final slow lift, the effort end moves vertically twice as far as the load. Half the force acting through twice the distance transfers the same work. Force advantage does not create energy.

<h2 id="try">Move the force line before changing its size</h2>

- Keep the effort at 60 N and move its attachment from 2 m to 1 m. Compare the opposing moments.
- Return to 2 m and tilt the force 60° away from vertical. Its moment arm is now 1 m. Increase the effort to 120 N to restore balance.
- Point the effort along the bar. Check the moment reading rather than judging by arrow length.

Exploration holds the bar at a horizontal reference position and reports its tendency to turn. It does not predict the acceleration after release. Pause or seek to preserve any geometric construction.

<h2 id="deeper">One geometry for moments and work</h2>

<details>
<summary>Line of action and static balance</summary>

For position vector r from the pivot and applied force F, the signed planar moment is:

<div className="formula">τ = rₓ Fᵧ − rᵧ Fₓ; |τ| = F d⊥</div>

Opposing moments cancel; the pivot reaction balances the remaining force. The model omits bar mass, bearing friction, bending and structural strength. The 120 N label is a weight, not a mass.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why saving force does not save work</summary>

With vertical forces and a two-to-one radius ratio, balance requires F = W/2. For rotation θ, the vertical travels are 2 sin θ m and sin θ m. Their products with the respective forces are equal. The calculation uses displacement along the force, not the distance along a curved path.

A real lever can also expend energy against friction, raise its own mass or deform. Quasi-static motion means changes in kinetic energy are neglected; it does not mean energy is created.

</details>
