<h2 id="understand">Distinct evidence cannot be collapsed into one experiment</h2>

Fossils, comparative anatomy, embryology and molecular observations complement each other. Anchiornis combines dinosaur skeletal and feather features; forelimbs preserve corresponding relationships; embryos show homologous pharyngeal and tail regions; molecular comparisons use the textbook’s cytochrome c difference counts. These support common ancestry through different observations, not one classroom wet experiment.

Human embryos possess homologous pharyngeal structures, not functional fish gills. Development does not replay modern adult forms. One fossil does not establish direct ancestry, and one protein alone cannot provide an exact dated phylogeny. Evolution branches, includes extinction and diverse adaptations, and is not a ladder toward a predetermined “highest” organism.

<h2 id="try">Keep assumptions beside the calculation</h2>

- Highlight corresponding forelimb bones and distinguish shared relationships from changed proportions.
- Start the moth model at SS = 10, Ss = 20 and ss = 70. Dark classes multiply by 1.1 annually and pale by 0.9. Year two gives 11, 22 and 63, totaling 96. S frequency = (2 × 11 + 22) / (2 × 96) ≈ 22.92%.
- Change background and selection strength. Neutral conditions leave these frequencies unchanged. The exercise follows the textbook’s direct count updates without an added Hardy–Weinberg mating step.
- Compare one blank and three matched antibiotic disks on a virtual plate. Follow preset clone R-3 into its descendants and inspect the three zone readings and their mean. Remove the preset to test the assumption.
- Separate geography from reproductive evidence. Diverging allele frequencies do not automatically declare a new species.

<h2 id="deeper">Numerical assumptions and boundaries</h2>

<details>
<summary>What does the selection calculation assume?</summary>

The starting 10%, 20%, 70% and annual ±10% are textbook hypothetical values, not complete Manchester field data. Counts may represent noninteger expectations. The model omits random mating, migration, drift, mutation and density dependence. S frequency counts alleles and differs from the fraction of dark moths, because SS and Ss are both dark. A background change does not alter an individual genotype. Actual moth studies also consider resting locations, predation and other conditions.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What can virtual inhibition zones establish?</summary>

Normalized concentration is exp(−r² / (2σ²)) with σ = 18 model units. Its growth-threshold crossing determines each zone, with disk radius 8. This illustrative diffusion snapshot omits disk interaction, actual dose, degradation, nutrients, time and strain variation. It supplies no clinical susceptibility criteria.

Different growth thresholds are preset for the ordinary population and R-3. Selecting R-3 preserves its threshold in descendants, so further rounds need not reduce zone size. Slight threshold differences provide three illustrative readings and a mean, not measured textbook data. Changing zones alone cannot establish mutation timing; classic replica-plating work supplies a separate line of evidence. Actual resistance has multiple genetic mechanisms. This is a virtual explanation without operational instructions for culturing or enriching resistant bacteria.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Anatomical and isolation limits</summary>

Bones follow shared structural relationships rather than measured 3D specimen coordinates. Absolute dimensions, joint details and whale digit segment counts are schematic, unsuitable for identification or measurement. Embryos emphasize homologous regions without asserting identical age or exact stage. Human tail regression retains derivatives including the coccyx.

The isolation model assumes opposing selection on one allele. Geography limits exchange without guaranteeing speciation. Fertility and infertility after reunion are independent outcomes to test, not predictions from a genetic-distance threshold. The biological species concept has limits for asexual organisms and fossils, among other cases.

</details>
