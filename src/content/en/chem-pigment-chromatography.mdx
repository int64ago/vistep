<section id="understand">

## Motion and residence separate a mixture

In the textbook’s historical account, calcium carbonate is the adsorbent and cotton supports the packed column. Different distributions between mobile liquid and stationary material produce different pigment migration speeds. The colored adsorbent zones are then removed separately and extracted with ethanol.

This is historical research, not a student operating procedure. Figure 1-10 on the same textbook page shows a modern general-purpose column. This film follows the historical prose and does not substitute that figure’s silica and sand for the calcium carbonate bed.

</section>

<section id="try">

## Change retention and watch separation

Set retention contrast to zero: the three component distributions coincide. Increasing contrast separates their mean migration speeds. Increasing band spreading raises overlap. The extraction view maintains each component’s inventory across the removed zones, residual adsorbent and ethanol solution.

A, B and C are illustrative components. Colors, retention factors and equal starting amounts are selected model values, not historical measurements or a universal order for chlorophyll, xanthophyll and carotene. Real ordering depends on the pigments, solvent and adsorbent.

</section>

<section id="deeper">

<details>
<summary>Retention model, band width and historical evidence</summary>

For ideal linear retention, k is the component amount in stationary phase divided by its amount in mobile phase. The mobile fraction is 1/(1+k), giving average migration speed u/(1+k), where u is mobile-liquid speed. Default selected factors are 4, 1.8 and 0.4; the equal-retention comparison uses 2 for each. These establish causality rather than fitting textbook band positions.

The representative particle moves during the same mobile fraction of each period and stays during the rest. This deterministic residence illustration is not stochastic adsorption kinetics. Macroscopic bands use normalized triangular profiles with finite support. Their centers follow average velocity and their selected width increases with migration time. Integration over 160 spatial cells preserves an initial amount of one for each component. All distributions remain inside the column during the displayed interval. Elution beyond the outlet, nonlinear competitive adsorption and actual solvent flow rates are omitted.

Cuts lie midway between adjacent modeled band centers. Inventory is allocated by actual overlap lengths between cells and cuts, allowing several components in a colored section. The overlap measure sums the smaller fraction of two distributions in each cell; it is not experimentally measured sample purity. The sideways exploded view identifies removed solid sections. Shape and color intensity are not calibrated mass or absorption-spectrum measurements.

Ethanol extraction selects 92% final transfer to liquid, leaving 8% on the solid while conserving each component. This is not a historical yield or a real solvent-quantity/time/efficiency law. Color is a state identifier, not a computed mixed absorption spectrum.

The textbook uses 1903 as a historical publication milestone. Accessible translated excerpts from the 1906 primary papers describe multiple zones under different solvent conditions and caution that a zone need not be absolutely pure. This film’s three illustrative bands are not a reconstruction of the complete original chromatogram.

</details>
