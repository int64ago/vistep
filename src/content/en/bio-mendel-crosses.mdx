<h2 id="understand">Has a trait that disappears for a generation really vanished?</h2>

Pure tall and short parents produce tall first-generation offspring. That observation alone neither reveals the factors nor proves that the short factor remains. The next generation supplies a constraint: the textbook records 787 tall and 277 short plants. The return of short plants challenges explanations in which the factor permanently blends or disappears.

The film shows that record before introducing a hypothesis that can predict another result. Paired factors segregate into gametes and pair again at fertilization. Equally likely D and d gametes combine into DD, Dd, dD and dd. The middle two represent the same heterozygous genotype, giving an expected 1:2:1 genotype ratio and, with complete dominance, a 3:1 phenotype ratio.

An explanation must do more than reproduce the existing result. Crossing Dd with a short parent that supplies only d instead predicts equal tall and short offspring. This **new test-cross prediction** can confront another experiment. Two traits follow the same logic: observe new green-round and yellow-wrinkled combinations, then derive different selfing and test-cross predictions from the gamete hypothesis.

<h2 id="try">Change a hypothesis and test its prediction</h2>

- Change the tested tall parent from Dd to DD. Why does the expected test-cross result change? Can a finite all-tall sample absolutely exclude Dd?
- Allow only YR and yr gametes in the two-trait counterexample. Can this specified coupling phase without recombination explain yellow-wrinkled and green-round offspring?
- Swap the parental roles and compare probabilities with the two fixed textbook records. Equal probabilities do not require identical finite counts.
- Follow the same ball out of its bucket, into a pairing and back again. Larger samples tend to stabilize frequencies, but the next draw can increase the deviation.

The five learning entries retain Required 2, Chapter 1, P 3, P 6, P 7, P 9 and P 11 in textbook order. The P 3 and P 9 windows each finish with a new prediction; later test-cross windows reveal the corresponding record. Changing exploratory inputs never recalculates those records.

<h2 id="deeper">What the records and model actually establish</h2>

<details>
<summary>Fixed records and two textbook adaptations</summary>

The textbook’s 787/277 selfing counts agree with the stem-height experiment in the consulted English translation of Mendel’s paper. Its 87/79 test-cross counts also have an original source, but the paper presents them as **stem-height marginals** among 166 flowering offspring in an experiment involving flower color and stem height together. The film follows the textbook by isolating height; this is not a claim that the original contained a separate monohybrid table.

For two-trait selfing, yellow-round, green-round, yellow-wrinkled and green-wrinkled counts are 315, 108, 101 and 32. For test crosses, in the order yellow-round, yellow-wrinkled, green-round and green-wrinkled, the textbook gives 31, 27, 26, 26 with F₁ as seed parent and 24, 22, 25, 26 with F₁ as pollen parent. The corresponding last value in the consulted original-paper translation is **27**. The film explicitly labels its values as fixed textbook records, retains 26 and discloses the discrepancy here.

These are source records, never outputs of the probability model or random seed. Sources are the relevant stem-height, hybrid-progeny and two-trait test-cross passages in [Mendel’s original paper in English translation at MendelWeb](https://cs.brown.edu/people/rblumber/MendelWeb/Mendel.html), together with the textbook passages. The university-hosted translation was read; a scanned German original was not independently verified.

<details>
<summary>Factor transmission is a hypothesis diagram</summary>

Letter capsules trace which hypothetical parental factor copy a gamete carries. Maternal and paternal contributions retain different colors and outlines, with faint source references. They are neither unreplicated genes leaving a literal living cell nor molecules directly observed by Mendel.

The main model assumes equal segregation probabilities, random fertilization, complete dominance and comparable viability. Two traits additionally require independent assortment. The YR/yr-only counterexample specifies a coupling phase and excludes recombination; it does not represent all linkage. Differential viability, incomplete dominance and other mechanisms can alter phenotype predictions.

The ball activity is seeded pseudorandom sampling with replacement. A fixed seed reproduces the same sequence. Frequencies tend to stabilize with larger samples, rather than moving monotonically closer to the expected ratio after every draw. Agreement supports the hypothesis within its conditions; it does not by itself exclude every alternative.

</details>
