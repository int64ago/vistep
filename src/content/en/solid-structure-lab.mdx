<h2 id="understand">Shape, heat flow and layered structure provide different evidence</h2>

Salt grains often show regular crystal faces, whereas rosin fragments are irregular. Shape offers a clue but cannot alone classify a broken fragment. Crystals have periodic internal order; amorphous solids lack this long-range periodicity. The particle inset is a structural diagram, not an optical microscope image of atoms.

Coat glass and mica sheets with thin wax, then heat one point from behind. The melted region marks where temperature reaches the melting threshold. Glass produces a nearly circular region; mica can produce an ellipse, revealing different in-plane heat spreading along different directions. Anisotropy must refer to a particular property and directions, not every property of every crystal.

Adhesive-tape exfoliation exploits graphite’s layered structure. Repeated peeling, selection and transfer can yield graphene monolayers, retaining a honeycomb carbon network. Real peeling does not reliably halve each flake.

<h2 id="deeper">Model and scale limits</h2>

The thermal field integrates a continuous point-source kernel in an anisotropic 2D sheet. A 0.5-second pre-diffusion regularizes the source; the 50 °C contour represents melting. Illustrative diffusivities are 0.4 mm²/s for glass and 0.8 and 0.25 mm²/s along mica’s two directions. These are teaching parameters, not measurements of a specific material. Edge losses, latent heat and temperature-dependent parameters are omitted.
