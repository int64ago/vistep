<h2 id="understand">Magnetic fields can brake or drive</h2>

Disturb a sensitive meter and its pointer oscillates. Short its terminals: the moving winding becomes a closed circuit, and induced current produces a torque opposing motion. The pointer settles sooner. The energy came from the initial disturbance, not from an added power source.

A rotating magnet supplies continuing mechanical input. Induced current creates a torque on the aluminium frame, which begins to follow. Some slip remains between frame and rotating field. Without relative motion, this effective model would have no driving torque.

A copper disc slowing near magnetic poles, a magnet falling through an aluminium tube, and a spring magnet oscillating above a closed coil each reveal the same energy transfer through a different apparatus. Electrical conduction can convert mechanical energy to heat without magnetic attraction or physical contact.

<h2 id="try">Preserve the initial conditions</h2>

- Compare open and shorted meter terminals at the same initial pointer angle.
- Preserve the disc's initial speed and move the magnet away or near its edge.
- Compare a complete aluminium tube with a longitudinally slit one.
- Keep spring displacement fixed and open or close the coil.
- Reverse the rotating magnet and inspect frame direction and slip.

<h2 id="deeper">Each apparatus has its own motion equation</h2>

<div className="formula">F = −cv; braking torque τ = −bω; driving torque τ = k(Ω−ω)</div>

These are effective low-speed models. Disc speed follows its torque equation rather than prescribed animation. Reversing the field does not reverse braking. Tube motion balances gravity and velocity drag; the magnet stops at the catch position one metre below. A slit reduces large circulating paths while retaining weak residual damping. The spring model is linearized about equilibrium. The frame uses an average slip-torque relation, separating mechanical output from slip heating.

Coefficients illustrate mechanisms rather than fit particular instruments. The models omit field-mesh solutions, skin effects, saturation and contact friction. Current loops indicate paths, not calculated field contours. Initial oscillation or rotation energy dissipates; the rotating magnet instead receives continuing external mechanical power.
