<h2 id="understand">Retain sex and parental origin alongside overall ratios</h2>

Morgan’s fly crosses require more than totals of red and white eyes. Recording sex reveals the association of white eyes with males in F2. An X-recessive model explains it and predicts distinct test-cross and reciprocal-cross outcomes. White-eyed females can occur: sex association is not restriction to one sex.

Dalton’s observations introduced a research question. The scene compares reported observations rather than using an arbitrary screen filter to claim his exact visual experience. Human pedigrees are observational evidence, not deliberately arranged genetic crosses.

<h2 id="deeper">Pedigrees and population proportions answer different questions</h2>

<summary>Assumptions, selection bias and statistical limits</summary>

The model assumes the textbook’s single X-recessive locus, full penetrance and typical diploid XX/XY combinations. It computes gamete-based classes rather than personal medical risks. Color-vision differences have several genetic and non-genetic causes; a single pedigree cannot establish every mechanism. Human B/b and fly W/w refer to distinct example loci, not the same gene.

All 20 textbook individuals and parent–child relationships are retained. Desktop shows the complete tree; mobile shows one branch with common ancestors. Dots represent inference rather than original observations.

The 200 participants and affected states are synthetic. Restricting selection to households already containing affected people changes the sampling frame. Sex-specific analyses require corresponding denominators. Pooling 2/20 and 1/80 gives 3/100 = 3%, not the unweighted mean of 10% and 1.25%. Individual IDs remove duplicate registrations. This is a cross-sectional sample proportion, not an incidence rate of new cases during follow-up or an update to old textbook population figures. High myopia, mentioned in the textbook, can have diverse causes and must not universally be forced into this single-locus model.
