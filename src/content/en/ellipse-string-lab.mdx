---
name: Drawing an ellipse with string
title: Why does a taut thread trace an ellipse?
---

<section id="understand">

The activity on page 45 of Required Book 2, Chapter 7 §1, uses two pins, one thread and a pencil. Attach the thread ends separately to the pins and slide the pencil while keeping the thread taut.

The pins are the foci. Fixed thread length keeps the sum of the pencil’s distances to both foci constant. The center is midway between the foci; a focus is usually not the center.

</section>
<section id="try">

Watch one segment lengthen while the other shortens. Trace the whole curve and check that their sum remains one metre. The full major axis also equals one metre.

Keep thread length fixed and move the foci closer. The minor axis grows until coincident foci produce a circle. Its radius equals half the thread length, which was already the semimajor axis.

</section>
<section id="deeper">

<details>

<summary>Geometric model and constraints</summary>

The semimajor axis is a = L/2, focal offset c = d/2 and semiminor axis b = √(a²−c²). Points x = acosθ, y = bsinθ have distance sum L. Valid settings require 0 ≤ d &lt; L; equal separation and thread length would give a degenerate line segment, not an ordinary ellipse.

Pins and pencil contact are ideal points, and the thread stays taut and inextensible. Only its two ends are pinned; no extra thread segment joins the foci. Pencil parameter speed is chosen for clear geometry, not planetary motion, and cannot verify Kepler’s area law. Finite pin and pencil radii or slack thread introduce errors in a real drawing.

</details>

</section>
