<h2 id="understand">Uncertain individual outcomes produce repeatable distributions</h2>

At each peg a Galton-board ball can deflect either way. One landing bin is uncertain, but many balls usually collect near the centre more often than at the edges. This is an analogy for statistical regularity, not a model of the molecular speed distribution itself.

Four fair coins can show zero through four heads. Those five categories are not equally likely: two heads can occur through more arrangements. Pooling individual and class observations generally improves agreement with theoretical probabilities, without guaranteeing monotonic improvement in every sample.

Falling beans provide a collision analogy. One bean creates a brief effect; a continuous stream produces a smoother average. Greater drop height increases impact speed and momentum. Beans that remain on the pan also add static weight, which must be separated from collision force.

<h2 id="try">Preserve the comparison process</h2>

- Track one ball through all its deflections, then inspect two hundred landings.
- Increase four-coin trials from ten to four hundred and compare category frequencies.
- Compare a single bean with a stream, then change only release height.
- Wait after all beans land: the impact contribution vanishes while accumulated weight remains.

The eight-row board uses independent equal-probability choices and paths that clear its pegs. It does not solve real friction, spin or elastic contacts. Fixed random inputs make seeking repeatable. Finite samples still fluctuate.

Beans stop on the pan. A 0.08-second first-order scale response includes collision impulses and accumulating gravitational load. An ideal gas particle rebounding normally from a wall transfers twice its incident momentum and does not remain on the wall. The bean experiment illustrates the average effect of many discrete collisions, not every microscopic detail.
