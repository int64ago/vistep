<section id="understand">
## Ions must be mobile

Dry sodium chloride and potassium nitrate contain ions, but their lattices restrict motion. Dissolution releases existing ions into the water, providing mobile charge carriers. Connecting a circuit gives the ions directed drift; the connection does not cause the salt to dissociate.

Positive ions drift toward the negative electrode and negative ions toward the positive electrode. Negative charge moving oppositely to positive charge contributes to the same conventional-current direction.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Change the sample and circuit separately

Compare dry salts, distilled water and salt solutions. Open the circuit: the solution still contains ions. Reconnect and inspect their opposing short-time displacements.

The concentration control covers only dilute solutions, 0–1 mmol/L. At zero salt concentration the reading returns to the ideal pure-water background, not exactly zero. A bulb can fail to indicate distilled water’s weak conductivity.

</section>

<details>
<summary>Numerical basis and omissions</summary>

At 25°C, limiting ionic conductances are 50.1 for sodium, 76.3 for chloride, 73.5 for potassium and 71.4 for nitrate, in S·cm²/mol. The model adds ionic contributions and uses an illustrative cell constant of 1 cm⁻¹ and 3 V to calculate short-time current. The 10 µA indication threshold is a teaching setting, not a calibration of the textbook bulb. Infinite-dilution relations are approximate at finite concentration.

Hydrogen and hydroxide ion contributions at concentrations of 10⁻⁷ mol/L give a pure-water background of about 0.05478 µS/cm. Real distilled water can absorb gases or acquire impurities. The enlarged view omits water molecules, thermal motion and electrode reactions. It shows local drift directions, not the full electrolysis process under prolonged current.
