<section id="understand">
## Flame colour combines several wavelengths

Sodium salts give a yellow flame and potassium salts a lilac appearance. The textbook uses cobalt-blue glass when observing potassium to reduce sodium-yellow interference. A filter changes transmitted wavelengths, not sample identity. Checking the clean wire is part of the control.

Separating light by wavelength provides more specific evidence. Atoms can emit photons when moving to lower-energy states or absorb matching photons in allowed upward transitions. Photon energy equals the energy gap.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Separate sample, interference and filter

Select sodium or potassium, alter yellow-light interference and insert or remove cobalt glass. Attenuation changes line strength without moving a line to a new wavelength.

The energy view compares emission and absorption through one transition. The spectrum view retains the textbook examples lithium, helium and mercury, with selected bright and dark lines. Multiple line positions provide stronger identification evidence than a single blended colour.

</section>

Selected positions use NIST neutral-atom air wavelengths, converted from angstroms to nanometres by dividing by10. The sodium doublet is588.995 and589.5924nm, separated by0.5974nm. The photon-energy calculation approximates vacuum wavelengths with air wavelengths, a difference of a few parts in ten thousand; this is not precision spectrometry.

Line widths, intensities, flame colours and the two-passband cobalt-glass curve are illustrative. Some potassium lines lie near the red edge of human vision. The display is neither a colour-matching computation nor a complete line list. Absorption comparisons require suitable lower-state populations: emission lines do not all become equally strong absorption lines under arbitrary conditions. Horizontal energy levels are not classical electron orbits.
