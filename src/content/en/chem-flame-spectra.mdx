<section id="understand">
## The filter changes the light reaching your eye

The textbook begins with a clean-wire control, then sodium's yellow and potassium's lilac appearance. Adding sodium adds another set of wavelengths; the potassium lines retain their positions. Cobalt glass attenuates light by wavelength. It cannot turn sodium into potassium or move sodium lines to potassium positions.

Connect one sodium line to its two atomic energy states. When the atom reaches the lower state, a photon carries away the same energy. Keep that lower state and choose a higher allowed upper state: the larger gap produces a shorter wavelength. The second sodium transition here is near 330 nm, in the ultraviolet. Its neutral marker represents invisible radiation.

Absorption also requires an appropriate initial state and allowed transition. Follow an unmatched photon and then a matching one. In the selected-pair model, one passes while the other can transfer its energy to the atom. Greater photon energy does not guarantee absorption by that state pair.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Keep the record fixed while changing the explanation

The unknown spectrum is a fixed synthetic teaching record. First inspect only the yellow region, then reveal the observed wavelength range. Switching lithium, helium and mercury references never changes the unknown. Several matching positions support mercury within this limited library. After adding sodium lines, no displayed pure element explains every observation. This is a test of a finite reference set, not unique identification of a real sample.

The lithium comparison uses separately prepared samples. Ground-state sample A illustrates a selected absorption near 670.8 nm and a possible radiative return outside the detector direction. Samples B and C use a separate weak probe near 610.4 nm. That transition begins at 2p: the state is empty in B, while C is prepared with atoms occupying it. The initial state determines whether this selected absorption can occur.

Explore the salt, sodium interference and glass. Choose between real allowed sodium transitions, trace one event, or test whether extra observed lines defeat a single-element explanation.

</section>

<details>
<summary>Energy, wavelength and model limits</summary>

<div className="formula">ΔE = hν = hc / λ (vacuum)</div>

The NIST sodium 3s–3p gap is about 2.10443 eV. Its vacuum wavelength is about 589.158 nm, whereas the tabulated air wavelength is 588.995 nm. The higher 4p state gives a gap of about 3.75332 eV to the same 3s state: about 330.332 nm in vacuum and 330.237 nm in air. Reference strips use air wavelengths; the exact energy account uses independent level data. Discrete state markers do not trace an electron orbit through intermediate energies.

Line widths, relative strengths, flame colours and cobalt-glass transmission are illustrative, without instrument or human-colour calibration. Input and output use a common intensity scale; a nearly extinguished signal is not rescaled to full brightness. The selected lines are from neutral gas-phase atoms, which are not identical to hydrated ions in solution.

A depicted photon event is a selected outcome, not a probability model of every possible interaction. The 670.8 nm reference and 610.4 nm trials have distinct sample identities. They do not form one shared sample's complete net transmission spectrum. Preparing 2p also affects other spontaneous and stimulated transitions; weak light alone does not eliminate those processes. The model concerns each freshly prepared initial state and selected pair, without continuous pumping, thermal populations or complete spectra. The illustrated off-axis photon is one possible radiative return, not a claim about every decay route or direction.
