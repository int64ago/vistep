<section id="understand">

The research chapter of Required Book 2 (pages 102–105) investigates flicking water from a hand using video, geometric estimates and a phone sensor, then proposes javelin throwing as another topic. This film distinguishes textbook data, an independent articulated illustration and a research proposal.

Upper-arm, forearm and hand rotation jointly determine fingertip position and velocity. Their contributions add as vectors, not as speed magnitudes. A detached drop retains its release velocity and then accelerates under gravity; flying away does not imply an extra outward force.

</section>
<section id="try">

The textbook calibrates its photograph with a measured shoulder-to-fingertip length of 65 cm. At 25 frames per second, adjacent frames are 0.04 s apart. A 26 cm fingertip displacement gives 6.5 m/s average speed. Approximating the final segment as local circular motion with radius 17 cm gives about 249 m/s², or 25g. These are approximate values from one textbook study, not measurements of this animation or universal human performance.

The phone measures the wrist, with wrist rotation suppressed and the phone aligned with the forearm. The textbook reports about 32 m/s². A speed ratio of 2.2 and radius ratio of 3/5 suggest a fingertip normal acceleration roughly eight times larger, about 256 m/s². This is comparable in magnitude to the photographic estimate. The unrounded ratio is about 8.07; retaining it does not improve the original data’s precision.

Fix the model wrist to compare trajectories and speeds, or change forward run-up speed while preserving the same arm configuration. The javelin proposal considers release-velocity composition and quantities to measure. It does not predict flight distance or provide athletic coaching.

</section>
<section id="deeper">

<details>

<summary>Articulated model and local circular approximation</summary>

Three rigid links represent upper arm, forearm and hand with lengths 0.25, 0.23 and 0.17 m. These segment lengths and angle curves are illustrative inputs, not fits to the textbook photographs or a particular participant. Smooth quintic joint-angle curves provide analytical position, velocity and acceleration. Locking the wrist changes all three.

In general the wrist itself accelerates. Dividing squared ground-relative fingertip speed by the wrist-to-tip distance is therefore not an exact ground-frame normal acceleration. The full trajectory’s velocity and acceleration vectors determine normal acceleration and curvature radius, which need not equal hand length. The textbook’s 25g remains a local circular approximation; the ratio cross-check inherits similar assumptions.

The illustrated drop detaches at a prescribed time and then follows drag-free projectile motion. Adhesion, surface tension and skin contact are absent, so the model does not predict the detachment threshold.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Phone readings and research design</summary>

A raw accelerometer measures specific force: coordinate acceleration minus gravitational acceleration, resolved along the rotating phone axes. A stationary phone has raw magnitude approximately g but zero gravity-corrected coordinate acceleration. The scene displays both model readings on the same axis instead of equating a raw signal with centripetal acceleration. Real analysis must establish sensor location, range, axes, sampling rate and whether the app removes gravity.

A javelin study would measure run-up, trunk and joint timing, and release direction. The current illustration adds uniform shoulder translation to the same prescribed joint motion to isolate a transport-velocity contribution. It omits muscle dynamics, a whole-body kinetic chain, spear bending and post-release aerodynamics. Research conclusions require recorded data and uncertainty analysis.

</details>
