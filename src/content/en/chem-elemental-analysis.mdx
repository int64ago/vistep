<h2 id="understand">Understand the principle</h2>

Liebig’s quantitative elemental analysis converts carbon and hydrogen into collectable CO₂ and H₂O. Weigh the sample, remove water with anhydrous CaCl₂, then collect CO₂ in KOH. Separate before-and-after absorber masses reveal the amounts of the two products.

The connected apparatus follows the textbook’s copper-oxide oxidation and separate absorption principles. Its bulb assembly is an explanatory reconstruction, not a replica of one historical artifact. Enlarged product markers represent portions of material; their capture states drive the mass readings.

m(C) = m(CO₂) × 12.01 / 44.01.

m(H) = m(H₂O) × 2.016 / 18.016.

Only if the sample contains just C, H and O can oxygen be obtained by subtracting carbon and hydrogen masses from the sample mass. Product oxygen also comes from the oxidant, so it cannot all be assigned to the original sample.

<h2 id="try">Try it yourself</h2>

The default ideal sample has a mass of 0.46068 g and produces 0.54048 g of water and 0.88020 g of CO₂. Unrounded calculations give a C:H:O ratio of 2:6:1. Displayed percentages are rounded and are not fed back into the calculation. These are teaching data, not Liebig’s original records.

Change sample mass and compare the proportional absorber gains. Leave the drying tube empty: water then reaches the alkaline absorber along with CO₂. Treating that combined gain as CO₂ falsely raises the carbon result. Remove the assumption that only C, H and O are present, and the oxygen result becomes unknown.

<h2 id="deeper">Go deeper</h2>

The textbook separately gives a rounded example with 52.2% carbon and 13.1% hydrogen, leaving 34.7% oxygen. Dividing by atomic masses and reducing gives approximately 2:6:1. That benchmark is tested separately rather than silently substituted for the animation’s ideal mass data.

Elemental analysis generally yields an empirical formula. Relative molecular mass establishes its molecular-formula multiple; further evidence establishes connectivity. Ethanol and dimethyl ether demonstrate why equal composition does not identify structure.

<summary>Conditions behind the calculation</summary>

The sample must be pure, oxidation and collection complete, and the gas train leak-free. Environmental water and CO₂, absorber evaporation and interfering reactions must be controlled. Calibration, blank correction and detection limits are omitted. Copper-oxide oxygen accounting explains the added product mass; the drawing is not an operating protocol.
