<h2 id="understand">How can traveling waves make a stationary pattern?</h2>

Two equal-frequency, equal-amplitude waves traveling in opposite directions can form a standing wave. Its ingredients still travel, but some positions in the sum never move. These are nodes. Between neighboring nodes, the largest oscillation occurs at an antinode.

The film first separates the component waves, then adds the same phases. Dark nodes stay fixed while gold markers oscillate at the antinodes. Marker identities belong to positions on the string rather than to traveling crests.

<h2 id="try">Change the mode while keeping the ends fixed</h2>

Choose harmonics one through five, adjust tension and inspect any part of a cycle. Both endpoints remain nodes. Higher mode number adds internal nodes and shortens wavelength. Higher tension increases speed and frequency while leaving the chosen mode's node positions unchanged.

The energy strip compares integrated kinetic and elastic energy. Near the straight equilibrium shape, velocity is greatest; at maximum deformation, velocity vanishes. Zero displacement at a node does not mean the neighboring string stores no elastic energy.

<h2 id="deeper">Boundaries select the wavelengths</h2>

For an ideal string of length L, linear density μ and tension T, v = √(T/μ). Fixed ends require L = nλ/2, so fₙ = nv/(2L) for positive integer n. The model uses L = 1 m, μ = 0.01 kg/m and initially T = 4 N. The visual motion is slowed for observation.

Adding A sin(kx−ωt) and A sin(kx+ωt) gives y = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt). Choosing k = nπ/L makes both endpoints exactly zero at every time. Energy density is μ(∂y/∂t)²/2 + T(∂y/∂x)²/2; the strip approximates its spatial integral.

This is one normal mode of a uniform, lossless, small-amplitude string. It excludes bending stiffness, radiation, driven buildup and mixed modes. The opening deliberately isolates a traveling component; that component alone is not presented as a complete fixed-end solution.
