<h2 id="understand">A fridge moves heat rather than creating cold</h2>

The cooling compartment gets colder while the heat-releasing parts outside feel warm. These are connected effects: the same circuit transports heat from one side to the other.

Heat naturally flows from warmer to cooler places. Moving it continuously from a cold compartment into a warmer room requires work. The compressor uses electricity to establish different pressures and temperatures around the circuit.

Follow the marker. The compressor compresses vapor. Hot refrigerant releases heat and condenses outside. Pressure drops through the expansion device, producing a cooler liquid–vapor mixture. Inside, the refrigerant boils and absorbs heat. Vapor returns to the compressor.

**Refrigerant is not a disposable ice cube. It repeatedly changes state in a closed loop.**

<h2 id="try">Why does more heat come out than was taken from the food?</h2>

With an electrical input of 80 W and a cooling COP of 2.5, the fridge removes 200 joules from the cabinet each second. It also consumes 80 joules of electrical energy. The room receives 280 W of heat.

<div className="formula">Heat released = heat absorbed + electrical work</div>

A larger COP means more heat moved per unit of work. COP can exceed one because it measures heat transported, not conversion of electricity into heat.

> Could you cool the whole room by leaving the fridge door open? Treat both sides as one system and repeat the energy balance.

If the hot side stays in the room, the running fridge adds net heat corresponding to its electrical input. An air conditioner can cool a room because it releases that heat outdoors.

<h2 id="deeper">What do pressure and phase changes contribute?</h2>

<details>
<summary>Why compress first, then reduce pressure?</summary>

Boiling and condensation conditions depend on pressure. The high-pressure side allows heat rejection at a temperature above the room's; the low-pressure side allows heat absorption below the cabinet's temperature.

Throttling is usually approximated as constant enthalpy. Pressure drops and some liquid flashes into vapor. A narrow pipe does not create cold from nothing, and throttling is not equivalent to a piston extracting expansion work.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Where does the COP come from?</summary>

Here it is an input chosen to isolate the energy balance. Real COP depends on evaporating and condensing temperatures, refrigerant, heat exchange and equipment losses. This is not a live fridge measurement. Thermostat cycling, defrosting and heat leakage through the door are omitted.

</details>
