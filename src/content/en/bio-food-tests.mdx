<h2 id="understand">Conditions are part of the evidence</h2>

Mix equal volumes of the two Fehling solutions before adding them to the sample. A 50–65 °C water bath develops the brick-red precipitate used to detect reducing sugars. Unhydrolyzed sucrose normally gives no such response, so a negative result does not establish that a sample contains no sugar.

Sudan III stains lipid droplets orange. Washing away background dye makes them easier to distinguish in a thin section. The Biuret assay instead requires alkaline solution A followed by a little copper-containing solution B; proteins turn violet without a warm bath.

<h2 id="try">Keep the control alongside the procedure</h2>

- Disable warming and decide whether the pear assay is ready for interpretation.
- Substitute unhydrolyzed sucrose to distinguish sugar from reducing sugar.
- Scrub the peanut preparation before and after washing away excess stain.
- Reverse the Biuret addition order and examine why a flawed procedure cannot establish a reliable negative.

<h2 id="deeper">This is not a concentration meter</h2>

<details>
<summary>What does the model represent?</summary>

A qualitative state machine records material type, reagent steps, warming and endpoint. Color development illustrates procedure progress, not measured reaction kinetics or concentration. Samples represent clear textbook cases. Real foods contain multiple substances, and dilution, background color and reagent condition affect detection. Water blanks accompany tube assays. The lipid image is schematic and has no invented calibrated microscope scale.

</details>
