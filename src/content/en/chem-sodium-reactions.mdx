<section id="understand">
## Keep conditions attached to evidence

Sodium is soft and has a silvery fresh surface that soon tarnishes. The textbook represents the initial room-temperature oxide as sodium oxide. Heated sodium melts and burns with a yellow flame, leaving pale-yellow sodium peroxide. Different conditions must not be collapsed into one product.

Sodium and water produce sodium hydroxide and hydrogen. Floating relates to density, rounding to heating and melting, and indicator colour to alkalinity. Sodium peroxide also forms an alkaline solution with water, but its gas is oxygen, supported by a glowing-splint test.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Track the changing object

Select a fresh surface, heated sodium, sodium on water, peroxide, heated potassium or potassium on water. Progress controls observation stage and reaction extent, not real elapsed seconds. Follow decreasing metal volume and distinguish oxygen-testing evidence from hydrogen production.

The more vigorous potassium reaction comes from the textbook teacher demonstration. Its discussion of lithium is a structure-based prediction, not a three-metal quantitative rate measurement. This scene explains evidence without providing a home experiment procedure.

</section>

Extent determines gas, hydroxide and remaining solid. Sodium and potassium mass balances are tested separately. Droplet radius follows the cube root of remaining material. Ideal buoyancy equates displaced water volume with metal mass, using density ratios0.97 and0.89 for sodium and potassium. Bubble support, surface tension and changing shape are omitted.

Motion, flames, warming and paper colour are qualitative, not hydrodynamic or kinetic predictions. Peroxide uses the textbook net equation; intermediate hydrogen peroxide can occur. Long air exposure and potassium oxidation can produce more complex mixtures, so the scene does not invent a unique product from limited observations.
