<section id="understand">
## Follow what actually changes

A fresh sodium face dulls at the exposed surface while a metal core remains. The film compares dry oxygen-containing air with oxygen exclusion as a virtual explanatory control. Removing oxygen prevents new change in this model; it does not turn the existing layer back into metal. The textbook represents the initial room-temperature product as Na₂O. Real humid air can also involve water and carbon dioxide.

Under the textbook heating conditions, the product is Na₂O₂. Sodium is +1 in both compounds, but oxygen differs. Oxide oxygen is −II; a peroxide O₂²⁻ unit has total charge −2, so each oxygen is −I. Consequently, 4 mol Na needs 1 mol O₂ to form Na₂O, or 2 mol O₂ to form Na₂O₂. Atom and electron accounting establishes this distinction. Flame colour alone does not, and temperature alone does not predict real-air phase composition.

When sodium meets water, water supplies the hydrogen in H₂. The two electron units lost by two sodium atoms correspond to two water hydrogen atoms changing from +I to zero. The remaining oxygen and hydrogen form hydroxide. In peroxide, sodium is already +1. Its water reaction instead involves peroxide oxygen changing from −I to both zero and −II in the net account. A gold ring preserves the identity of a tracked atom; the film does not claim a unique microscopic pathway.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Test the explanation with a counterexample

In exploration, switch off oxygen contact and add another model exposure. The existing layer should remain without growing. Only resetting to a fresh face removes that accumulated layer.

Compare metal sodium, sodium oxide and sodium peroxide, each containing **4 mmol sodium atoms**, with excess water. They give 2 mmol H₂, no gas, and 1 mmol O₂ respectively, yet each gives 4 mmol OH⁻. Oxide reacting with water is a theoretical comparison on the same textbook page. The film labels it as a prediction rather than another performed experiment. An alkaline result does not identify the gas.

Finally compare sodium and potassium. Equal amounts in moles both give 1 mol H₂ per 2 mol metal. A more vigorous potassium demonstration does not create extra hydrogen. At this textbook location, a gentler lithium response remains a prediction informed by structure.

</section>

<summary>Conservation and the limits of the model</summary>

<div className="formula">4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O</div>
<div className="formula">2Na + O₂ → Na₂O₂</div>
<div className="formula">2Na + 2H₂O → 2Na⁺ + 2OH⁻ + H₂</div>
<div className="formula">Na₂O + H₂O → 2Na⁺ + 2OH⁻</div>
<div className="formula">2Na₂O₂ + 2H₂O → 4Na⁺ + 4OH⁻ + O₂</div>

The material account checks Na, O, H and charge together. Moving atoms retain their identities. Electron markers represent net allocation, not a measurable pool of free electrons in water. Formal oxidation states are not measurements of every atom’s physical charge. Local formula units are counted within extended ionic solids, not depicted as isolated molecules.

Peroxide water chemistry can involve hydrogen peroxide followed by its decomposition. The illustrated net atom mapping conserves matter, but is neither an isotope-provenance experiment nor a claim of one concerted molecular step.

Metal volume follows the amount remaining. The submerged spherical-cap **volume fraction**, not its diameter fraction, equals the density ratio. Sodium uses 0.97 and potassium uses the RSC value of 0.89 g/cm³; the textbook table lists 0.86 for potassium. These source values must not be silently relabelled as identical. Surface tension, gas support and hydrodynamics are omitted. Paths, warming, flame and progress are qualitative; the model supplies no measured rates, temperatures or pH values.

NIST first ionization energies for Li, Na and K are approximately 5.392, 5.139 and 4.341 eV. These concern isolated neutral ground-state gas atoms. They support an outer-electron removal trend, not an aqueous rate law or an electrode-potential calculation. Solvation, melting, heat transfer and interface contact introduce other factors.
