<h2 id="understand">Understand the principle</h2>

This scene covers the chirality mechanism on PEP Selective Compulsory 2 page 60 and the historical account of Pasteur on page 61. A, B, C and D are group labels, not elemental symbols. The single tetrahedral centre explains superposition; it is not a complete tartrate structure.

Enantiomers are mirror images that cannot coincide through translation and proper rotation alone. Group identity is preserved. A 180° rotation around the chosen spatial axis aligns A and B but exchanges C and D. If those two groups are identical, the interchange leaves no distinguishable mismatch and this model becomes achiral. See the [IUPAC enantiomer definition](https://goldbook.iupac.org/terms/view/E02069).

This four-distinct-group centre is an introductory case. Multiple stereocentres need not make the whole molecule chiral, and axial or other chirality need not use this type of centre. Meso tartaric acid and a racemic mixture are not the same substance merely because both may show no rotation.

<h2 id="try">Try it yourself</h2>

Compare hollow reference rings with the filled moving groups. The alignment button chooses the 180° position of a proper rotation; it neither reflects again nor exchanges labels. The signed volume of four labelled tetrahedral vertices keeps its sign under proper rotations and reverses under reflection, giving a geometric impossibility argument beyond a single failed trial.

The historical sequence magnifies small facets, tracks individual sorting, then dissolves the groups separately. Sodium ammonium tartrate formed separately sortable crystal types under the relevant conditions. Not every racemate permits this manual separation. Opposite rotations remaining in solution extend the evidence beyond macroscopic crystal form. See the [University of Illinois exhibition of Pasteur's 1848 paper and figures](https://xray-exhibit.scs.illinois.edu/books/pasteur.php).

The eight tracked crystals are illustrative, not a statistical sample. Their morphology is a chiral polyhedron built from unequal prism axes and alternating corner truncations. Reflection changes actual facet geometry; this is not an indexed tartrate crystal or unit-cell reconstruction. Forms A and B are diagram labels, not direct assignments of R/S or optical sign. The solutions are clear; visual colours aid reading rather than imply different enantiomer colours.

<h2 id="deeper">Go deeper</h2>

Angles are viewed toward incoming light. Positive denotes clockwise and negative anticlockwise; R/S configuration cannot generally be translated into (+)/(−). Under fixed solvent, temperature, wavelength and path length, the teaching model uses α=30°×(c₊−c₋) for relative concentrations. The 30° coefficient is not a historical specific rotation. Real specific rotation requires stated conditions, concentration units and path units. See [IUPAC's optical-rotation angle definition](https://old.goldbook.iupac.org/pdf/A00348.pdf).

Light after polarizer P defines the 100% reference. Sample rotation and analyzer A at β give I/I₀=cos²(β−α), omitting absorption, scattering, windows and polarizer losses. The dial, number and detector share the same angle calculation. See [OpenStax on polarization and Malus's law](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/1-7-polarization).

Mixing varies composition at fixed total concentration. Equal amounts cancel optically without eliminating either component. Selective consumption instead starts both relative concentrations at 100. Consumed fractions are u and u(1−s), with s a teaching selectivity. This is material accounting, not fungal-growth kinetics or a fit to historical rates. Consumed material enters unspecified metabolic products. Rotation follows the remaining concentration difference, not enantiomeric excess alone: a tiny amount of pure enantiomer can still give only a small rotation.

Culture-sample measurements do not establish universal biological rules. Microbial selectivity depends on the particular system. Biological products are not necessarily all chiral or optically active, and drug action cannot be inferred from rotation direction alone.
