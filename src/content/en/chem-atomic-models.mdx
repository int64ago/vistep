<section id="understand">
## Let evidence change the picture

Dalton’s indivisible sphere is a historical hypothesis. A cathode-ray spot bends toward the positive plate and reverses when the field reverses. Thomson changed gases and cathodes, yet found mass-to-charge ratios of the same order. A common negative component requires internal structure. Neutrality requires positive charge too; spreading it through a sphere remains a further hypothesis.

Occasional large-angle alpha scattering adds another constraint. The comparison gives a broad sphere and a compact centre the same positive charge. With the stated modern parameters, the sphere cannot stop the head-on particle; the compact centre converts kinetic energy into potential energy until the particle turns. The historical observation stays fixed while the calculation changes.

Bohr’s discrete energies produce another test: an emitted photon carries the difference between two states. A larger gap predicts a shorter wavelength. Fixed hydrogen lines support that relationship without photographing circular electron paths. A modern orbital describes a quantum state, and cloud dots sample its probability density rather than count electrons.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Predict, then reveal

Reverse the plates, or choose a positive or neutral candidate, and inspect the computed spot. These controls do not rewrite historical records. In the scattering model, raise total energy from 5 MeV to 10 MeV. The fixed-centre Coulomb approximation predicts half the head-on turning distance.

Change the hydrogen transition from 3→2 to 4→2. The larger gap predicts a shorter wavelength. The page separately labels NIST air records and model vacuum predictions; its 1 nm display cannot test fine structure.

More cloud samples clarify the same distribution without adding electrons. A larger probability sphere encloses more probability; it is not a wall. The 1s/2s comparison uses one length scale and retains the 2s radial node. Each p orientation represents a separate state.

<summary>Records, calculations and scientific limits</summary>

**Cathode rays.** [Thomson’s 1897 paper](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Cathode_Rays) describes electric deflection and material comparisons. The table transcribes the seven second-method m/e values, 1.3, 1.1, 1.2, 1.5, 1.5, 1.3, 1.1, multiplied by 10⁻⁷ in the original units. The first five used aluminium cathodes, the last two platinum. Variation is preserved. The animation uses a modern uniform-field calculation with geometry derived from qE and flight time, with an independently enlarged transverse scale that differs from the horizontal scale. A charge-to-mass ratio alone does not separately determine mass.

**Scattering.** [Rutherford’s 1911 paper](https://personal.math.ubc.ca/~cass/rutherford/r.pdf), pp 669–671, discusses large deflections and concentrated charge, and notes that the scattering deductions alone do not determine the central charge’s sign. The modern example uses alpha charge +2e, central charge +79e, a diffuse radius of 100 pm and total energy 5 MeV. Omitting negative electrons makes the diffuse positive sphere an upper-bound positive barrier: its maximum is about 3.413 keV. A fixed point centre gives a head-on turn at about 45.503 fm. The views have different length scales and separate slow playback; their normalized progress is not a common physical flight time. Nuclear recoil is omitted, and the turn lies outside the gold nucleus. This is neither an angular-distribution fit nor a reconstruction of 1911 particle records.

**Spectra.** [Bohr’s 1913 paper](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/72787/pg72787-images.html) proposes stationary states and ΔE=hν, then tests the relationship against hydrogen spectra. The calculation uses reduced mass: R_H=R∞/(1+m_e/m_p) and λ_vac⁻¹=R_H(1/n_low²−1/n_high²), with [NIST CODATA constants](https://physics.nist.gov/cuu/Constants/Table/allascii.txt). Fixed examples are selected air fine components from the [NIST hydrogen table](https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/Handbook/Tables/hydrogentable2.htm). [NIST’s wavelength guidance](https://physics.nist.gov/PhysRefData/ASD/Html/lineshelp.html) distinguishes air and vacuum. Line widths and brightness are illustrative; observations are not adjusted to fit, and the energy-transfer glyph is not a classical electron trajectory.

**Probability.** In units of a₀, the 1s radial density is 4 r²exp(−2 r), with a 90% radius near 2.66116 a₀. The 2s density is r²(2−r)²exp(−r)/8 and has a node at 2 a₀; 2p uses r⁴exp(−r)/24 with a cos²θ angular distribution. The [MIT hydrogen lecture](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/5-61-physical-chemistry-fall-2007/c2078ff25c61c136e67ceb6fd8eb9b34_lecture21to22.pdf) provides the analytic basis. Deterministic low-discrepancy samples are generated in three dimensions before projection. Outside-sphere membership uses the 3D radius. Sample tails are limited to the 0.1%–99.9% quantiles, while cumulative probability uses the full analytic distribution. These are nonrelativistic, field-free hydrogen states, not general many-electron radii.

Both are model-learning positions, not student laboratory experiments.
