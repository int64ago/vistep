<h2 id="understand">How does a volume reading become a concentration?</h2>

An acid–base titration uses a known acid or base concentration to determine an unknown concentration in a measured sample. Pages 67–69 introduce the principle using 0.1000 mol/L NaOH added to 20.00 mL of 0.1000 mol/L HCl. pH changes steeply near 20.00 mL added. The demonstration has a known sample concentration to explain the principle; it cannot determine the concentration of your actual unknown sample in advance.

For the one-to-one HCl–NaOH reaction, stoichiometric equivalence means equal amounts of acid and base. Thus:

**c(NaOH) = c(HCl) × V(HCl) ÷ V(NaOH).**

Burette numbers increase downward. Delivered volume is **final reading minus initial reading**. The textbook activity starts at zero; exploration also permits another initial reading to show why the final number alone is not generally the delivered volume. A typical 0.1 mL scale is estimated to 0.01 mL, reading the bottom of the concave meniscus at eye level.

<h2 id="try">Follow Experimental Activity 2 on pages 91–93</h2>

1. **Check leaks, condition and clear bubbles.** Check the stopcock first. Rinse clean burettes two or three times with their intended solutions, using the textbook's 3–5 mL portions, tilting and rolling to wet the walls, then discarding through the tip. Fill with reagent, clear tip bubbles and record the starting level. The visual uses three 5 mL rinses, with separate wall film and waste paths.
2. **Deliver the sample.** The acid burette contains 0.1000 mol/L HCl. Use the conditioned base burette to deliver 20.00 mL unknown NaOH, then add two drops of phenolphthalein. The practical direction is **acid into base, pink to colorless**, opposite to the introductory textbook curve.
3. **Approach the endpoint dropwise.** Place white paper below the flask and swirl while adding acid. Near the endpoint, use partial drops: form a small drop at the tip, touch it to the inside flask wall and wash it into the bulk with distilled water. The visual accounts separately for hanging, wall-bound and bulk-delivered acid. Acid still on the wall cannot change bulk pH prematurely.
4. **Observe, read and repeat.** Record the final reading when the pink just disappears and does not return within half a minute. Repeat twice. The demonstration compresses the 30-second observation clock. Your three-run table starts blank, calculates each result from your readings and shows a mean only when all three rows are valid. You must still assess anomalies and repeat flawed experiments.
5. **Plot and compare conditions.** The activity's final question requests a NaOH-into-HCl curve, reversing the practical direction. Exploration switches direction, magnifies 19.8–20.2 mL, and compares residual water, extra analyte and uncleared bubbles.

The traditional glass-stopcock acid burette should not hold alkaline solutions; the textbook also notes that PTFE-stopcock burettes can serve both. The base burette visual distinguishes the rubber sleeve and glass-ball valve.

<h2 id="deeper">Endpoint, equivalence and errors</h2>

Equivalence is defined by reaction stoichiometry; an indicator endpoint is defined by an observable color change. They are not exactly identical. The textbook gives **pH 8.2–10** for phenolphthalein and **pH 3.1–4.4** for methyl orange. Both can lie within the steep region of this strong acid–base curve without changing exactly at pH 7. The visual marks transition ranges separately from exact equivalence.

Water location matters. Residual water in the burette dilutes the standard solution, requiring a greater volume; using its original concentration then biases the inferred result high. Distilled water in the flask increases total volume without adding analyte, leaving the ideal equivalence requirement unchanged. Rinsing the flask with analyte can introduce an unmeasured extra amount. If liquid fills an initial tip bubble during titration, the reading difference exceeds the volume delivered into the flask.

The three displayed example results come from **a separate worked problem on textbook page 69**: 0.1032 mol/L HCl titrates 25.00 mL NaOH, consuming 27.84, 27.83 and 27.85 mL, giving approximately 0.1149 mol/L on average. These conditions differ from the activity's 0.1000 mol/L standard acid and 20.00 mL sample. Do not copy them into your experimental record. Similar repeated results may support averaging; averaging does not remove systematic errors.

<summary>Teaching model and operational boundaries</summary>

The ideal 25 °C strong acid–base calculation solves charge balance and water dissociation, avoiding a logarithm of zero at equivalence. Color interpolates the textbook ranges rather than modeling every indicator species or slow fading in strongly alkaline solution. Liquids are assumed to mix immediately; stirring, heating and carbon dioxide uptake are not simulated.

The half-drop is 0.025 mL and the wash is 2 mL water. These illustrative parameters do not calibrate real drops, which depend on tip and technique. Acid conservation includes the hanging drop, wall residue and bulk solution. The main apparatus compresses the full burette scale, while the reading view magnifies a separate local section; screen pixels do not establish physical dimensions.

Error examples change one condition at a time: a final 50 mL standard solution containing 1 mL water gives 0.098 mol/L actual acid; the flask can contain 5 mL extra water, 1 mL extra 0.1 mol/L analyte, or a 0.20 mL initial tip bubble. The conditioning model separately assumes a 0.5 mL retained film between rinses and calculates residual water by mixing and drainage.
