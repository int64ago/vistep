<h2 id="understand">Understand the principle</h2>

This film follows the supramolecular discussion on pages 98–100 of PEP Selective Compulsory 2, Chapter 3, Section 4. It compares calixarene-mediated fullerene separation with crown-ether recognition of cations. This is a structure and process demonstration, not a student laboratory procedure.

C₆₀ and C₇₀ contain 60 and 70 carbon atoms, connected by 90 and 105 edges respectively, with 3 neighbors per carbon. Coordinates come from [Beloit College’s fullerene models credited to H. Kroto](https://chem.beloit.edu/edetc/pmks/pages/bucky.html). The cages undergo rigid transformations; C₇₀ is not an arbitrarily stretched C₆₀. Individual bond orders are not distinguished; see the [IUPAC fullerene nomenclature resource](https://publications.iupac.org/pac/74/4/0629/index.html). A gold ring follows the same atom in each cage.

The host and guest retain separate covalent skeletons. Page 99 illustrates separation in toluene and release using chloroform. The original evidence supplies an essential qualification: [Atwood and colleagues’ 1994 paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/368229a0) reports complexes with **both C₆₀ and C₇₀**, using repeated recrystallization for high-purity separation. The schematic must not imply absolute exclusion of C₇₀ or complete purification after one filtration and release. Solubility claims also depend on concentration, temperature and the particular host derivative.

<h2 id="try">Try it yourself</h2>

The binding-constant ratio changes a competitive equilibrium. Default normalized totals are host H=0.7, C₆₀ A=0.6 and C₇₀ B=0.4, with illustrative constants K₆₀=30 and K₇₀=3. These are **teaching parameters, not fitted experimental constants or measured purity**. Equal constants preserve the original 60:40 guest proportion in the bound pool.

For free host h, the two 1:1 complex amounts are b₆₀=A K₆₀h/(1+K₆₀h) and b₇₀=B K₇₀h/(1+K₇₀h). We solve h+b₆₀+b₇₀=H. Assigning bound material to the retained phase and free guests to filtrate is an additional phase-separation assumption, not a complete precipitation or solvent thermodynamics model. Release transfers both bound inventories to released pools while recovering their hosts. Every guest species and the host remain conserved. Enlarged markers indicate destinations, not molecular scale or filter-pore size.

The name 18-crown-6 denotes 18 ring atoms, including 6 oxygens; each adjacent oxygen pair is separated by two CH₂ groups. Try different crowns with Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Rb⁺ and Cs⁺. Oversized ions move away from the idealized ring plane. Geometry is not converted into an affinity or binding-probability prediction. Background appears in the [1987 Nobel chemistry overview](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1987/speedread/) and [Pedersen’s Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/pedersen-lecture.pdf).

<h2 id="deeper">Go deeper</h2>

Page 100 lists cavity ranges of 120–150, 170–220, 260–320 and 340–430 pm for 12-crown-4, 15-crown-5, 18-crown-6 and 21-crown-7. Approximate diameters for Li⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Rb⁺ and Cs⁺ are 152, 204, 276, 304 and 334 pm. Ionic radii depend on coordination environment. The four pairings compare dimensions; they are neither exclusive binding rules nor claims of optimal selectivity.

The idealized cup-shaped calix[8]arene skeleton omits hydrogen and para substituents, retaining 8 phenolic rings, methylene bridges and 8 oxygens. Ring edges of 1.40 Å and bridge bonds of 1.51 Å are drawing geometry, **not an experimentally determined conformation**. Closed crown skeletons use C—O distances of 1.43 Å and C—C distances of 1.54 Å. Oxygen donors occupy an idealized plane, using the midpoint of each textbook cavity range plus an effective oxygen contact envelope of 1.4 Å. Larger ions shift axially to avoid geometric overlap; this is not an optimized molecular structure. Flexibility, solvation, counterions and coordination affect actual selectivity. Progress and paths describe teaching states, not measured kinetics, molecular trajectories or yields.
