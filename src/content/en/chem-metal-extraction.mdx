## Why does the same reductant give different outcomes?

Carbon monoxide participates in extracting iron in a blast furnace, while industrial aluminium production uses molten-salt electrolysis. Both routes let a metal gain electrons. The question is: **why can we not simply apply the same CO route to alumina?**

Follow oxygen first. The net reaction `Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂` transfers it from the oxide to CO. Iron changes from oxidation state +III to zero while carbon changes from +II to +IV. The same atoms remain accounted for, and the six electrons accepted by iron have a source. This is a net equation; a blast furnace passes through intermediate iron oxides.

Now compare the same amount of CO with two oxides. NIST-JANAF data give a net free-energy change of about **−25 kJ for iron oxide but +775 kJ for alumina at 1000 K and a CO₂/CO partial-pressure ratio of 1**, per mole of oxide. The common-scale bars add the positive value for reversing oxide formation to the negative value for oxidizing CO. Alumina's positive term is much larger; the same CO term cannot offset it.

Free energy here is neither the temperature rise nor plant electricity use. A negative value favours the forward net reaction under the stated conditions; a positive value favours its reverse. **Favourable does not mean fast, and does not establish complete reduction.**

</section>

<section id="try">

## Can the prediction change without changing the metal?

Predict first, then increase CO₂/CO from 1 to 10 in the comparison. The temperature and oxide remain unchanged, yet the iron reaction changes from roughly −25 to +32 kJ. Product accumulation changes the net tendency. Reactivity order is a useful summary, but cannot answer every question about temperature and gas composition.

Temperature offers three actual tabulated points: 800, 1000 and 1200 K. Changing it tests the comparison rather than inventing a rate law. Switch to aluminium reducing iron oxide: at 1000 K, `Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃` has a net free-energy change near −800 kJ; its reverse is near +800 kJ. The same data support oxygen transfer toward aluminium.

In the material account, progress still tracks atoms and electrons. Removing power or the molten condition stops the selected ideal electrolytic route. The thermal-condition switch stops the selected thermal route. These are necessary-condition gates, not predictions of temperature thresholds, rates or electrode overpotentials.

Aqueous sodium chloride cannot replace the melt to obtain sodium by this route. The diagrams explain phases and interfaces, not a buildable plant. They provide no mercury or thermite operating procedure.

<section id="deeper">

<details>
<summary>Calculation and boundaries of the comparison</summary>

The NIST-JANAF standard formation Gibbs energies at 1000 K, in kJ/mol, are Fe₂O₃(cr) −561.866, α-Al₂O₃(cr) −1361.437, CO(g) −200.275 and CO₂(g) −395.886. Elements use their stable reference phases at that temperature: solid iron and liquid aluminium at 1000 K. Standard pressure is 1 bar. Pure condensed phases have unit activity, and gases are treated as ideal.

For iron, `ΔG° = 3(−395.886) − [−561.866 + 3(−200.275)] = −24.967 kJ`. With partial-pressure ratio `r = p(CO₂)/p(CO)`, the net reaction quotient is `Q = r³`, so `ΔG = ΔG° + 3RT ln(r)`. The iron net equation changes sign at r about 2.72 at 1000 K. This ratio describes the gases, not amounts of oxide.

Adding these terms is a state-function calculation, not a microscopic sequence that first dismantles oxide and then burns CO. Negative net ΔG does not prove that each intermediate oxide-reduction step is favourable. A real blast furnace requires separate consideration of intermediate phases, local gas composition, heat transfer and kinetics. Bars use a common absolute-value scale; the vertical gap for aluminium reduction is a net free-energy difference, not an activation-energy profile.

Electrolysis supplies external electrical work. This CO comparison does not calculate cell voltage. Industrial aluminium extraction also involves a cryolite melt, overpotentials, heat losses and carbon anodes. The comparison rules out simply reusing the CO net reduction under the stated conditions; it does not rule out every alternative process at every temperature.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Conservation, oxidation states and industrial limits</summary>

Thermal decomposition: `2HgO → 2Hg + O₂`. Two Hg(+II) centres accept four electrons while two O(−II) atoms release four.

Carbon-monoxide reduction: `Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂`. Two Fe(+III) centres accept six electrons as three C(+II) atoms become C(+IV). This net equation omits intermediate iron oxides, coke reactions and slag.

Aluminium reduction: `Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Fe + Al₂O₃`, with six electrons on each side of the transfer account. Oxide circle clusters are formula units, not a claim that the solid consists of separate little molecules. Animated paths track identities rather than measured reaction trajectories.

Electrolysis: `2NaCl(l) → 2Na(l) + Cl₂(g)` and `MgCl₂(l) → Mg(l) + Cl₂(g)` each transfer two electrons, accepted by two sodium ions or one magnesium ion. Ions move within the melt; electrodes and the external circuit exchange electrons. Displayed ion and bubble counts are schematic, not concentrations.

For aluminium production with carbon anodes, a common net equation is `2Al₂O₃ + 3C → 4Al + 3CO₂`, transferring twelve electrons. Cryolite belongs to the electrolyte system and is not a net reactant here. Complex aluminium-fluoride speciation, carbon monoxide and other operating effects are omitted. Oxygen alone is not presented as the industrial anode product.

</details>
