<h2 id="understand">Two reference cases belong to one investigation</h2>

The textbook’s investigation of decomposition by soil microbes offers litter and starch as two reference cases. Both require a question, controlled variables, sustained records and interpretation. The animation shows the design and possible model behavior, not completed experimental results.

The litter comparison uses natural and treated soil. The textbook suggests enclosing soil and treating it at 60°C for 1 h to reduce microbial effects while minimizing physicochemical changes. **This is not a guarantee of complete sterilization.** Residual activity, extracellular enzymes and changed soil properties can complicate interpretation. Match leaf source and amount, approximately 5 cm soil depth, moisture, temperature, time and replication. Organic-matter properties and aeration also matter: wetter is not invariably faster. [USDA soil-respiration guidance](https://www.nrcs.usda.gov/state-offices/illinois/soil-tech-note-17a-soil-respiration)

Leaf holes illustrate structural damage; their geometry does not measure true mass. Practice masses come from an explicit decay model, while personal measurements begin blank. Mass comparisons require consistent drying and removal of adhering soil, with replication to help distinguish decomposition from handling effects.

<h2 id="try">From soil extract to separate chemical tests</h2>

Place soil in a beaker lined with thick gauze, add distilled water and mix, then remove the gauze and coarse soil and let the extract settle. Gauze is not a sterilizing membrane. Microbes, soluble compounds, extracellular enzymes and pigments may remain.

Give A and B equal starch paste. Add 30 mL of soil extract to A and 30 mL of distilled water to B, then incubate at about 20°C for 7 days. For explicit volume accounting, this reconstruction chooses 10 mL of paste per group, giving 40 mL total. That paste volume is a modeling choice, not a textbook requirement. Take 20 mL from each incubation and split it into two 10 mL aliquots, leaving 20 mL. Pipette drawing, movement and delivery remain consistent with the volume ledger.

Add iodine to A1 and B1 to inspect remaining starch. Add freshly prepared Fehling reagent to A2 and B2 and heat to inspect brick-red precipitate from reducing substances. The reagents test **separate aliquots**. Iodine response depends on starch structure, breakdown and background color; Fehling chemistry is not uniquely specific to glucose. [University of Maryland: hydrolysis assays and background correction](https://user.eng.umd.edu/~nsw/ench485/lab5.htm)

<h2 id="deeper">An endpoint test is not a recording of the entire process</h2>

Extracellular enzymes produced by some microbes hydrolyze starch into smaller usable products. Reduced iodine response together with increased reducing activity can support breakdown, without independently identifying a species or enzyme. [Austin Community College starch-hydrolysis laboratory](https://sites.google.com/austincc.edu/microbugz/lab-exercises/differential-tests/starch-hydrolysis)

The model tracks carbon equivalents through starch, reducing products and other carbon pools, with a total of one. **It does not equate dry starch mass with hydrated glucose mass.** Faster product consumption can leave both low starch and low reducing products at the endpoint. A negative Fehling result therefore does not establish that hydrolysis never occurred, and a positive day-seven test is not a guaranteed outcome.

Extracts can already contain reducing substances and colored compounds. Existing extracellular enzymes may remain active even when viable-cell activity is reduced. Initial tests, extract blanks without starch, treatment controls and replication strengthen interpretation. Preserve unexpected records and investigate timing, samples, reagents and conditions rather than changing observations to match the animation.
