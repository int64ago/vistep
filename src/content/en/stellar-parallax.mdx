<h2 id="understand">Move the observer and measure relative angles</h2>

Observing from different orbital positions slightly changes a nearby star's direction against distant references. The plate explicitly enlarges a local angular field with arcsecond coordinates. Background crosses are fixed reference symbols rather than a star catalog. The orbital inset has a separate length scale and is not a shared physical-distance map.

A circular observing orbit has radius B, so opposing positions six months apart are separated by 2B. The favorable opposite-view angular displacement is 2p; parallax p is its half-amplitude. For B = 1 AU, `d(pc)≈1/p(arcsec)`. With another baseline use `d≈B/p` instead.

<h2 id="try">Distance, baseline and projected shape</h2>

Changing distance from 1 pc to 2 pc halves parallax from about 1″ to 0.5″. Keeping distance fixed but halving baseline also halves parallax. A smaller displacement can mean greater distance or a shorter baseline, so baseline must be known before inference.

Ecliptic latitude β controls the annual projection: near a line at the ecliptic, near a circle at the pole, and generally an ellipse between. A circular observer orbit and actual three-dimensional sight-vector projections determine this shape. Horizontal and vertical angular scales are equal.

<h2 id="deeper">Separate proper motion from annual parallax</h2>

<details>
<summary>Periodicity, units and no baseline</summary>

Adding transverse proper motion of 0.35″ per year places the annual pattern on a linear drift. Equal seasons one year apart no longer coincide. Proper motion does not change the model's parallax parameter. Real astrometry fits position, parallax, proper motion, uncertainties and possibly other effects; this film decomposes known ideal quantities rather than solving a real catalog.

The IAU defines 1 pc as exactly `648000/π AU`. The model uses that conversion and the tangent relation for geometric angles and inverse distance. The familiar `B/p` formula is the small-angle approximation. A zero baseline produces no parallax distance information, so the readout stays undefined instead of reporting a finite distance or claiming an infinitely distant star.

Explore changes distance, observing-orbit radius, ecliptic latitude, transverse proper motion and epoch. Assumptions exclude aberration, refraction, noise, radial perspective effects and companions; background references are fixed. A periodic wobble alone does not establish a planet, and no real stellar distance is inferred here.

</details>
