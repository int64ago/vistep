## A reflection can reverse the field

For an air–film–air layer, the front reflection encounters a higher index and reverses the electric field. The back reflection encounters a lower index and does not. Even when the extra propagation path approaches zero, this half-cycle difference remains. A quarter-wave optical thickness adds another half cycle and produces reinforcement.

The film follows the two contributions with monochromatic light, then replaces the air backing with higher-index glass at the same thickness. Both reflections now reverse, swapping the bright and dark conditions. A thickness wedge finally reveals wavelength-selective reflection.

<a id="try"></a>

## Change one condition at a time

Find two neighbouring reflectance maxima by varying thickness. Their spacing is the vacuum wavelength divided by twice the film index. Hold thickness fixed and change wavelength. Then change the backing to see why propagation alone cannot determine the result.

## Phase does not specify amplitude

<div className="formula">
  Round-trip optical path = 2nd
  <br />
  Propagation phase = 4πnd / λ
</div>

The calculation assumes normal incidence, no absorption, and a uniform nondispersive film. The electric-field reflection coefficient is the index difference divided by the index sum. The reflectance readout sums all internal reflections with the Fresnel coefficients. The waveforms use those same complex amplitudes: the front reflection, every return from inside the layer, and their actual sum. Opposite phases therefore do not guarantee zero reflection for arbitrary materials.

The ray paths are separated laterally for readability; real normal-incidence paths overlap. Thickness is enlarged. The swatch uses three sampled wavelengths to illustrate spectral selection, not to predict human colour perception, dispersion, illumination spectra or curved soap-film optics.
