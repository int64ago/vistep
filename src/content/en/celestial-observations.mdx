<h2 id="understand">A point that refuses to meet the prediction</h2>

Matching one date is easy. Can the same orbit explain other dates and predict records that have not yet been used? Tycho’s observations constrained Kepler in this way. Here, a **frozen synthetic table** makes the test visible. Gold records do not move when the candidate changes. This is not Tycho’s original table or a reconstruction of the historical eight arcminutes.

A circle and a specified ellipse face the same first five records and the same search over starting phase. The circle leaves a pattern of errors. The ellipse comes closer, but two records remain hidden until its parameters are locked. Agreement with those unused records adds evidence. Comparing these two candidates does not uniquely derive Kepler’s laws from seven points.

Uranus posed another question: was an extra body missing from the gravity model? A distant planet pulls both Uranus and the Sun. Their **difference in acceleration** changes the heliocentric relative motion. The synthetic example fixes other initial conditions, compares twelve mass/phase candidates, and tests three unused records. Explaining a discrepancy still requires an independent check in the sky.

<h2 id="try">Reserve evidence before testing a prediction</h2>

In the Tycho view, choose a circle or ellipse and change its starting phase. The gold direction records stay fixed. “Fit first 5 only” restores the defined comparison. Lock the prediction, decide where the last two records should lie, then reveal them. Changing a candidate hides the reserved records again; a result already consulted should not be treated as a fresh prediction.

In the Neptune view, set the extra mass to zero. The model residual approaches zero while the fixed records remain. Compare other candidates on the same scale. “Gravity cause” separates the pulls on Uranus and the Sun from their relative acceleration; arrow lengths use a common acceleration scale.

“Three historical nights” switches to actual coordinates from 1846. Inspect two nights, lock a classroom linear extrapolation, then reveal the third. This is a **retrospective exercise using original data**, not Le Verrier’s inverse calculation. A new point must also be checked against the chart and on subsequent nights.

Both candidates search only their starting phase, from −0.08 to 0.32 rad in 0.0005 rad steps, minimizing the first five angular errors. The circle’s best RMS error is about 252.72′; the specified ellipse gives about 0.23′. Their two reserved-record errors are about 387.73′ and 0.29′ respectively. These belong to this synthetic example, **not the historical 8′**. Plot axes stay fixed across candidates.

The Uranus example uses AU and years, with solar gravitational parameter 4π² AU³/yr². Uranus starts at 19.2 AU, the prescribed circular perturber at 30.1 AU, and its example mass is 5.15×10⁻⁵ solar masses. RK4 advances Uranus. Heliocentric perturbation includes the indirect term:

<div className="formula">δa = GMₙ[(rₙ − rᵤ)/|rₙ − rᵤ|³ − rₙ/|rₙ|³]</div>

Fixed direction differences are recorded every ten model years from year 10 through year 80, with specified offsets no larger than 0.2″. Five are used for comparison and three are reserved. The candidate grid contains mass scales 0, 0.5, 1 and 1.5, and initial phases −0.4, 0.6 and 1.6 rad. Other initial conditions remain fixed. This is a **conditional comparison within a restricted grid**, not a complete inverse problem or a unique discovery. The residual is heliocentric, not an Earth-observed ecliptic longitude.

<summary>The original 1846 records and an independent third night</summary>

Encke’s report of 26 September 1846 lists these three positions. The original mean Berlin time is retained. Right ascension is expressed here in angular degrees; the values are not silently converted to UTC or J2000.

| Date in 1846 | Mean Berlin time | Right ascension | Declination  |
| ------------ | ---------------- | --------------- | ------------ |
| 23 September | 12:00:14.6       | 328°19′16.0″    | −13°24′8.2″  |
| 24 September | 08:54:40.9       | 328°18′14.3″    | −13°24′29.7″ |
| 25 September | 09:44:45.0       | 328°16′59.8″    | −13°24′55.4″ |

Between the first two nights, RA changes by −61.7″ and declination by −21.5″. The local sky chart multiplies RA differences by the cosine of mean declination and uses equal angular scales horizontally and vertically. The third-night classroom prediction uses only the first two positions and their time interval. Once revealed, the third position differs by about −1.21″ in RA and −0.16″ in declination. This is retrospective short-term linear extrapolation; three nights do not determine a complete orbit.

The report separately compares **geocentric ecliptic longitudes** at the same epoch, 23.5 September 1846: calculated 324°58′ and observed 325°52.75′, a difference of 54.75′. This specific reported comparison must not be mixed with the heliocentric orbital elements for 1 January 1847, discovery-night RA, or error figures from other accounts. The film clears the previous axes when changing coordinate systems.

<details>
<summary>The retained telescope, moon and comet models</summary>

The Galilean telescope is an ideal paraxial system with a positive objective, negative eyepiece and magnification five. Jupiter’s moons use coplanar circular orbits viewed edge-on, with periods 1.769, 3.551, 7.155 and 16.689 days. Initial phases are illustrative; light time, mutual occultations and oblateness are omitted. This is not Galileo’s original observation sequence.

Five teaching snapshots remain fixed at elapsed days 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4. Advancing time reveals new rows without changing earlier positions; the live view and readout share one clock. Rewinding hides later observations without changing their dates.

Halley uses e=0.967, T=75.3 years and a=T^(2/3) AU. Kepler’s equation determines position rather than uniform motion along the ellipse. The tail only indicates the antisolar direction. The historical dates 1531, 1607, 1682 and 1758 are not fitted with the fixed 75.3-year period.
