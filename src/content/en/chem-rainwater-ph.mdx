<section id="understand">

The textbook activity directly collects rain, retains distilled or tap water as a reference, measures with pH paper or a meter, and suggests repeated records when feasible. Water that first crosses a roof, leaves or the ground also reflects contact and wash-off effects. Measure the reference rather than assigning it pH 7 in advance.

The preceding page uses 5.6 as an approximate normal-rain reference and describes rain below 5.6 as acid rain. Atmospheric CO₂ can make water mildly acidic; real precipitation also contains other natural and human-derived components. This film explicitly calls 5.6 the textbook reference. A value below 7 alone does not establish pollution-caused acid rain.

</section>

<section id="deeper">
<details>
<summary>Calibration, resolution and measurement limits</summary>

The teaching electrode relation is E=E₀−gSpH, with S=RTln10/F, approximately 59.16 mV per pH unit at 25℃. The fixture uses offset E₀ = 12 mV and slope factor g=0.96. Standards at pH 4 and pH 7 recover those two parameters exactly for this affine model. Real electrodes also face junction potentials, temperature effects, response time and contamination.

Paper is represented by integer pH steps with an ideal half-step interval on either side. Actual paper has its own range, step size and colour chart; animation colours are not a manufacturer's chart. The meter displays two decimals while the separate ±0.15 teaching interval prevents display resolution being mistaken for accuracy.

Rain and other low-conductivity waters pose specific difficulties. The [USGS research abstract](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/ph-measurement-low-conductivity-waters) discusses sources of error and improved methods. The depicted calibration explains a principle rather than certifying accuracy or providing a complete operating protocol.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Mean pH and mean hydrogen ions answer different questions</summary>

Strictly, pH is the negative common logarithm of hydrogen-ion activity. In a dilute-solution teaching approximation, 10 to the negative pH is proportional to hydrogen-ion concentration. Multiply this quantity by each water amount, sum, divide by total water, and then take the negative logarithm. This differs from averaging pH values directly.

Equal water amounts at pH 4 and pH 6 give approximately 4.30 after this conversion, while their arithmetic mean pH is 5. With only 10% of the water at pH 4, the weighted conversion is approximately 4.96. Zero-volume or invalid samples are excluded; an empty valid record returns no statistic.

These are statistical comparisons, not predictions of equilibrium after mixing arbitrary real water samples. Buffering, gas exchange and acid-base reactions can change actual mixtures. Nor is pH a measure of total titratable acidity. A pH reading alone cannot distinguish sulfuric, nitric or organic acids, or locate an emission source.

</details>
