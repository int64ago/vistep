<h2 id="understand">Read the evidence before interpreting acid strength</h2>

Equal analytical concentrations do not imply equal hydrogen-ion concentrations. Experiment 3-1 on page 58 of the textbook compares **0.1 mol/L** hydrochloric and acetic acids by pH, conductivity and reaction with equal amounts of magnesium. These observations measure different things. pH concerns hydrogen-ion activity; conductivity also depends on other ions and their mobility; gas production rate depends on the reaction conditions.

The ideal 25 °C model uses acetic Ka = 1.75 × 10⁻⁵, matching page 60. At 0.1 mol/L, calculated pH values are approximately 2.88 for acetic acid and 1 for hydrochloric acid. The combined glass electrode reads only after its sensitive end enters the liquid. The conductivity comparison keeps electrode geometry and alternating voltage the same. These are teaching calculations, not instrument measurements.

A weak acid is only partly ionized in water. This does **not** imply that a sample contains less total acid. Dilution may increase the ionized fraction while reducing hydrogen-ion concentration. Once analytical concentrations differ, acid strength alone cannot rank conductivity.

<h2 id="try">Follow the three textbook learning positions</h2>

1. **Experiment 3-1: several observations at equal concentration.** Compare pH, AC electrical response and gas from equal magnesium samples. Because this experiment does not specify exact volumes or magnesium masses, the visual adds a design of 2 mL acid and 5 mg Mg per sample. Explore concentrations independently or lift the probes. Your observation field starts blank.
2. **Experiment 3-2, page 61: acetic acid and carbonate.** Add 1 mol/L sodium carbonate dropwise to 2 mL of 1 mol/L acetic acid. With sufficient acid, the overall reaction is 2 CH₃COOH + CO₃²⁻ → 2 CH₃COO⁻ + CO₂ + H₂O. Acetic Ka exceeds the textbook first dissociation constant for carbonic acid, and gas escape helps drive the process. Bubbles alone do not measure a numerical Ka.
3. **Pressure comparison in the page 61 body text.** Put 0.05 g Mg in each sealed flask and inject 2 mL of 2 mol/L hydrochloric or acetic acid. Liquid visibly travels through the needle into the flask, while the transducer connects to the gas space. Injection also compresses the original air. The subsequent comparison therefore uses hydrogen pressure increments relative to a common post-addition baseline.

The pressure curves reconstruct the textbook's qualitative shapes: hydrochloric acid rises early and levels off; acetic acid initially accelerates and then slows, approaching the same gas yield. The textbook provides no pointwise raw dataset, headspace volume or temperature record. This explicitly labelled parametric reconstruction is not a fitted kinetic law.

<h2 id="deeper">Fewer initial hydrogen ions can still supply the same total yield</h2>

Each pressure experiment contains 2 mL × 2 mol/L = **4 mmol acid**. The stoichiometry Mg + 2 H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂ permits at most **2 mmol hydrogen**. The 0.05 g magnesium sample contains about 2.057 mmol, leaving approximately **1.39 mg Mg** at completion. The common endpoint does not mean that all the magnesium disappears.

Acetic acid initially has far fewer free hydrogen ions than its total acid content. As hydrogen ions are consumed, further ionization supplies more. Compare total reactive acid and magnesium to determine yield, rather than counting only hydrogen ions present before reaction. Continued ionization explains the source of reaction capacity; **it does not by itself derive the initial acceleration of an S-shaped curve**. Temperature changes, surface conditions and other influences need separate measurements.

<details>
<summary>Model, readings and boundaries</summary>

- pH uses concentration as an activity approximation and solves weak-acid equilibrium, water dissociation and charge balance. Especially at high concentration, the ideal result is not a precise pH prediction. HAc abbreviates CH₃COOH.
- Conductivity uses limiting ionic conductivities, 0.1 V AC and a cell constant of 1 cm⁻¹. It omits real concentration corrections at 0.1 mol/L and does not replace electrode calibration.
- Both magnesium time processes are illustrative. Pressure uses ideal hydrogen at 25 °C and defaults to a 100 mL post-injection gas space: 2 mmol gives about 49.58 kPa. Gas-space volume changes pressure at equal yield; leakage changes the captured gas amount. Dissolved hydrogen, vapor changes and actual heating are omitted.
- Carbonate drops are 0.05 mL each. The model tracks delivered volume and acid equivalents using an open-system overall reaction with excess acid. Once acid capacity is exhausted, that reaction cannot keep producing gas. Bicarbonate and dissolved carbon dioxide equilibria are not fully resolved.
- The blank observation field contains your conditions and measurements; demonstration curves are never entered as your results.
