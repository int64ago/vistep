<section id="understand">
## From one electron to the whole reaction

One sodium–chlorine atom pair contains eight valence electrons. Marking one makes sodium's loss and chlorine's gain two views of the same allocation. A chlorine molecule contains two chlorine atoms, so two sodium atoms must each provide one electron. The objects, atom counts and electron account agree with 2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl. The resulting formula units belong to an extended ionic lattice, not isolated NaCl molecules.

A changed oxidation state need not mean complete physical transfer. HCl retains a shared pair whose distribution favours chlorine. Formally assigning that pair to chlorine gives H an oxidation state of +1 and Cl −1. Changing the accounting overlay does not turn the neutral molecule into separated ions.

Return to iron and copper sulfate on the same textbook page. Fe becoming Fe²⁺ accounts for two lost electron units; Cu²⁺ becoming Cu needs two. One iron therefore corresponds to one copper ion, not two. The electron account predicts both the ratio and what remains in excess.

</section>

<section id="try">
## Predict the copper amount before revealing it

In “Predict copper,” choose one Fe unit and two Cu²⁺ units. Hold the explanation at its start, predict the result, then advance: one Cu²⁺ becomes Cu while the other remains. Add a second Fe to supply enough electron units for the second reduction. Set either reactant to zero to check that no reaction is invented.

The same ratio scales to amount of substance: under the textbook net reaction, 1 mmol Fe and 2 mmol Cu²⁺ yield 1 mmol Cu and leave 1 mmol Cu²⁺. This is not a quantitative prediction of reaction speed or solution colour.

In “Covalent sharing,” toggle the formal allocation layer. The shared pair remains visible. Electron counts, the polarity envelope and oxidation states answer different questions and must not be treated as interchangeable measurements.

</section>

<details>
<summary>Physical charge, formal allocation and model limits</summary>

IUPAC defines oxidation states through an ionic approximation of heteronuclear bonds; homonuclear bonds are divided equally. This is formal electron allocation, not a measurement of physical atomic partial charges. An electron's atom of origin does not define its final oxidation-state assignment. The HCl envelope only illustrates polarity; no wavefunction or numerical partial charge is calculated.

The Na–Cl diagrams track the initial valence electrons and omit inner shells. The local pair preserves eight tracked units and the whole reaction sixteen. A departing marker enters the explicit transfer account before arriving at the recipient. The total count and charge stay consistent. The connector and moving marker are not observable classical electron paths.

Iron–copper uses the near-complete net account Fe + Cu²⁺ → Fe²⁺ + Cu. Two electron units match one copper ion without introducing mandatory Fe⁺ or Cu⁺ intermediates. The moving packet is an allocation illustration, not a pool of free electrons accumulating in water. Sulfate stays unchanged and is included in charge accounting. Rates, activities, complexation and hydrolysis are not solved. The slider controls explanation progress, not physical time.
