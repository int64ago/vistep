<h2 id="understand">Does a feather fall slowly simply because it is light?</h2>

A Newton tube turns that intuition into a controlled comparison. Keep the same iron piece and feather, the same release height, the same release instant, and zero initial velocity. Change only the air inside the tube.

With air present, the iron arrives first and the feather follows. After evacuation, they fall almost together. Air resistance affects the motion, so the difference observed in air cannot be attributed to mass alone.

**Here we study free fall from rest, with gravity as the only force.** At the same location, with air resistance neglected, the feather and iron have the same gravitational acceleration. Released together from the same height and from rest, they therefore arrive together.

This lesson accompanies Chapter 2, Section 4, “Free Fall,” in PEP High School Physics, Required Book 1, printed page 48. The textbook first contrasts a sheet of paper with a paper ball, then controls the air using a Newton tube, before studying free-fall patterns with timing tape. This film focuses on the Newton-tube comparison within that sequence.

<h2 id="try">Watch the comparison, then change the air</h2>

In exploration mode, both trials share one physical time. The first always contains air. The second can use an ideal vacuum, ordinary air, or an adjustable relative air density.

- Pause the physical time at 0.40 s and compare both objects across the two trials.
- Hold that time fixed and gradually remove the air from the second trial. The feather travels farther. The iron is affected too, though much less with these parameters.
- Set the air to zero, then move the time slider. The two objects remain level, and the distance covered in each equal time interval increases.
- Put air in both trials. Their positions coincide: a useful check that the comparison really holds the other conditions fixed.

The larger colored dots mark past positions every 0.12 s. They are not air molecules. Small pale dots inside the tube merely indicate the presence of air.

<h2 id="deeper">Separate the observation from its model</h2>

<details>
<summary>The shared free-fall rule</summary>

For release from rest, with downward taken as positive, ideal free fall satisfies:

<div className="formula">v = gt　　s = ½gt²　　timpact = √(2h/g)</div>

The model uses g = 9.8 m/s² and a common falling distance h = 1.4 m. Both ideal-vacuum objects arrive after about 0.535 s. That is a calculation from chosen parameters, not a measurement extracted from the textbook illustration or a single Newton-tube demonstration.

Equal acceleration alone does not guarantee equal arrival times. Different release heights, starting velocities, or release instants change the result. This comparison fixes all three to isolate the effect of air.

</details>

<details>
<summary>The air model and its limits</summary>

Motion in air uses a one-dimensional quadratic-drag approximation with fixed orientation. The force relation can be revisited after learning Newton's second law:

<div className="formula">D = ½ρCdAv²　　a = g − D/m</div>

Here ρ is air density, Cd a drag coefficient, and A frontal area. The iron uses m = 20 g, A = 0.0012 m², Cd = 1.1; the feather uses m = 0.2 g, A = 0.0032 m², Cd = 1.2. Ordinary-air density is set to 1.225 kg/m³. These are illustrative teaching parameters, not measurements of a particular apparatus. The drawing is not an engineering scale plan.

A real feather rotates, flutters, and changes its frontal area. Tube walls, internal flow, buoyancy, and residual air can also matter. Constant drag coefficients explain the qualitative comparison; they do not predict every pressure or feather shape accurately. At zero density the model explicitly uses drag-free motion. Evacuation is shown by fading the density indicator, without modeling a pump or its actual pumping rate.

Both drawings use the same falling-distance and contact reference. They stop at the common floor; collisions and bouncing are omitted. Slow motion changes the viewing speed only. The physical-time readout always uses seconds from the model.

</details>
