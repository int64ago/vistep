<h2 id="understand">Record positions before identifying the pattern</h2>

The horizontal strobe records a position every 0.05 s. The vertical video records every 0.04 s. One ball moves sideways and the other vertically; both graphs obtain time from the sampling clock. The joining curve describes displacement against time, rather than a curved spatial trajectory.

The vertical ball is lifted and released from above equilibrium. Its origin is the stationary centre after the load is attached, not the end of the unstretched spring. In the pencil experiment, uniform paper motion converts time into distance: at 5 cm/s, 5 cm represents 1 s.

<h2 id="try">Give each comparison one question</h2>

Time ten complete oscillations, then halve the amplitude. The ideal linear spring retains its period. When identical oscillators are released one quarter-period apart, the second remains supported until its release and then maintains the same frequency with a fixed phase delay.

For the research path, retain one spring while changing load mass. Then retain mass while selecting different lengths of the same uniform spring. The period appears only after complete timing. Recorded rows are model results; physical experiments require independent mass, length and clock calibration.

<h2 id="deeper">Vertical equilibrium and period</h2>

A light Hookean spring extends statically by mg/k. Measuring x from loaded equilibrium cancels the static spring force against weight, leaving −kx and T = 2π√(m/k). For a uniform spring, active coil count scales with length, so a shorter segment is stiffer.

The model neglects spring mass and damping. It does not claim amplitude independence for every real oscillator. A total timing error δt produces δT = δt/n. Counting more full oscillations reduces this contribution without eliminating frame-rate, parallax or nonlinear systematic errors.
