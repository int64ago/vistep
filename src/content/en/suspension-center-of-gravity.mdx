<h2 id="understand">The board turns. Why do its marks still identify one point?</h2>

Hold the lamina at an angle. Weight acts vertically, but its line misses the suspension pin. The perpendicular distance from the pin to that line is the lever arm: this offset gives gravity a turning effect. Release the board and it turns, possibly overshooting before swinging back. Drag removes mechanical energy. At stable rest, the centre of gravity is below the pin and the weight line passes through it.

That observation identifies **a line**, not a unique point. Transfer the plumb direction onto the material as an ink mark, then use another hole. The first ink mark turns with the board while the plumb line remains vertical. After settling again, add the second mark. Two records in different directions locate their intersection. Changing the support changes orientation, not the board's mass distribution.

The medians of a uniform triangular card supply an independent prediction. Draw the medians first, then test them with suspension records. Adding a mass on one side leaves the old medians and ink fixed on the material, but shifts the combined centre toward the mass. The film adds three quarters of the board mass, so the new centre moves three sevenths of the way from the old centre to the added mass. The hollow circle is a prediction; two new blue marks provide the model's subsequent test.

<h2 id="try">Keep the ink while changing the conditions</h2>

- Select a hole and move time to release. Follow the weight line, lever arm and turning direction. Zero speed at a turning point is not enough to record a line.
- Move to a settled state and select “Record settled ink”. Change holes, release, settle and record again. “Show ink intersection” reveals what two independent records imply.
- Keep the existing ink and add a mass. Make two fresh records and compare their intersection with the old one. Mass distribution changes; old ink must not move to fit the answer.
- Expand the model conditions and set damping to zero: the swinging does not decay away. Changing shape clears records from the previous board; changing load preserves earlier records.

<h2 id="deeper">Equilibrium becomes a testable constraint</h2>

<details>
<summary>Zero torque, zero speed and stable equilibrium</summary>

Gravity torque about the pin is weight times the signed lever arm. The drawing uses rightward and downward coordinates, with clockwise rotation positive. Away from the lowest position, gravity turns the board toward that position. The board may still be moving away, so torque and angular velocity need not have the same sign.

Zero angular velocity alone does not establish rest: it also occurs at each turning point. Zero gravity torque alone does not establish stability: a centre exactly above the pin moves away after a small perturbation. A pin at the centre gives no gravity-induced orientation preference. The record button requires both a small residual lever arm and a small angular velocity.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Independent records, mass weighting and computation</summary>

Material coordinates and screen coordinates are stored separately. Each ink record comes from inverse-transforming the world vertical at the observed settled pose. The recording function does not receive the centre position. The intersection is calculated from fixed record endpoints and compared with the centre calculated independently from area integrals and mass weighting. Coincident or nearly parallel records do not reliably identify a unique point.

The board mass is 0.10 kg, with 0.003 m per material coordinate unit. Polygon area integrals give its centroid and rotational inertia; the added disc is treated as a point mass at its centre. For board mass M and added mass m, the combined centre is C = (M C₀ + m W)/(M + m). An unchanged outline therefore need not imply an unchanged centre.

The nonlinear dynamics combine gravity torque with viscous drag: Iθ″ = τgravity − 2γIθ′, where γ has units of s⁻¹. Geometry, lever arms and readouts share one integrated state. A freely rotating fixed pin represents the support; the textbook's light-thread suspension gives the same settled conclusion. Support is not asserted to equal mg during motion. The drag model does not resolve airflow or dry pin friction. The presentation clock is directed for teaching and is not an experimental timing measurement.

These records are model results under explicit assumptions, not newly collected physical data. A real test still needs a light rigid sheet, freely rotating support and enough time for swinging to decay before marking.

</details>
