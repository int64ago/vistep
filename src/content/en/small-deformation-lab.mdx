<h2 id="understand">Make a hidden change observable</h2>

Pressing a tabletop can produce deformation too small to see directly. The textbook places two mirrors on the table so that one beam reflects from both and reaches a distant wall. Tiny rotations of the mirror supports change the outgoing direction and move the light spot.

First record a zero position, increase the force, move the wall while holding force constant, and release. A longer optical path converts the same angular change into a larger spot displacement. It amplifies the observable signal, without increasing the table's deformation.

<h2 id="try">Change force and observation distance separately</h2>

- Increase the applied force. Table deflection, mirror tilt and spot displacement all respond.
- Keep the force unchanged and move the wall farther away. The center of the tabletop sinks by the same amount, but the light spot moves farther from zero.
- Return to zero force. Within the assumed elastic range, the table and light spot return to their initial states.

The main apparatus uses the actual tiny angles. The profile explicitly magnifies vertical deformation by 500; the wall is a separate close view with a millimetre scale. Their pixel distances are not directly comparable.

<h2 id="deeper">Shared geometry connects the beam and mirrors</h2>

<details>
<summary>What is calculated</summary>

The table is approximated by a uniform, simply supported, 1 m beam under a central load, with flexural rigidity EI = 20000 N·m². Its central deflection is F L³ / (48 EI). Real tables have different supports, joints and materials; these illustrative values cannot determine the stiffness of a particular table.

Each mirror support follows its local beam slope. A ray from the fixed source intersects the moving first mirror, reflects, intersects and reflects from the second, then reaches the wall. Mirror-center motion is included. One mirror rotating through a small angle turns a fixed incoming beam through twice that angle; both mirrors jointly determine the final spot here.

</details>

<details>
<summary>The textbook's other optical example</summary>

The same page illustrates loaded acrylic viewed under special optical conditions. Its patterns reveal information about internal stress. This is a different engineering method for displaying an otherwise hidden change, rather than mirror-based angular amplification.

At this point the textbook introduces the method and an illustration, not a complete student polarization experiment. We preserve that distinction and do not present arbitrary colored patterns as quantitative stress measurements. Interpreting photoelastic fringes requires material, thickness, optical arrangement and calibration information.

</details>
