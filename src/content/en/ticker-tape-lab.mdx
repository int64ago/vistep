<h2 id="understand">Why do the gaps grow when the ticking stays steady?</h2>

The timer controls **when** a mark appears. The moving tape determines how far apart the marks are. A larger gap means the tape travelled farther during the same time interval.

Wide gaps alone do not establish acceleration. Two tapes moving at different constant speeds both have evenly spaced marks; the faster tape has wider gaps. To detect acceleration, follow one record: does the displacement keep increasing over successive equal times?

Four textbook paths use this evidence. The timer chapter establishes time; the velocity chapters measure an interval and then a local neighborhood; the cart chapter turns equal-time strips into a velocity plot; the free-fall chapter predicts a later mark from earlier gaps. A dot keeps its acquisition identity throughout the explanation.

<h2 id="try">Predict before revealing the next mark</h2>

- **Fixed ticks:** compare two constant-speed records. The faster tape still has equal gaps. Five intervals require six dots. At the 50 Hz used here, neighboring marks are 0.02 s apart.
- **Measure velocity:** compare 0.20–0.40 s with 0.60–0.80 s. The later interval is equally long in time but covers more distance, so its average velocity is higher. That number describes an interval.
- **Local velocity:** keep 0.50 s fixed and bring the recorded endpoints closer on both sides. The gold finite-interval average approaches the blue instantaneous model value; these are different kinds of information.
- **Accelerating cart:** group five short gaps at a time. The example gives 1.5, 2.1, 2.7 and 3.3 cm, each over 0.10 s. If that pattern continues, the next gap should be 3.9 cm. Pause, then reveal the new mark.
- **Free fall:** successive gaps are 4.9, 14.7 and 24.5 cm, increasing by 9.8 cm each time. Constant acceleration predicts a next gap of 34.3 cm. Doubling the mass leaves the predicted drop distance unchanged in the ideal drag-free model.

Exploration lets you change records, inspect acquisition, vary the cart's model acceleration, narrow the velocity interval and reveal a predicted mark. Acceleration comparisons keep common physical scales. Rescaling every record to the same displayed length would conceal the effect being compared.

<h2 id="deeper">How a paper record supports a physical inference</h2>

<details>
<summary>An interval directly gives an average velocity</summary>

<div className="formula">vaverage = Δx / Δt</div>

The velocity path has smooth but nonconstant acceleration. A wide interval centered on 0.50 s does not give exactly the velocity at its midpoint. Narrowing the interval reduces the difference. The instantaneous value is an analytical model reference, not a precise reading supposedly obtained directly from two marks.

The instrument records only at tick times. In a 50 Hz record, the nearest marks on either side of the center are 0.02 s earlier and later: the smallest symmetric neighborhood still spans 0.04 s. Continuous screen interpolation does not create additional samples. Shorter intervals also magnify real ruler errors.

Constant acceleration has a special property: an interval's average velocity equals its instantaneous velocity at the midpoint. The cart's velocity points therefore belong at 0.05, 0.15, 0.25 and 0.35 s, rather than at arbitrarily chosen endpoints. This property does not hold for every changing motion.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why can equal-time gaps predict another mark?</summary>

For constant acceleration, consecutive displacements over the same duration T differ by aT². Three already recorded positions give:

<div className="formula">a = (xnext − 2xmiddle + xprevious) / T²</div>

If that acceleration continues, the next gap equals the last gap plus the increment between the last two gaps. The dashed strip shows this conditional prediction. The solid marks appear afterward as a new model record. The prediction function receives only three known positions; it does not look up the future position.

The cart's gap increment is 0.6 cm. With T = 0.10 s, acceleration is 0.006 / 0.10² = 0.60 m/s². Free fall has a 9.8 cm increment, giving 0.098 / 0.10² = 9.80 m/s². Increasing velocity does not imply increasing acceleration.

</details>

<details>
<summary>The apparatus and the model's limits</summary>

An electromagnetic timer uses a vibrating striker and carbon paper; a spark timer uses periodic discharges. The illustration shows their recording function with ideal 50 Hz sampling. It does not model electrical transients or high-voltage operation. In a real experiment, check the frequency, choose clear marks and repeat the record. The textbook also recommends avoiding the closely packed initial marks when they are difficult to measure.

Old marks move with the tape; new marks form at the stationary needle. Analysis arranges the same record from early to late time. Separating its intervals and aligning their starting points changes the view, not the data.

Cart acceleration is prescribed. The string gives the cart and hanging mass equal displacement magnitudes. This scene does not substitute hanging weight for tension; an accelerating hanging mass has unequal weight and tension. Free fall neglects air and tape resistance. These are teaching-model records, not independent experimental measurements of g or a fresh validation of F = ma.

</details>
