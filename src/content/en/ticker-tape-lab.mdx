<h2 id="understand">Time stays regular; the spacing records motion</h2>

A ticker timer marks at fixed time intervals. At the ideal50 Hz used here, neighboring dots are0.02 s apart. Five intervals occupy0.10 s and require six dots. Faster tape travels farther during the same interval, leaving wider gaps.

Four textbook paths use this recording method: learning the timer, measuring average and instantaneous velocity, investigating an accelerating cart, and studying a falling mass. Shared apparatus does not make the questions or analysis steps interchangeable.

An electromagnetic timer drives a vibrating striker, making marks through carbon paper. A spark timer uses periodic electrical discharges. The actual marking frequency must be checked for either instrument. This film uses ideal50 Hz sampling and does not model the electrical transient, high voltage, or friction on the tape.

<h2 id="try">Choose a measurement question before reading the tape</h2>

- **Timer:** move the recording time and watch a new dot appear after each fixed interval. Count gaps, rather than multiplying the number of dots by the period.
- **Velocity:** select two recorded positions and divide their displacement difference by their time difference. Shortening the measurement window brings its average closer to the velocity at the window’s start.
- **Accelerating cart:** calculate velocities from equal-time records, then plot them against time. The slope gives acceleration.
- **Free fall:** apply the same sampling method to a vertically falling mass. Differences between consecutive equal-time displacements yield9.8 m/s² in this drag-free model.

The apparatus and the removed tape are two views of one record. The removed strip is arranged from early to late time for reading. Its dots, ruler measurements, and graph all come from the same motion.

<h2 id="deeper">Measurements and their assumptions</h2>

<details>
<summary>Average velocity is not the velocity at an arbitrary instant</summary>

<div className="formula">vaverage = Δx/Δt</div>

For general changing motion, a shrinking interval is needed to approach instantaneous velocity. The hand-pulled path deliberately uses smooth nonconstant acceleration, so a broad interval is not mistaken for an exact instantaneous measurement.

Constant acceleration has a useful special property: the average over a time interval symmetric about its midpoint equals the velocity at that midpoint. This is the usual cart and free-fall tape method; it is not a general rule for all motion.
