<h2 id="understand">Why do two steady tones swell and fade?</h2>

Two nearby frequencies continually change their relative phase at a receiver. Each source can remain perfectly steady. Sometimes their pressures reinforce; sometimes they nearly cancel. The combined amplitude therefore rises and falls slowly.

A remains at 220 Hz while B begins at 222 Hz. The local traces magnify a 50 millisecond window, while the envelope spans 2.5 seconds. These are deliberately different time scales. A cursor connects the current observation time to the slow envelope.

<h2 id="try">Reduce the frequency difference</h2>

In exploration, move B from 224 Hz toward 220 Hz. The swelling slows and disappears when the frequencies match. Reduce B's amplitude to see why unequal waves no longer cancel completely. This visual experiment does not automatically play a test tone.

Seeking reconstructs both component signals, their sum, the envelope and the phase difference. The reinforcement and cancellation shots move slowly around selected phase relationships rather than pretending that the drawn animation rate is the acoustic frequency.

<h2 id="deeper">The envelope is not a new low note</h2>

For equal amplitudes, sin(2πf₁t) + sin(2πf₂t) = 2 cos[π(f₂−f₁)t] sin[π(f₁+f₂)t]. The rapid oscillation has a center frequency near the average, while the amplitude magnitude reaches a maximum |f₂−f₁| times each second.

The signed cosine factor changes sign. Since the swelling depends on its magnitude, the beat frequency is the absolute frequency difference, not half that difference. For amplitudes A and B, the envelope is √[A²+B²+2AB cos(Δφ)].

This is linear pressure addition at one receiver. It excludes room reflections, perceptual loudness, nonlinear difference-tone generation and source directivity. Normalized pressure is not decibels, and the envelope is not an additional low-frequency sound wave.
