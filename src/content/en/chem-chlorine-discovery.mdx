<section id="understand">
## Obtaining the gas and recognizing the element are separate events

Scheele obtained the yellow-green gas in 1774; Davy recognized chlorine as an element in 1810. A distinctive observation does not automatically provide the correct explanation of composition. The bottle and Cl₂ symbols in the history view are modern diagrams, not reconstructions of the original apparatus or Scheele’s knowledge.

The textbook’s modern apparatus connects generation, collection and tail-gas absorption. A long inlet feeds the lower collecting jar, and gas leaving the upper outlet reaches sodium hydroxide solution. Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O transfers chlorine into dissolved ions rather than destroying it.

<summary>The collection model does not predict real efficiency</summary>

The collecting jar is an ideal well-mixed vessel with unit gas capacity. After relative throughput q, retained chlorine is 1−exp(−q), while cumulative departing chlorine is q−[1−exp(−q)]. Actual chlorine is denser than air and undergoes nonuniform displacement and mixing. This model connects input, retention and exhaust; it cannot specify actual collection times.

Absorption consumes two hydroxide units per chlorine unit, producing chloride and hypochlorite. Gas–liquid transfer, reaction rates, temperature, pressure differences and competing reactions are omitted. Tracking dots use the actual tubing polyline.
