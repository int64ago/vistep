<section id="understand">
## An observation can survive a change of explanation

Scheele obtained a yellow-green gas in 1774. Its colour was an observation, not a direct determination of composition. A later oxygen-based explanation proposed that the gas contained readily transferable oxygen. That proposal needed a testable consequence.

In his 1810 paper, Davy discussed charcoal previously freed of hydrogen and moisture. Even strong heating failed to produce the expected decomposition. This challenged the proposed transferable oxygen, but a single failed reaction could not establish an element. The film’s vessel is a modern observation diagram; the dashed O? is a proposed explanation, not a particle Scheele observed.

Another result sharpens the question. Gay-Lussac and Thénard had observed water when hydrogen chloride contacted lead oxide. Did oxygen in that product mean the gas originally contained it? Davy identified another possible source: the oxide itself.

The modern net account is:

**PbO + 2HCl → PbCl₂ + H₂O**

Follow the same amber oxygen. It begins in lead oxide and is then counted in water with hydrogen supplied by the acid. Lead and chlorine are counted in the salt. The water observation remains; explaining it does not require water to have been present in the gas beforehand.

In 1810 Davy argued from several observations for treating chlorine as an element. Only at the end does the film overlay the modern molecular account H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, separating a present-day explanation from a historical observation.

</section>

<section id="try">
## Test explanations without rewriting observations

Change the explanation in “Fixed record”. The historical observation stays unchanged; the explanatory prediction changes.

In “Oxygen source”, remove PbO while retaining HCl and adding no external water. The listed inputs contain no oxygen, so the model cannot create new water. Then add one initial water unit: water is now present, but the initial water remains separately tagged and the amount of new water remains zero. A positive observation alone does not identify a material source.

The allocation slider moves the same atoms through a net account. It is not elapsed reaction time, an elementary pathway or a rate measurement. Element counts remain invariant throughout.

The modern-apparatus textbook entry has its own two-chapter explanation. Follow the connected generator, collector and absorber. Collector capacity is finite, so later chlorine also leaves in the outflow. Reduce hydroxide capacity: absorption becomes limited, and unabsorbed chlorine remains in the balance.

<section id="deeper">
<details>
<summary>Historical record and modern model have different roles</summary>

The historical argument follows a reprint of Davy’s 1810 paper. The carbon test, the lead-oxide discussion and combination with hydrogen are distinct pieces of evidence. The water observation was attributed by Davy to Gay-Lussac and Thénard; it is not presented here as an experiment discovered by Davy alone.

Davy also reported a little condensation after attempted drying, decreasing as impurities were reduced. The film does not replace that account with “exactly zero water was measured”. Zero new water with no oxygen input is a modern conservation prediction for explicitly listed substances, not a historical measurement.

Coloured atoms represent a net material allocation, not isotope tracing, direct imaging or an elementary reaction mechanism. PbCl₂ identifies product composition; its diagram does not claim an isolated salt molecule’s structure. Lead, oxygen, hydrogen and chlorine identities persist during the allocation, and external initial water is tracked separately. Gas–solid interfaces, aqueous dissociation, conversion rates and side reactions are outside the model.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Conservation through collection and absorption</summary>

The collector is an ideal well-mixed vessel with unit gas capacity. After relative throughput q, retained chlorine is 1−exp(−q), while cumulative outflow is q−[1−exp(−q)]. Their sum is q. Real apparatus includes nonuniform displacement and mixing; this model does not predict efficiency or collection times.

For the textbook’s ordinary-temperature alkaline net reaction, Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O, one chlorine unit consumes two hydroxide units. Available hydroxide limits absorption. Retained, absorbed and unabsorbed portions jointly conserve chlorine. Gas–liquid transfer, actual flow, pressure and products under hot concentrated alkaline conditions are not included.

Tubing and tracer positions use the same vertices. The phone composition changes the arrangement while retaining the long inlet to the collector bottom and its upper outlet to the absorber. Bubbles rise only beneath the liquid surface. Chlorine is toxic; this explanation does not provide home generation conditions or recipes.

</details>
