<h2 id="understand">Interface shape reveals surface tension</h2>

Paper clips submerged in brim-full water displace liquid into a small raised surface. This differs from the floating-needle experiment: the clips sink and the observation concerns interface shape and edge pinning. Once the pinning limit is reached, further displacement spills.

Soap-film threads have two different constraints. A slack fixed-end thread forms an arc after film on one side is punctured. A closed thread loop becomes nearly circular after its inner film is removed. Neither requires the thread to stretch; remaining film area decreases.

Equal-volume water drops on clean and waxed glass have different contact angles and spreading. Tissue can gently place a needle on water, then sink after wetting while the needle remains. Once pushed completely underwater, the needle loses interface support and its buoyancy cannot balance its weight.

<h2 id="try">Control conditions and compare</h2>

- Count submerged clips and compare surface rise with spillage; the detail view enlarges only vertical changes.
- Compare intact and punctured film for fixed-end thread and closed loop separately.
- Change contact angle while retaining a 5 μL drop volume.
- Compare a needle at the interface with one completely submerged.

<h2 id="deeper">Four distinct approximations</h2>

The brim uses the small-slope axisymmetric Young–Laplace equation including gravity. The edge is pinned with an illustrative limiting slope of 20°. Glass radius is 22 mm, surface tension 0.072 N/m and each clip has mass 0.35 g and density 7800 kg/m³. Raised volume plus spillage equals displaced volume; predicted clip capacity is not measured data.

Thread shapes conserve arc length. The final fixed-end thread is circular, and the closed loop becomes a circle. Intermediate shapes are constrained interpolations rather than fluid-rupture or viscous-flow simulations. A soap film has two interfaces, so its force includes contributions from both surfaces.

Drops are constant-volume 5 μL spherical caps neglecting gravity. The 35° and 110° angles are illustrative. The steel needle has length 3 cm and radius 0.45 mm; its interface-state model emphasizes upward tension and neglects smaller buoyancy. After complete submersion, buoyancy, weight and simplified linear damping determine sinking. The needle cross-section and depression are enlarged for teaching.
