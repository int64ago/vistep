<section id="understand">
## Connect observations by following the same material

Two clear solutions produce a white solid. Follow barium and sulfate from the water into barium sulfate. Sodium and chloride remain dissolved. Cancelling spectator ions in an equation does not remove them from the sample. With too little barium, sulfate remains; with excess barium, the extra barium stays in solution.

A steady solid can still exchange material with water. At equilibrium, dissolution and precipitation balance. This surface exchange is explained in [OpenStax’s treatment of solubility equilibrium](https://openstax.org/books/chemistry-2e/pages/15-1-precipitation-and-dissolution).

For magnesium hydroxide, acid first removes dissolved hydroxide. Follow a proton from hydrated hydrogen to hydroxide, forming water, then follow magnesium and hydroxide leaving the solid. The ion product Q = [Mg²⁺][OH⁻]² falls below the solubility product, allowing further dissolution. A retained water control still contains solid. Magnesium changes phase without disappearing. [Rutgers’ magnesium hydroxide demonstration](https://www.chem.rutgers.edu/cldf-demos/1023-cldf-demo-milk-of-magnesia) describes hydroxide consumption followed by dissolution of more suspended solid.

The silver window retains the same silver identities. Yellow silver iodide takes up dissolved silver, allowing silver chloride to dissolve further. Each silver sulfide formula unit then needs two silver atoms. The iron window tests a different inference: does the same brown precipitate prove the same amount of old-solid conversion? Extra dissolved hydroxide can also supply the new precipitate. Tracking magnesium distinguishes the explanations.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Predict before changing a condition

In “Acid,” compare limited and sufficient acid while keeping total magnesium constant. Predict whether solid remains, then inspect the solid and water inventories. The separate neutralization view explains proton transfer; its molecule count does not represent the selected acid dose.

In “Silver salts,” set initial iodide to 0.3 mmol and subsequent total S(-II) to 0.2 mmol. The selected intermediate contains AgCl 0.6, AgI 0 and Ag₂S 0.2 mmol, with released iodide in water. If residual AgCl buffers dissolved silver at saturation, that iodide makes AgI supersaturated. Select “Re-form AgI” to follow silver and iodide reallocation. The near-complete inventory limit is AgCl 0.3, AgI 0.3 and Ag₂S 0.2 mmol. The intermediate cannot simply be declared final.

In “Iron & magnesium,” give both samples the same iron(III) amount but different initial extra hydroxide. Equal brown precipitates can accompany different dissolved magnesium amounts. A photograph of the brown solid alone is insufficient: check magnesium’s destination and the initial excess base.

<details>
<summary>Models, textbook locations and limits</summary>

The four windows follow PEP Required Book 1, printed page 17 (PDF 22, Experiment 1-3), and Elective Book 1, printed pages 82–83 (PDF 87–88, Experiments 3-3, 3-4, 3-5). Each has its own starting observation, explanation and test. The enlarged views explain material transfer; they are neither experimental instructions nor microscope recordings.

Barium sulfate uses 1 mmol initial sulfate in a fixed final volume of 0.1 L and Ksp=1.1×10⁻¹⁰ at 25°C. Magnesium hydroxide uses total Mg=0.010 mol/L, Ksp=1.5×10⁻¹¹, water autoionization, magnesium balance and charge balance. Constants follow the [UMass solubility-product table](https://owl.oit.umass.edu/departments/Chemistry/appendix/ksp.html). Concentrations approximate activities. These model quantities are not conversions of textbook drops. Acid intermediates conserve charge and Mg while illustrating relaxation after acid mixing; they are not a kinetic prediction. Q reaches Ksp only at equilibrium with solid remaining. After all solid dissolves, Q may stay below Ksp.

Silver uses a near-complete net inventory limit, **not a complete three-solid equilibrium solution**. Under the illustrated alkaline conditions with effective sulfide present, silver sulfide formation is taken as nearly complete. Trace dissolution, protonation, complexation and rates are omitted. Removing silver from AgI first is a selected explanatory path, not an observed reaction order; released iodide can subsequently re-form AgI. Textbook page 84 gives AgCl and AgI Ksp values of 1.8×10⁻¹⁰ and 8.5×10⁻¹⁷. Conditional on residual AgCl saturation, the example intermediate has Q(AgI)/Ksp≈1.59×10⁶. This establishes its instability without claiming to solve all free-ion concentrations.

Each of ten silver markers represents a 0.1 mmol inventory parcel. Departure removes it from the source solid; before arrival it belongs to the water/transit ledger. This does not predict that such a large dissolved Ag⁺ concentration accumulates. The separate surface close-up shows exchange direction, not bulk phase proportions. Paired markers express the 2Ag:1S formula ratio rather than a real crystal lattice. Exploration doses keep pairs visible. Motion is explanatory progress, not measured reaction time.

The iron model separates direct hydroxide supply from supply by the original magnesium solid. Allocating extra hydroxide first is an accounting convention, not a claim of strict chronological reaction order. The net conversion is 3Mg(OH)₂ + 2Fe³⁺ → 2Fe(OH)₃ + 3Mg²⁺. Enough extra hydroxide can instead supply 3OH⁻ + Fe³⁺ → Fe(OH)₃. Iron hydrolysis, colloid form, ageing and full activity effects are not solved; brown colour alone cannot determine the contribution of either route.
