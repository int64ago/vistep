<h2 id="understand">Apply the same disturbance to different samples</h2>

The textbook compares tap water, phosphate buffer and liver homogenate. Each comparison begins with a fresh 25 mL aliquot and equal additions of acid or base. Sample volume, addition and reading time are controlled; composition changes. The acid and base series use separate fresh aliquots, rather than adding base to a previously acidified sample.

A conjugate pair accepts or releases hydrogen ions, interconverting between acid HA and base A⁻. The pair's total amount remains present. Buffering reduces a pH change within a finite range; it does not keep pH permanently at seven. A sufficiently large acid addition overwhelms the available capacity.

<h2 id="try">Compare an equal addition, then cross the boundary</h2>

Compare ten and thirty drops in all three samples. Changing to base reconstructs the calculation from the initial composition while preserving drop count for comparison. Extend the addition to 120 drops to inspect exhaustion. Thirty drops is the textbook recording range; the longer interval is an explicit boundary exploration.

Sample color identifies the material; it is **not an indicator color**. The curve, liquid level and pH reading come from the same calculation. The expandable amount record follows acid and base forms whose sum remains constant.

<h2 id="deeper">Solve the charge balance</h2>

At 25 °C the model uses a water ion product of 10⁻¹⁴ and one effective buffer pair with pKa 7.2. For hydrogen-ion concentration H and total buffer concentration C, the base-form concentration is CKa/(Ka+H). Bisection solves:

<div className="formula">H + Cstrong ions − Kw/H − CKa/(Ka + H) = 0</div>

The initial strong-ion charge is chosen consistently with pH seven. Acid supplies additional anions, base supplies cations, and each drop increases volume. This balance remains usable near exhaustion without extending the Henderson–Hasselbalch approximation beyond its valid composition range.

A 50 μL drop and total effective buffer concentrations of 20 and 12 mmol/L are **teaching choices**. The biological sample uses a single effective pair, not the actual composition or measured titration curve of liver homogenate. Water omits minerals, atmospheric carbon dioxide and activity corrections, so its exact reading is not a prediction for tap water. Respiratory and renal regulation are outside this beaker model.

An independent benchmark puts 0.15 mmol of strong acid into 26.5 mL after thirty drops of 0.1 mol/L titrant: pH is approximately 2.247. Tests also check the fresh initial state, acid/base symmetry, conserved buffer amount and finite capacity.
