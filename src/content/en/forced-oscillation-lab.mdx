<h2 id="understand">One drive, different natural frequencies</h2>

A motor pin slides in a horizontal slot, moving the shared crossbar vertically and driving two unlike spring-and-weight systems. Startup contains both a transient and the driven response. After transients decay, both follow the motor frequency, although their amplitudes and phases differ.

The displayed settled frequencies come from measured intervals between later zero crossings. Changing motor frequency changes both responses. Merely changing stiffness does not make sustained forced motion ultimately return to each spring’s natural frequency.

<h2 id="try">Transmit motion through the support</h2>

Pendulums A through G share a slightly movable crossbar. Releasing heavy pendulum D moves the support, which acts on the lighter pendulums. When A, D and G have equal lengths, A and G respond strongly over the observation window. Shortening D to match B changes the strongest responding light pendulum.

No motor continuously replenishes the released heavy pendulum. Energy exchanges and gradually dissipates, so this finite-time response is not a permanent steady state. The inset magnifies the tiny support displacement twentyfold; the main apparatus keeps a consistent scale.

<h2 id="deeper">Connections and back reaction in one model</h2>

The motor apparatus uses prescribed spring-support motion, light springs and linear damping, with an analytic solution satisfying startup conditions. The pendulum apparatus solves the horizontal crossbar motion and all seven small-angle pendula together. Every light pendulum reacts back on the support and the driver.

Without damping, total support kinetic energy, support spring energy, and all pendulum kinetic and gravitational energies are conserved. Damping reduces this total. Similar natural frequencies generally produce stronger response, but the exact peak also depends on damping, coupling and the drive mechanism; the textbook’s weak-damping approximation is not universal.
