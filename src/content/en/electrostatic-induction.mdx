<h2 id="understand">Charge changes location, not its total amount</h2>

Conduction electrons can move through a metal while its positive ions remain in the material. Rubbing may transfer electrons between materials: one becomes positive and the other equally negative. Glass and silk illustrate one familiar case, not a universal direction of charge transfer.

A positive object near two connected conductors draws electrons toward the nearer conductor A. B is left with positive net charge. **Separate the conductors before removing the external object** and they retain opposite charges. Reconnect them and the charges redistribute. The external object never touches the pair.

An electroscope's knob, stem and leaves form a connected conductor. Its leaves may separate when a charged object approaches even though the instrument's total charge remains zero. Opening alone therefore does not identify charge sign. A known initial charge makes changes in opening useful for comparison.

<h2 id="try">Choose the apparatus and follow the same charge</h2>

- **Friction:** compare the opposite charges on the glass and cloth.
- **Induction:** approach, separate, withdraw and reconnect. Reverse the external charge and the conductors exchange signs.
- **Electroscope:** compare neutral, positively precharged and negatively precharged states. Approach slowly: an opening can decrease, pass through zero, then increase again.
- **Charged filaments:** increase their common charge and observe spreading against elastic restoring forces. This is a virtual model of the textbook's charged-hair observation.

<h2 id="deeper">What the numerical model assumes</h2>

<details>
<summary>Conductors, foil leaves and elastic filaments</summary>

A lumped electrostatic-potential approximation enforces equal potential and total-charge conservation for connected conductors. Once separated, each retains its net charge. This explains the ordering of induction operations without predicting detailed surface charge density.

Foil opening follows a static balance between gravity and Coulomb repulsion, approximating charge at the tips. Filaments include pairwise Coulomb forces, gravity and elastic root torques. The displayed angles are derived from those models; they are neither an instrument calibration nor a prediction of real hair geometry.

Leakage, breakdown, air motion and contact potentials are omitted. Symbols indicate net charge, and a few drawn carriers do not represent actual particle counts. The lesson contains no construction or operating instructions for high-voltage apparatus.

</details>
