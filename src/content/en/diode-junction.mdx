<h2 id="understand">Depleted of mobile carriers, not of all charge</h2>

A PN junction joins regions with different doping. Holes are the majority carriers in the p region, electrons in the n region. Far from the junction, mobile carriers approximately balance the fixed dopant ions, making each bulk region electrically neutral.

Near the junction, mobile-carrier concentrations are greatly reduced. Negative acceptor ions remain on the p side and positive donor ions on the n side. They create a built-in field pointing from n to p. At thermal equilibrium, drift and diffusion balance: **terminal current is zero**, even though the internal field is not.

Making the p terminal positive relative to n lowers the junction barrier, narrows the depletion layer and increases carrier injection. Ideal forward current grows continuously and exponentially. There is no universal gate that suddenly opens at 0.7 V.

Reverse bias increases the barrier and depletion width. Here the ideal reverse current approaches −1 pA. Real leakage and breakdown require a more complete device model.

<h2 id="try">Connect the spatial profile to the terminal readings</h2>

- At zero applied voltage, observe a nonzero built-in potential difference alongside zero terminal current.
- Select space charge. The negative region is wider with lower density; the positive region is narrower with higher density. Their signed areas cancel.
- Increase forward voltage. Watch the depletion boundaries approach, the barrier fall and current rise.
- Inspect the logarithmic I–V plot. At 300 K, roughly 60 mV multiplies ideal forward diffusion current by ten.
- Add a 100 Ω series resistor. As applied voltage rises, an increasing share appears across the resistor rather than the junction.

<h2 id="deeper">Separate approximations for space and current</h2>

<details>
<summary>Abrupt-junction depletion approximation</summary>

The depletion region contains fixed ionized dopant charge; the bulk regions outside are approximately neutral. Charge balance requires `Nₐxₚ = Nᴅxₙ`. With three times as much doping on the n side, the p-side depletion width is three times larger.

<div className="formula">V_bi = V_T ln(NₐNᴅ/nᵢ²); V_T = kT/q</div>
<div className="formula">W² = (2ε/q)(1/Nₐ + 1/Nᴅ)(V_bi − Vj)</div>

At 300 K, this example uses silicon permittivity 11.7ε₀, `Nₐ = 10²² m⁻³`, `Nᴅ = 3×10²² m⁻³` and `nᵢ = 10¹⁶ m⁻³`. These give a built-in potential near 0.743 V and a zero-bias depletion width near 358 nm.

Potential follows Poisson's equation. Both it and its slope are continuous; electric field is its negative gradient. The internal potential difference is not an accessible battery voltage at the external terminals. Contact potentials and thermal equilibrium must be considered together.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Exponential current with a series resistor</summary>

The ideal diffusion-current relation is:

<div className="formula">I = I_s [exp(Vj/V_T) − 1]</div>

A device saturation current of 1 pA is specified independently. It also depends on junction area and minority-carrier transport, so doping values alone do not determine it. Temperature remains fixed at 300 K.

With a series resistor, `Vapplied = Vj + IR` must also hold. The model solves for junction voltage before computing current and depletion width. Substituting the entire applied voltage into the exponential would ignore the resistor drop.

These are steady configurations, not individual carrier trajectories or a junction-formation transient. The model omits high injection, recombination leakage, breakdown, distributed fields in series material, self-heating and stored-charge switching. Bare-junction forward voltage is limited to 0.60 V. With 100 Ω in series, applied voltage can reach 1 V.

</details>
