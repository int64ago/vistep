<h2 id="understand">Identify the recipient of each force</h2>

Connect the hooks of two spring balances and pull slowly, with one end fixed or both held. The readings have equal magnitudes but refer to forces on different objects: B pulls A rightward, while A pulls B leftward. They describe the same interaction.

A different balance stiffness can produce a different internal extension. Each scale is calibrated independently, so unequal extensions do not imply unequal forces. Neither greater motion nor greater stretch identifies a stronger member of an action–reaction pair.

Force sensors preserve the full history. With rightward defined as positive for both, the simultaneous traces are mirror images about zero. Moving both instruments does not remove the equal-and-opposite interaction; Newton's third law does not require rest.

<h2 id="try">Change the apparatus without changing the force pair</h2>

- Increase endpoint separation. Both readings rise while the common hooks remain connected.
- Change B's equivalent stiffness and compare extension with force. These are different quantities.
- Translate the entire apparatus. Moving the coordinate origin does not change the interaction.
- Pause sensor records and compare the same instant, rather than two peaks reached at different times.

The action–reaction forces act on A and B separately. They cannot cancel as balanced forces on one object. To test A's equilibrium, also include the hand's force and any other forces on A.

<h2 id="deeper">Instrument assumptions are separate from the law</h2>

<details>

<summary>Connected-instrument model</summary>

Each unloaded instrument spans 0.20 m. A has equivalent stiffness 80 N/m; B is adjustable. Internal inertia, connector mass and self-weight are neglected in a quasistatic elastic calculation:

<div className="formula">T = (D − 0.40) / (1/kₐ + 1/kᵦ); xₐ = T/kₐ; xᵦ = T/kᵦ</div>

D is the outer-end separation. Adding A's length and extension to the left end gives the same joint position as subtracting B's length and extension from the right end. Springs, rods, cases and readings share this geometry.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Reading moving instruments</summary>

Fast motion of real instruments introduces internal-mass, response-time, zero and synchronization effects. This scene uses quasistatic instruments to isolate the common-contact force pair. That instrument approximation does not restrict Newton's third law to stationary objects.

Sensor mounting directions can differ. Real records require a common sign convention and synchronized time axis. Here both signs use rightward positive, rather than directly overlaying two raw channels that each define tension as positive.

</details>
