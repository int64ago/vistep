<h2 id="understand">How can visible products reveal an unseen particle?</h2>

Detectors record energy deposits, curved charged tracks, recoiling ions and counts. Charge, momentum and energy conservation connect these observations to the properties of an unseen parent. A reconstructable candidate still needs another test: can background explain a peak, does the peak survive a changed magnetic field, and does a separate experiment find a similar scale?

The film preserves all seven textbook entries, including the two parity entries. Each selected window starts from its own measurable phenomenon; apparatus qualifications and calculation limits remain available below.

<h2 id="try">Change a condition and make a prediction</h2>

Boost the example Higgs parent. The two photon energies change, but including total momentum keeps the reconstructed mass at 125 GeV/c². Lower the spectrometer field from 1 to 0.9 T and watch the hit move on the same detector plane, eight meters downstream. Should the mass peak move too? For the antihyperon event, compare an ordinary neutron's kinetic energy with the minimum energy required by the charged products at B.

Published records are fixed. Controls change an example, a hypothesis or a displayed fit component; they neither regenerate observations nor refit an experiment.

<summary>Mass is not the sum of laboratory energies</summary>

<div className="formula">m²c⁴ = (ΣE)² − |Σp|²c²; two photons: m²c⁴ = 2E₁E₂(1−cosθ)</div>

A stationary 125 GeV/c² example parent produces opposite 62.5 GeV photons. Boosting along one photon at 0.6c changes their energies to 125 and 31.25 GeV. Total energy is 156.25 GeV and total momentum times c is 93.75 GeV, leaving the same 125 GeV/c² mass. This is a kinematic example, not a published candidate event. Photons do not produce continuous charged tracks in the inner detector; dashed lines indicate reconstructed flight directions.

The fixed two-GeV-bin markers and curves are approximate readings of [ATLAS's 2012 discovery paper, figure 4(a)](https://arxiv.org/abs/1207.7214); counts are rounded to five. They are not synthetic draws. The background curve is the **background component of the published joint fit**, not a separately fitted background-only hypothesis. Residuals expose the local excess; no discovery significance is calculated from these approximate figure readings.

The later comparison shows the published combined mass centers, 126.0 GeV/c² for ATLAS and 125.3 GeV/c² for [CMS](https://arxiv.org/abs/1207.7235). These combine several decay channels and cannot be derived from this diphoton illustration alone. The weighted CMS histogram is not overlaid as raw counts. Error bars are omitted, so the displayed centers do not support a precision consistency calculation.

<details>
<summary>A positive antihyperon: conservation at A, identification at B</summary>

The [1960 original paper](https://wulixb.iphy.ac.cn/article/doi/10.7498/aps.16.365) reports an event recorded in 1959. Both the parent and the pion emerging at A are positive. The decay is anti(Σ⁻) → n̄ + π⁺; the minus sign in the name of the ordinary Σ⁻ must not be mistaken for the antiparticle's charge.

The pion momentum is 244±10 MeV/c. Direct parent curvature gives 1104±600 MeV/c, a broad uncertainty whose nominal missing-momentum direction does not exactly coincide with AB. Using the measured AB direction, angles 39°38′ and 5°29′, and the pion momentum as **inputs**, two-body reconstruction with modern masses gives about 1809 MeV/c for the parent momentum and 1183 MeV/c² for its mass. These agree within uncertainties with the paper's rounded reconstruction, 1798±100 and 1182±14. AB cannot also serve as an independent prediction of that same calculation.

At B, measured charged-product kinetic energies total 1203±60 MeV. Adding about 280 MeV to create the two charged pions gives a minimum requirement of 1483±60 MeV. The incoming ordinary neutron would carry only about 941 MeV of kinetic energy. An antineutron annihilating with a target nucleon can additionally release roughly 2mc² of rest energy, making about 2820 MeV available. The drawing retains associated vertices and measured charged products, but omits target residue and unseen neutral products. Its six outgoing tracks are not a closed charge or energy account for the entire nuclear reaction.

</details>

<summary>J/ψ: change the tracks, test the mass; scan the beams, test a resonance</summary>

<div className="formula">
  p /(GeV/c) = 0.2998 |q/e| B /T · r /m; equal head-on beams: Σp=0 and √s=2E
</div>

The example uses a forward electron and positron, each of energy 10 GeV, whose opening angle reconstructs 3.1 GeV/c². A single circular-motion model determines both paths and intersections with a fixed detector plane. Lower field means less curvature and a changed hit; using the actual field to recover momentum preserves the mass. This cross-section illustrates the relation and does not reproduce the original spectrometer's full magnetic optics.

Fixed 25 MeV bins from [Ting's 1976 Nobel lecture, figure 12(a)](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/ting-lecture.pdf) retain the hatched distribution and the lowered-field sample drawn as a white region above it. The blue outer envelope is not a second complete count distribution starting at zero. Equal exposure is not assumed. The historical calibration near 3.1 GeV remains unchanged rather than being shifted to a modern precision mass.

[Richter's lecture, figure 5](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/richter-lecture.pdf), supplies different evidence: vary the beam energy and measure hadron-production cross section. Fixed approximate figure readings use nanobarns on a logarithmic vertical axis. The horizontal break separates the 3.050 GeV point from the main scan. This is a center-of-mass energy scan, not a fixed-target two-particle mass histogram. Beam energy spread and instrumental effects contribute to the observed width, which cannot be read directly as the particle's natural width.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Teaching approximations retained in the other textbook windows</summary>

[Chao's 1930 paper](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/gxwz8-ke553) compares absorption of gamma rays near 2.6 MeV in materials. This film's energy scan is a later teaching explanation of a threshold, not a reconstruction of the original experimental scan. Pair creation starts near 2m_ec²=1.022 MeV, with the nuclear field and recoil participating in momentum conservation. The attenuation curve is not a material cross-section calculation; the early absorption anomaly is not identical to positron discovery.

Capture recoil selects the ⁷Be-to-⁷Li ground-state branch. A neutrino energy of 861.8 keV and nuclear mass near 7u give about 57 eV of recoil; other branches and atomic relaxation are omitted. The ideal planar retarding relation T=max(0,1−√(eU/E_R)) selects longitudinal energy from isotropic forward recoil. It does not reproduce the 1942 apparatus; the textbook paper's [publication record](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.61.97) does not supply a complete apparatus description.

[Wu and colleagues' 1957 paper](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.105.1413) tests spatial inversion in cobalt-60 beta decay. The film uses the normalized teaching distribution W(cosθ)=1−0.7P cosθ, with polarization P. The coefficient incorporates an illustrative velocity factor, not a historical-count fit. Inversion changes momentum p→−p while spin I→I.

</details>
