<section id="understand">

Molecular shape describes the arrangement of nuclei; electron-domain geometry also includes lone pairs. Carbon dioxide has two bonding directions despite its two double bonds. Formaldehyde has three regions. Methane, ammonia and water all have four electron regions but different atomic frameworks.

The textbook model-building practice contains H₂, Cl₂, H₂O, CO₂, NH₃ and CH₄, plus a four-balloon analogy. It remains a construction activity, not a chemical reaction experiment. Formaldehyde supplies the three-region comparison.

</section>
<section id="try">

Compare hydrogen and chlorine at a common scale, then rotate tetrahedral methane, pyramidal ammonia and bent water. Pale lone-pair markers are not extra atoms. The balloon necks remain tied while their four spatial directions turn.

Use the polarity layer to compare CO₂, H₂O, CH₄ and CH₃Cl. Arrows follow the physical electric-dipole convention, **from negative to positive**. This is opposite to the common crossed-tail chemistry arrow pointing toward the negative end. Blue arrows show bond contributions; brown shows their sum. Changing the viewpoint cannot change molecular polarity.

</section>
<section id="deeper">
<details>
<summary>Textbook inputs and geometry</summary>

One model unit is 100 pm. The six construction inputs are H–H 74 pm; Cl–Cl 198 pm; O–H 96 pm and 105°; C–O in CO₂ 116 pm and 180°; N–H 101 pm and 107°; and C–H 109 pm and 109°28′. Water’s 105° follows this textbook’s rounded model value, not a claim of greater precision than the usual approximately 104.5°. Methane uses an exact tetrahedral dot product of −1/3, with its displayed angle rounded as in the textbook.

Ammonia’s three hydrogen directions are evenly spaced around a cone whose height is solved from 107°. Water’s two bond directions follow ±52.5°. VSEPR does not calculate precise angles or an energy minimum here. Formaldehyde uses ideal 120° geometry, C=O 120 pm and C–H 109 pm; chloromethane uses an ideal tetrahedron and C–Cl 177 pm. These are teaching approximations, not fitted precision structures.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Lone-pair, balloon and polarity limits</summary>

Lone-pair outlines mark directions rather than computed electron-density surfaces. Balloons have a fixed profile and tetrahedral axes; no pressure or rubber elasticity is solved. Dashed geometric edges are not chemical bonds.

Bond vectors multiply unit directions by qualitative relative weights, then add in three dimensions. They are not Debye measurements. Equivalent bonds receive equal weights: water’s unit-vector sum has magnitude 2cos52.5°, whereas carbon dioxide and ideal methane sum to zero. This approximation does not explicitly calculate lone-pair contributions to a real molecular dipole, so it cannot predict measured magnitudes. Switching models is not a chemical conversion.

</details>
