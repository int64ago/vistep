<h2 id="understand">Random individuals, spreading populations</h2>

A drop of red dye gradually spreads through still water. Individual tracers move randomly, but a concentrated region contains more particles that can leave it. Net transport therefore spreads the dye. A nearly uniform concentration does not stop microscopic motion.

The gas thought experiment begins with particles in A and an empty chamber B. Removing the partition opens more accessible space. Four coloured identities let us enumerate every left/right assignment without confusing particles.

<h2 id="try">Count all sixteen diagrams</h2>

- Track the highlighted dye tracer: it does not always move downward.
- Compare the accessible space before and after removing the gas partition.
- Select 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4 particles in A and count 1, 4, 6, 4 or 1 assignments.
- Increase total particle count from 4 to 1000 and compare the all-in-A probability.

<h2 id="deeper">Counting and irreversibility</h2>

<div className="formula">
  Total assignments = 2ᴺ; P(all in A) = 2⁻ᴺ; four-particle multiplicities: 1, 4, 6, 4, 1
</div>

These are side assignments, not complete position-and-momentum microstates. Equal probabilities require independent particles, equal-volume halves and equilibrium. All four in one half is possible; a macroscopic number of particles returning together is overwhelmingly unlikely.

The dye model uses magnified tracers, reflected random walks and accelerated time to isolate diffusion. It omits convection, dissolution rates and molecular scale. The gas illustration omits particle collisions while preserving wall reflections and continuous positions and velocities when the partition is released.
