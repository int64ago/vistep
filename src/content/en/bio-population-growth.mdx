<h2 id="understand">A plateau need not mean inactivity</h2>

A stable population count can conceal very different processes. Individuals might stop reproducing, or new individuals might continue to appear while others die. A total alone cannot distinguish these explanations. To explain a curve, separate additions from losses.

The textbook describes five Paramecium in 0.5 mL of culture, reaching approximately 375 after about five days. Gause’s Chapter III and Figure 4 support those quantities. The following Table IV contains calculated curve values, not a daily raw-observation table. This film fixes the textbook’s initial and later endpoints, then explicitly switches to a separate teaching model. [Gause, 1934, original pp. 36–38](https://archive.org/download/struggleforexist00gauz/struggleforexist00gauz.pdf)

The model first compares a finite supply: the same 24 food markers are shared among more consumers, reducing the mean share. This illustrates competition; it does not measure Paramecium feeding or derive a birth-rate equation from food dots. We then state a testable summary: increasing density lowers births per individual and raises deaths. Equal rates give **zero net growth** without requiring either rate to vanish. Four identified individuals illustrate one possible short renewal interval, not an estimate of the population’s rates.

<h2 id="try">Predict what changes first</h2>

Changing carrying capacity or the initial count recomputes the model. An improved environment cannot create individuals instantaneously. The count stays continuous at the change, while subsequent rates differ. Carrying capacity depends on conditions rather than being an immutable species constant. With no individuals and no immigration, this model cannot spontaneously start a population.

The yeast window connects a meaningful record to a test of its explanation. The same 32 tracer cells disperse before sampling; they do not represent the flask’s entire population. A known counting area and depth allow a local count to become a concentration. The practice square is 0.2 × 0.2 mm at a depth of 0.1 mm, giving 4 × 10⁻⁶ mL. Include centers on the top and left, excluding the bottom and right. Only single cells appear in this exercise. Actual budding cultures require a stated, consistent counting convention. [Counting chambers and volume conversion](https://www.thermofisher.com/us/en/home/references/gibco-cell-culture-basics/cell-culture-protocols/counting-cells-in-a-hemacytometer.html)

A count of 20 at tenfold dilution gives 20 × 10 ÷ (4 × 10⁻⁶) = 5 × 10⁷ cells/mL, or an estimated 5 × 10⁸ cells in 10 mL. A count of 40 at fivefold dilution gives the same concentration. Each observation retains its own dilution. Convert individual samples before averaging; changing the current dilution never rewrites old samples. Missing observations differ from recorded zeros, and the number of valid repeats remains explicit.

Practice points are independent of the growth equation, not a mandatory experimental outcome. Even a later decline in total counts cannot establish that all living cells died; viability needs additional evidence. To test nutrient limitation, split the same culture, supplement one portion and give the other an equal volume of control liquid, matching other conditions. If that nutrient limits reproduction, more budding should resume after supplementation. The animation is a **conditional prediction**, not measured data or a calibrated recovery time. Other inhibition may persist despite supplementation. Equal added volumes dilute both groups equally; compare against their common post-addition baseline rather than treating dilution as an instantaneous loss of cells.

<h2 id="deeper">One curve can have several explanations</h2>

<details>
<summary>The rate model and the tracked interval</summary>

Let N be abundance, K carrying capacity and r a net-growth parameter. The model uses per-individual birth rate b(N) = 0.4 + r − rN/(2K) and death rate d(N) = 0.4 + rN/(2K), both per day. In the displayed N/K ≤ 2 domain, both remain nonnegative and N[b(N) − d(N)] = rN(1 − N/K). The curve uses this net equation’s analytic solution, with default N₀ = 5, K = 375 and r = 1.8 per day. These are illustrative parameters, not a new fit to Gause’s observations. Outside the stated domain, the program clips births to a nonnegative rate without claiming that branch is the same analytic logistic solution.

The four-individual interval freezes density and both rates over a short duration, allowing at most one first event per parent. Event probability is 1 − exp[−(b+d)Δt]; conditional birth probability is b/(b+d). Fixed draws make the example reproducible. It is not an unbiased stochastic simulation of the entire curve. One parent dividing adds one individual, while one death removes one; identities and the count ledger agree.

<details>
<summary>Why controlled comparisons remain necessary</summary>

Different combinations of births and deaths can produce the same N(t). An S-shaped record does not uniquely establish nutrient exhaustion. Monod discussed nutrients, inhibitory products and pH, while distinguishing cell numbers, dry-mass increases and growth rates. A biomass yield under a single limiting nutrient is neither this film’s yeast count data nor logistic K. Effects on eventual yield and immediate rate must also be distinguished. [Monod, 1949, original pp. 371–385](https://garcialab.berkeley.edu/courses/papers/Monod1949.pdf)

All 18 textbook canopy-survey values remain fixed when growth parameters change. The first two species generally decline, while thistle peaks at intermediate closure. Associations suggest explanations, but rainfall, temperature and day length may covary with season. A comparison must distinguish alternatives. [Population growth and regulation](https://openstax.org/books/concepts-biology/pages/19-2-population-growth-and-regulation)
