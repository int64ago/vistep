<section id="understand">
## A food sample and its filtrate have different scopes

The textbook uses spinach, grinding, water extraction and filtration to prepare a sample, then adds dilute nitric acid and KSCN. Preparation transfers recoverable components into liquid; filtration removes suspended residue; oxidation prepares Fe³⁺, which forms red complexes with SCN⁻.

This is qualitative evidence. Red colour supports accessible Fe³⁺ in the tested sample. No colour does not directly show that the original food lacks iron, and colour intensity does not directly measure nutritional content. The reagent blank shown here is an added mechanistic comparison, not a separate step listed in the textbook reference procedure.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Different causes can give the same negative observation

Compare a sample without iron, an iron-containing sample with zero recovery, and incomplete colour development. All can lack red colour while supporting different conclusions. Incomplete oxidation retains both initial Fe³⁺ and newly formed Fe³⁺, so omitting oxidation does not automatically mean no colour.

Twenty-four iron markers are teaching units. Recovery, an initial 25% Fe³⁺ distribution and colour intensity are illustrative assumptions, not spinach measurements, detection limits or absorbance calibration.

</section>
<section id="deeper">
<details>
<summary>Recovery, complex samples and source scope</summary>

The model conserves iron between filtrate and residue, and between Fe(II) and Fe(III) within the filtrate. Qualitative signal depends on accessible ferric iron and colour-development extent. Full complex equilibria, food pigments, ligands, digestion and interferences are not simulated.

[NJIT’s food-iron teaching experiment](https://web.njit.edu/~mitra/green_chemistry/EXP_6.htm) uses a different food and acid extraction to discuss recovery and calibration. It supports the importance of preparation; its recipe, reported food values and microwave steps do not replace the textbook water extraction. [RSC’s iron oxidation-state experiment](https://edu.rsc.org/experiments/oxidation-states-of-iron/517.article) supports the thiocyanate response and the effect of pre-existing Fe³⁺.

Textbook: Required Book 1, page 76, PDF page 81, research and practice on testing food for iron. This is not a quantitative nutritional analysis.

</details>
