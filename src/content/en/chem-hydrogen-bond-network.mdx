<h2 id="understand">Understand the principle</h2>

This mechanism scene covers PEP Selective Compulsory 2 page 57, with the distance table on page 58 and the ice illustration on pages 78–79. It is a structural explanation, not a student experiment or recorded molecular trajectory.

Distinguish covalent O–H within water from intermolecular O–H···O. The textbook's 276 pm is the donor-oxygen to acceptor-oxygen distance, not H···O. Moving the acceptor changes direction while the donor's covalent bond remains intact. Partial charges δ⁺ and δ⁻ are not full ionic charges.

In ordinary hexagonal ice Ih, an interior oxygen has four tetrahedrally arranged neighbours. Each molecule donates two hydrogen bonds using its own hydrogens and accepts two more. Rotation exposes these spatial relationships. The chosen orientation obeys ice rules; it does not imply that ordinary ice has this uniquely ordered proton arrangement. See [Caltech's ice-structure explanation](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~atomic/snowcrystals/ice/ice.htm).

<h2 id="try">Try it yourself</h2>

Change acceptor deviation or oxygen distance in the opening view. Dashes weaken according to geometry; the smooth display score is not an interaction-energy curve. Rotate the local cluster and extended network to distinguish projection crossings from actual connections.

The rearrangement retains 36 oxygens and their original pairs of hydrogens. Molecules translate and rotate rigidly. The oxygen framework contracts uniformly by 2.7%, with small deterministic displacements and orientation changes. Links are screened from O···O distance, H···O distance and donor alignment. They do not all vanish, and H₂O composition remains intact. This constructed path has no assigned temperature, physical time, phase fraction or measured bond lifetime. Its finite boundary cannot establish bulk density.

The last view varies a hypothetical solid density to test values below, equal to and above liquid density. It is not a temperature control that gives ordinary ice arbitrary densities. Equal-mass volumes use V=m/ρ; a freely floating object's submerged fraction is ρsolid/ρliquid. A denser object sinks. A fixed-depth rectangular vessel recomputes water level from displaced area, conserving water throughout entry.

<h2 id="deeper">Go deeper</h2>

The ideal hexagonal oxygen framework uses a=2.76/√(3/8) Å, c/a=√(8/3), and internal coordinate u=3/8. Nearest oxygens are 2.76 Å apart; tetrahedral directions have angle arccos(−1/3). Molecular O–H lengths are 0.96 Å and H–O–H angles 104.5°. Hydrogen directions are adjusted slightly between donor directions to preserve molecular geometry. These are teaching coordinates, not a refined crystal structure at a specific temperature and pressure. Sphere radii do not represent electron-cloud boundaries.

Melting partly rearranges intermolecular connections while preserving covalent molecules. Hydrogen bonding persists in liquid water, and hydrogen bonds can also occur within a molecule. See [Georgia Tech's molecular-interaction notes](https://williams.chemistry.gatech.edu/structure/molecular_interactions/mol_int.html).

The bulk view uses rounded near-melting references: ice 0.92 g/mL and water 1.00 g/mL. Thus 92 g occupies about 100 mL as ice and 92 mL as liquid, and the floating block is about 92% submerged. Density depends on temperature, pressure and dissolved material; this conclusion does not cover every high-pressure ice phase. The maximum density of water near 4 °C is also not predicted by the single rearrangement control. See [USGS water-density information](https://www.usgs.gov/water-science-school/science/water-density).
