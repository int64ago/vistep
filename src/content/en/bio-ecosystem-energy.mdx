<h2 id="understand">Put energy budgets on the same area and time basis</h2>

The lake values reproduce the textbook’s presentation of Lindeman’s historical research. Units are **J/(cm²·year)**. Producers fix 464.6, herbivores assimilate 62.8 and carnivores assimilate 12.6. These are annual energy flows, not animal masses or new measurements produced by the animation. Fixed energy is not all incident sunlight; consumer assimilation excludes ingested material that leaves unabsorbed as feces. [Lindeman’s 1942 paper](https://doi.org/10.2307/1930126)

For each trophic level, separate transfer to the next level, respiration, decomposer use and energy unused within the accounting interval. “Unused” does not mean all dissipated as heat. The carnivore-to-decomposer arrow is labeled only “trace.” The model preserves that unquantified label separately from its quantified terms. The printed totals add to 464.5, leaving a 0.1 trace/rounding residual. **That residual is not invented as a precisely measured decomposition flow.**

Transfer efficiencies are 62.8/464.6 ≈ 13.5% and 12.6/62.8 ≈ 20.1%. The familiar 10–20% range is a generalization, not a hard cap for every ecosystem or rounded dataset. Comparisons require consistent areas, intervals and accounting definitions. One J/(cm²·year) equals 10 kJ/(m²·year).

<h2 id="try">Build food-web relationships from field evidence</h2>

Choose a local farm, pond or campus and specify the time, place, organisms and division of work. Record producers, consumers, feed sources and the handling of straw and organic wastes. Label direct observations, interviews and unverified hypotheses separately. Watch mode uses an explicitly identified practice example. Your own evidence map and notes begin unverified and blank. Two organisms occurring together do not establish feeding, and a food-web link does not supply a measured energy flow.

Consider further uses of remaining organic material, such as feed, compost or biogas. Gold paths show energy passing through the system; gray paths show possible nutrient return to fields. **Nutrient reuse is not energy cycling.** These paths provide an analytical framework. Actual proposals require local evidence, technical conditions, costs and discussion with participants before revision.

<h2 id="deeper">Carbon conservation, accumulation and magnification differ</h2>

Twelve ordinary-carbon labels retain their identities across the carbon-cycle views. Counts in reservoirs plus labels in transit always total twelve. They represent carbon elements, not intact CO₂ molecules; **this is not an isotope experiment**. Photosynthesis, feeding, respiration and decomposition change carbon’s location. Ocean–atmosphere exchange can operate in both directions, while fossil burning transfers stored carbon into the atmosphere. Counts and timing are illustrative rather than scaled reservoir sizes or turnover times. [NOAA carbon-cycle basics](https://gml.noaa.gov/outreach/carbon_toolkit/basics.html)

The accumulation view is a closed water–organism model with 24 µg of contaminant, 10 kg of water and a 1 kg organism. Body burden changes by uptake minus elimination. Total contaminant mass remains constant, while each concentration divides its burden by the corresponding mass. The uptake coefficient is 0.3 and time is arbitrary. This is not a measured pesticide model or a risk prediction for a real ecosystem. [US EPA: toxics in the food web](https://www.epa.gov/salish-sea/toxics-food-web)

The trophic example considers 10 kg of cumulative food at 2 µg/kg: 20 µg ingested. Retaining 80% leaves 16 µg. Dividing by the specified 1 kg consumer mass gives 16 µg/kg, eight times the food concentration. Food portions depict cumulative feeding, not scaled animal sizes, and growth is not simulated. Reducing retention to 5% gives a body concentration below that of the food. Magnification therefore depends on substance properties, elimination, metabolism, diet and growth dilution; it is not inevitable for every contaminant at every trophic level.
