<h2 id="understand">The magnet stays present. Why does the needle respond only to motion?</h2>

Hold one pole over the coil, approach, pause, and withdraw. Flux can remain nonzero while the current returns to zero. Reversing the motion reverses the deflection. Compare how flux changes, rather than whether a field is present.

In the flux view, green shows the magnet's flux direction and copper the field established by the induced current. They oppose each other during approach, but can point the same way during withdrawal. The current opposes the **change** in flux. The four direction records come from textbook Fig. 2.1-1, viewed from the magnet side; they contain no measured current amplitudes.

A suspended aluminium ring makes the electrical response mechanical. It is pushed away during approach and pulled toward a withdrawing magnet. A gap leaves changing flux and a possible loop emf, but removes the complete circulating current and its magnetic force in this model. Force direction is not instantaneous velocity direction: a suspended ring can keep swinging.

<h2 id="try">Predict before changing the condition</h2>

- Keep the approach and reverse the pole. What if both the pole and motion are reversed?
- Split the ring. Which disappears: flux, emf, or the circulating current and its force?
- Keep the release height, magnet and trajectory. Change 80 turns to 160 and compare emf at the **same instant**.
- Stop the disc in the same field, then reverse it. What changes when its external circuit is opened?

Exploration remembers manual choices; the automatic film uses its own conditions. The motion clock includes genuine rests, and local views share the same physical state.

<h2 id="deeper">One flux function connects current, force and energy</h2>

<div className="formula">ε = −N dΦ/dt; I = ε/R; Fmagnet = NI ∂Φ/∂z</div>

The magnet is an axial point dipole. Flux through one circular turn is μ₀ma²/[2(a²+z²)^(3/2)], where m is magnetic moment and a is loop radius. Positive current is counterclockwise viewed from the magnet side. The circuit is resistive, with inductance neglected. Drawn turns are representative; the labelled total is used in calculations. The galvanometer has a stated teaching range of ±5 µA.

The ring follows an independent damped-pendulum equation. Electrical calculations use the physical relative position and velocity of magnet and ring. A local view enlarges angle 200 times without feeding this gain into the model. The strong-magnet moment of 3 A·m², ring mass of 1 g, resistance of 3 mΩ and pivot-to-centre distance of 14 cm are teaching parameters, not textbook measurements. Axial coupling neglects the tiny ring tilt, self-inductance and gap-capacitance transients. Input from the magnet's driver, minus power delivered mechanically to the ring, equals resistive heating.

Falling-magnet curves are **model calculations, not acquired voltage data**. The high-impedance sensor draws negligible current, so the magnet follows a gravitational trajectory without magnetic braking and stops at the lower catch. Both turn counts use one unclipped voltage scale. A higher release instead changes speed at the same position: a different controlled comparison.

<h2>Why can a disc generate in a constant field?</h2>

At radius r the conductor moves at ωr. The radial magnetic force on positive charge is qωrB. Integrating the motional emf from axle to rim gives ε = Bωr²/2. Brushes complete the external circuit; opening it removes current without necessarily removing emf.

The whole disc's constant area does not settle the behaviour of this sliding-contact circuit. Here v×B is evaluated directly. The drawn radial path represents the direction of a distributed current, not a single insulated radial wire. Brushes are ideal, the field uniform, and displayed rotation is slowed. Load current produces a braking torque; maintaining rotation requires mechanical input equal to I²R.
