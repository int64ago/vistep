<h2 id="understand">Understand the principle</h2>

The supplied PEP text connects experiment 3-1 on printed page 55, the inquiry on page 56 and experiment 3-2 on pages 61–62. Conditions suggest possible reactions; separation or purification comes before interpretation of a test.

Heating bromoethane with aqueous NaOH gives the net reaction C₂H₅Br + OH⁻ → C₂H₅OH + Br⁻. Bromoethane forms the lower layer. Sample the upper aqueous phase, acidify with dilute nitric acid and then add silver nitrate. Pale yellow AgBr supports the presence of free aqueous bromide. Acidification removes hydroxide interference that could otherwise produce Ag₂O. The displayed residual organic layer explains sampling and does not measure conversion.

Aqueous substitution of 1-bromobutane can produce butanol; alcoholic base and heat can favor elimination to 1-butene. These are competing pathways, not perfectly selective switches. Isolated-product evidence can combine an O–H IR feature with molecular-mass information. One O–H band does not distinguish butanol from ethanol solvent.

<h2 id="try">Try it yourself</h2>

Omit acidification and reduce hydrolysis progress to zero. Silver oxide can still appear, so precipitation alone is insufficient evidence for bromide. Relative initial amounts in the aliquot model are 1 for hydrolyzable bromine-containing material, 1.4 for hydroxide and 1.5 for silver ions; these are not textbook reagent amounts. The teaching limit assigns AgBr first and oxide from remaining silver, rather than solving a full competitive equilibrium. Selecting the organic layer represents missing the intended aqueous sample; entrained droplets are omitted.

Choose an impurity-only gas input. Ethanol vapor from the 1-bromobutane apparatus can consume acidified permanganate. It is not treated as an alkene undergoing bromine addition under these test conditions. The water wash reduces ethanol vapor. Ethanol dehydration may introduce SO₂ and other acidic impurities; the textbook uses NaOH solution to remove them. SO₂ can consume both permanganate and bromine.

Markers follow the same geometry as the tubes: the inlet is submerged and the outlet is above the liquid. Washing is an ideal effective-trap model with sufficient capacity, not a complete prediction of partition coefficients for every gas and wash solution. Marker counts are not concentrations; conditional color changes are neither reaction kinetics nor quantitative spectra.
