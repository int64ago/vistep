<section id="understand">

When a little water enters an ammonia-filled flask, gas transfers into solution and the inside pressure falls. Atmospheric pressure pushes reservoir water through the glass tube into the flask; new water dissolves more ammonia. The jet and liquid level follow one shared inventory, pressure and flow model.

The reservoir already contains phenolphthalein. Dissolved ammonia creates an alkaline solution and the indicator turns pink. Colourless ammonia gas does not become a pink gas. The initial dropper water contains no indicator, so pink appears only after indicator water enters from the reservoir.

</section>
<section id="try">

Change model time to follow inside pressure, water level and the jet. Remove the first drop, or close the water path. Without the drop, dissolution does not start; a closed path prevents continuous inflow even when pressure falls. Changing residual air changes how much water ultimately enters.

The residual-air comparison fixes initial air at 3% and 40% and compares endpoints of the same model. Remaining gas is not necessarily undissolved ammonia. These are mechanism calculations, not a reproduction of measured experimental timing or gas-preparation instructions.

</section>
<section id="deeper">
<details>
<summary>Pressure, flow and a spherical liquid level</summary>

A 1 L sphere is the reference vessel. Gas inventory uses equivalent volume at the initial ambient pressure and temperature. A 2 mL initial drop is a teaching parameter. Gaseous plus dissolved ammonia is conserved; ideal-gas pressure follows remaining gas and gas-space volume rather than making gas disappear graphically.

An effective partition relation n(gas)=n(total)V(gas)/(V(gas)+KV(water)), with K=700, is approached at a finite contact-dependent mass-transfer rate. This illustrates strong dissolution feedback. The textbook’s approximate 700 gas volumes per water volume is not presented as an exact Henry constant valid at every concentration. Concentrated ammonia thermodynamics, heat release, temperature changes, water-vapour pressure and mixing kinetics are not fitted.

Inflow follows the square root of the pressure difference after subtracting hydrostatic lift, with effective discharge coefficient 0.18 and tube area 6 mm². Reservoir lowering and extra liquid head after nozzle submersion enter that same pressure balance. Friction and local energy losses are combined in the coefficient. A jet reaching the wall is truncated and falls rather than travelling through glass.

The spherical liquid height is obtained from cap volume: V/V(sphere)=h²(3R−h)/(4R³). Neck volume is neglected. Height cannot be substituted linearly for volume fraction in a changing cross-section. Displayed pressures and percentages are ideal-model outputs, not textbook measurements, and narration time is not an experimental stopwatch.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Aqueous alkalinity and textbook position</summary>

The textbook writes NH₃+H₂O⇌NH₃·H₂O⇌NH₄⁺+OH⁻. The film uses the net relation NH₃+H₂O⇌NH₄⁺+OH⁻ to emphasize aqueous alkalinity. Ammonia is a weak base. Ion labels illustrate the local relation without computing pH or a precise ionized fraction; pink intensity is not a concentration measurement.
