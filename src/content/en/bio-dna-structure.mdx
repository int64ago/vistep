<h2 id="understand">Why do equal totals not determine the pairs?</h2>

Reordering the same bases preserves the total amounts of A, T, G and C while changing local contacts. Chargaff’s composition results constrain DNA structure without specifying every partner. Diffraction provides spacing and repeat clues; it is not a direct photograph of the double helix.

Hold the bases in a fixed alignment and expose their inward-facing chemical sites. In standard A–T pairing, A’s N6—H faces T’s O4, while T’s N3—H faces A’s N1. Each H remains covalently attached to its donor N; the dashed contact runs toward an acceptor. Replace T with C: the two-plus-one ring count remains, but donor faces donor and acceptor faces acceptor in this alignment. Ring count alone cannot explain complementarity. Standard G–C geometry has three corresponding contacts.

Local pairs must also fit the complete structure: sugar–phosphate backbones outside, bases inside, and antiparallel strands. Continuous covalent backbones run along each strand; hydrogen bonds between paired bases do not replace those connections. Keeping one strand lets standard complementarity predict the partner’s letters and direction. Actual replication also needs enzymes, substrates and energy; this structural model does not simulate replication.

<details>
<summary>Why is the contact diagram not a complete chemical simulation?</summary>

The diagram uses the standard Watson–Crick donor–acceptor pattern of common neutral bases. Sites and rings are unfolded into a readable, fixed alignment rather than measured atomic coordinates, bond angles or energies. The A–C counterexample tests this canonical alignment only. Real noncanonical pairs can adopt shifted, protonated or tautomeric states; this diagram does not rule them all out. Hydrogen-bond counts alone cannot calculate melting temperatures or replication fidelity. Stacking, solvent and ions also matter.

The backbone diagram tests connectivity without simulating polymerization. The 5′ and 3′ names refer to sugar carbon numbers; actual termini can carry phosphate or hydroxyl groups and are not fixed screen directions.

<summary>Where does the rotating spatial structure come from?</summary>

The spatial view retains both strands and all 24 nucleotide coordinates from [RCSB PDB 1BNA](https://www.rcsb.org/structure/1BNA): 12 base pairs with sequence CGCGAATTCGCG. Sugar rings, bases, the 22 intrachain links between neighboring nucleotides, and selected pairing contacts derive from the same coordinates. Solvent and some atoms are omitted. The scale bar uses the same orthographic projection, with 1 nm = 10 Å.

This measured structure dates from 1981; it is neither the 1953 model nor the only possible DNA conformation. Its self-complementary sequence reads the same along each strand’s own 5′→3′ direction, while facing positions run oppositely. Its 5′ termini have no separate phosphate atoms. The preceding ideal assembly and this measured spatial structure are labeled separately.
