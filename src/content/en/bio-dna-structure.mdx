<h2 id="understand">A model must satisfy several kinds of evidence</h2>

The textbook combines diffraction, base composition and molecular construction. Franklin and Gosling’s diffraction work, investigations by Wilkins and colleagues, Chargaff’s composition findings, and Watson and Crick’s modeling contributed distinct evidence. Diffraction is not a direct picture of molecular shape, and A = T with G = C cannot independently determine each paired position.

The lesson separates complementarity, sugar–phosphate connectivity and antiparallel orientation. Standard A–T and G–C pairs combine a purine and a pyrimidine, with two and three hydrogen bonds respectively. Equal ring totals do not make arbitrary partners chemically compatible.

<h2 id="try">Test incorrect proposals</h2>

- Compare straight strands and two helical twists; inspect their different calculated scattering patterns.
- Reorder the complementary strand. Composition stays fixed while local mismatches appear. These six-base teaching sequences are not Chargaff’s measured data.
- Try A–G and A–C to distinguish span from standard donor–acceptor matching.
- Trace the 3′ oxygen–phosphate–5′ connection between neighboring nucleotides, then inspect opposite strand directions.
- Rotate the measured 1BNA structure and select different base pairs. Inspect exterior backbones and unequal grooves. The scale bar uses the same orthographic projection: 1 nm = 10 Å.

Required 2, Chapter 3, Section 2 pp. 48–51; p. 51 asks students to construct a DNA model. Connectivity diagrams guide a physical paper or wire model rather than simulate polymerization or complete atomistic chemistry.

<h2 id="deeper">Separate ideal calculations from measured coordinates</h2>

<details>
<summary>What exactly is calculated?</summary>

The diffraction model uses equal point scatterers on two finite ideal strands, with 40 axial positions, 3.4 Å axial spacing and 10 Å radius. It compares 0°, 36° and 24° per step and averages intensity over eight azimuths. It uses no original diffraction measurements and is neither Photo 51 nor a complete DNA electron-density calculation. Finite length, coarse graining, angular sampling and logarithmic display affect the pattern. The supported demonstration is that changing geometry changes scattering.

The spatial view derives from both chains and all 24 nucleotide residues in PDB 1BNA, with sequence CGCGAATTCGCG. Sugar rings, base planes, continuous phosphodiester connections and selected hydrogen bonds share those measured coordinates. Solvent and many atomic details are omitted. This is a 1981 dodecamer structure, not the 1953 model or the unique shape of all DNA. The identical strand sequences complement each other because their orientations oppose.

Prime labels refer to sugar-carbon numbering, not fixed spatial directions. Actual termini may carry phosphate or hydroxyl groups; 1BNA has no separate terminal 5′ phosphate atom. The ladder is a connectivity-and-direction schematic, supplemented by the nucleotide close-up and measured structure. Hydrogen bonds are not the only source of stability: stacking, solvent and ionic conditions also matter.
