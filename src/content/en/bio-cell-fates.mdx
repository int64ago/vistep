<h2 id="understand">Use an outcome to ask what a cell retains</h2>

Carrot phloem cultures can regenerate roots, shoots and a whole plant. This classic observation supports retention of developmental genetic information after differentiation. It does not guarantee regeneration from every cell under every condition. The sequence reconstructs textbook p. 120; it is not a continuous recording of every starting cell.

A separate modern model retains the same DNA programs while changing expression. RNA is a distinct copy, followed by proteins and structures; DNA does not turn into RNA. “Root” and “shoot” programs summarize regulation rather than naming genes measured in that experiment. Regeneration supports retained information but does not, by itself, establish every expression mechanism.

<h2 id="try">Keep the observation fixed and change the comparison</h2>

- Compare complete loss of required developmental information with the observed regeneration. Then follow different expression programs from the same DNA. The hypothesis changes; the observation does not.
- In the culture comparison, change the assumption about culture conditions. The recorded 50, 20 and 2–4 generations remain fixed. Changing conditions makes source alone an insufficient explanation.
- Track the donor nucleus into recipient cytoplasm, then inspect the original studies and their limits.
- Change CED-3 or CED-4 function. Birth and branch identity stay fixed while subsequent death execution changes.

<h2 id="deeper">Proliferation, senescence and death are distinct</h2>

<details>
<summary>Do culture generations predict a person's lifespan?</summary>

No. Under the same culture conditions, the textbook compares lung fibroblasts from fetal sources at approximately 50 generations, middle-aged sources at 20 and older sources at 2–4. These are values for a specified comparison, not a rule for every tissue. A source association does not identify the entire mechanism of aging. A cell that stops dividing is not necessarily dead.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Does a young nucleus automatically rejuvenate a cell?</summary>

The textbook compares old nuclei with young cytoplasm and young nuclei with old cytoplasm to discuss a nuclear contribution. The animated division qualitatively summarizes that account; it is not a real-time recording of a transplantation.

[A 1976 reconstruction study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/001448277690269X) reported mostly limited division after nuclei from younger cultures were combined with cytoplasts from older cultures. Culture passage age is different from human donor age. [A separate 1983 study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6301866/) reported at least 40% inhibition of DNA synthesis after young cells were fused with senescent cytoplasts. The display normalizes the control to 100% and shows an upper bound of 60% for the fusion group. The hatched bar is not an exact mean or a complete raw dataset.

The conditions differ and the studies cannot be pooled. Their original abstracts were checked; unavailable full methods and raw measurements are not presented as if read. A young nucleus alone does not guarantee vigorous proliferation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What do lineage tracking and genetic intervention each establish?</summary>

Following parentage identifies the fate of a particular branch. The standard hermaphrodite somatic lineage of C. elegans generates 1090 cells; 131 undergo programmed death and 959 remain. Germ cells are excluded. The small tree contains 15 illustrative nodes, not a complete measured lineage, a named actual branch or the actual death fraction. Progressively tallying fates does not place all 1090 cells in an animal simultaneously.

[Ellis and Horvitz's genetic study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3955651/) showed that loss or reduction of ced-3 or ced-4 function can allow normally doomed cells to survive and differentiate. The model preserves a local cell's birth identity while comparing execution with intervention; color alone does not establish apoptosis. The 1986 genetic evidence establishes the requirement for these genes, but does not by itself determine protein activation order. [Chinnaiyan and colleagues (1997)](https://www.nature.com/articles/41913) subsequently supported CED-4-mediated activation of CED-3. The simplified CED-4 → CED-3 relation therefore draws on later molecular evidence. We checked the original abstract and figure titles; no complete experimental dataset is reconstructed. Condensation summarizes selected apoptotic features rather than a complete molecular mechanism or every death pathway.

Apoptosis is regulated cell death. Senescence, proliferative arrest and injury-associated necrosis must not be conflated. This scene neither substitutes necrotic morphology for apoptosis nor counts arrest as death.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Which part is historical evidence, and which is modern explanation?</summary>

The culture sequence follows textbook p. 120. The [bibliographic record of Steward, Mapes and Mears's 1958 paper](https://bsapubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/j.1537-2197.1958.tb10599.x) was also checked. Its complete original plates were not retrieved, so no success rate, exact timing or cell-by-cell clonal lineage is reconstructed. DNA → RNA → protein belongs to the modern explanatory layer, not a claim about direct gene measurements in that experiment. Regeneration remains conditional on cell state, culture conditions and developmental regulation.

</details>
