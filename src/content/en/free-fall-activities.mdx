<h2 id="understand">Separate observation, reasoning and measurement</h2>

**Paper and a paper ball:** use two equal sheets, leaving one flat and crumpling the other. Release them together from the same height. Equal masses can fall quite differently in air. Comparing both with an eraser shows how changing shape makes the paper’s motion more similar to the eraser’s. This controls conditions to reveal drag; it does not establish that mass can never affect motion through air.

**Tied stones:** suppose a heavy stone must fall faster than a light one. Tie them together. The lighter stone should slow the heavier one, making the combination slower. Yet the combination is heavier, so the same assumption says it should be faster. The contradiction exposes a problem with the assumption. This is a thought argument, not a timed record of a physical drop.

**A reaction ruler:** one person releases a ruler and another closes their fingers after seeing it move. The distance fallen during that delay gives a time estimate. The question has changed from explaining different falls to using a known motion law as a measuring tool.

<h2 id="try">Change one condition and respect the conclusion’s limits</h2>

- Keep both paper masses equal and compare the distances traveled with different shapes. Remove drag to check that all three return to the same ideal free-fall motion.
- Reveal the tied-stones argument step by step. Keep the same pair throughout; do not assign the contradictory predictions to different objects.
- Change the ruler-catch delay. Increasing it from0.10 s to0.20 s increases the distance from4.9 cm to19.6 cm: four times farther.

The ruler view follows the ruler, so the fingers move upward relative to its scale. Distance marks are evenly spaced; time marks are not. The displayed delay is a simulation setting, not a measurement of the viewer’s reaction ability.

<summary>Calibrating the reaction ruler</summary>

For release from rest with negligible drag:

<div className="formula">h = ½gt²；t = √(2h/g)</div>

Withg =9.8 m/s², delays of0.10,0.15,0.20,0.25 and0.30 s correspond to approximately4.9,11.0,19.6,30.6 and44.1 cm. Equal increases in time occupy progressively larger distances, so time labels cannot be placed evenly along the ruler.

A real catch also depends on anticipation, viewing direction, initial finger position and ruler tilt. Repeat measurements under consistent conditions and examine their distribution. One result is not a fixed physiological property of a person.

<details>
<summary>The paper-drop approximation</summary>

All three objects share a1.4 m falling distance, zero initial velocity and the same gravity. Both paper samples weigh5 g; the eraser weighs20 g. In air, a quadratic-drag model uses fixed projected area and drag coefficient. Areas are0.025 m² for the sheet,0.0004 m² for the paper ball and0.0007 m² for the eraser. These are illustrative parameters, not measured results.

Real paper tumbles, drifts and changes frontal area; crumpled paper is irregular too. The model isolates the controlled-comparison logic. Contact with the floor stops the objects without bounce. Removing drag returns every mass and shape to the same free-fall solution.
