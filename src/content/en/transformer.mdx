<h2 id="understand">Guess, then let the error change the model</h2>

This tiny model learns three sentences: “cat likes eating fish,” “dog likes eating meat,” and “bird likes eating worms.” It performs real Transformer calculations. Its initial weights come from a seeded random generator, not a memorized answer.

Training predicts the next token at each position and compares the result with the known continuation. Lower probability for the correct token means higher cross-entropy loss.

Backpropagation traces how changes to parameters would affect that loss. An optimizer makes small weight updates. Repeating this is learning. The displayed Q weights, attention matrix and probabilities come from the live model.

**Training changes weights. Generation uses the weights already learned.** Generating another token performs no backpropagation. Context and probabilities change while the weights stay fixed.

<h2 id="try">How does it choose fish rather than meat?</h2>

Before training, switch between cat, dog and bird. Train for several rounds and compare the same inputs again. Loss can vary between nearby steps because the training sentence changes; look at the broader trend.

Each attention row belongs to a position. It can use earlier positions and itself, but not the future. The crosses in the upper right mark the causal mask.

The word “eating” alone cannot tell fish from meat or worms. The subject supplies distinguishing context. Attention is a learnable route for exchanging information between positions; a large attention value does not by itself explain the whole decision.

Generation adds one token to the context at a time. At temperature zero, sampling chooses the most likely token. Higher temperatures make lower-probability tokens easier to sample. Temperature changes the sampling rule, not the model's knowledge.

<h2 id="deeper">Inside one forward pass</h2>

<details>
<summary>Tokens, Q/K/V and the feed-forward network</summary>

The underlying teaching vocabulary has nine symbols: eight Chinese characters and a period. The English interface shows their meaning as word labels. This keeps the exact same model and weights in both languages; it is not a separate English language model.

Each token selects an 8D embedding and adds a learned positional vector. Normalized representations are projected to Q, K and V. Dot products between a query and visible keys, scaled by the square root of dimension, pass through softmax to form attention weights that sum to one. These weights mix the values.

An output projection, residual connections, normalization and a feed-forward network lead to vocabulary probabilities. This model uses RMSNorm and a 16D hidden feed-forward layer.

</details>

<details>
<summary>How far is this from a large language model?</summary>

There is one layer, one attention head and a four-token context. Large models use far more parameters, longer contexts, multiple heads, much more data and additional engineering. Their tokenizers do not necessarily split by character or word.

Learning three sentences demonstrates a few statistical relationships, not general language ability or understanding of the world. The animal sentences are training examples, not comprehensive biological claims.

Training runs in your browser without uploading inputs or calling an AI service. Frozen weights refer to this experiment's generation step; whether a commercial product later retains data for training is a separate design decision.

</details>
