<h2 id="understand">First identify what the electrodes measure</h2>

Two electrodes lie on the nerve surface. At rest their potentials match; excitation reaches a before b, producing successive differences with opposite signs. Both deflections result from one traveling excitation, rather than two signals moving in opposite directions.

Membrane voltage is a different quantity: inside relative to outside. Changing ionic permeability contributes to an action potential. The film separates that voltage from the extracellular difference before approaching a synapse. A terminal releases transmitter, which binds receptors and alters postsynaptic channel state. Clearance then reduces the effect.

A historical comparison asks whether contraction through a metal bridge uniquely establishes a biological electrical source. It does not. Appropriate tissue contact without metal and interruption of the connection provide additional evidence. The images explain inference; they do not demonstrate animal procedures.

<h2 id="try">Distinguish different kinds of zero</h2>

Coincident surface electrodes give zero difference even when the nerve is active. Increasing propagation speed changes arrival times without changing this model pulse's amplitude. A zero meter reading therefore need not imply an inactive nerve.

The synapse exploration shows membrane voltage early in the sequence, followed by transmitter release and receptor response. Removing release differs from disabling the receptor: the latter still allows transmitter to reach the other membrane. The historical view separately compares metal contact, tissue contact and a disconnected route.

<h2 id="deeper">Waveforms, receptors and declared approximations</h2>

The surface record uses a translated negative pulse. Both electrodes sample the same function. The default 20 mm spacing and speed of 20 mm/ms give an independently checkable arrival difference of 1 ms. Amplitude uses relative units; it is not a measured frog compound action potential. A separate membrane waveform represents approximately −70 mV rest, depolarization and recovery without claiming to solve the Hodgkin–Huxley equations.

The synapse model has one release, a finite transmission delay, exponential clearance and a saturating binding relation:

<div className="formula">Bound fraction = C/(Kd + C)</div>

C is a normalized transmitter signal and Kd = 0.35 is a teaching choice. The voltage response illustrates an excitatory example, not every kind of synapse. Markers indicate identity and route rather than molecular size, number or microscopic diffusion trajectories.

Tests independently check coincident electrodes, opposite peaks, propagation delay, absent release versus blocked response, and the disconnected historical comparison. Spatial extracellular currents, thresholds, refractory behavior and many-fiber recruitment in real tissue are outside this pulse illustration.
