<h2 id="understand">Spectra identify matter and test theory</h2>

A salt flame provides a color; a spectroscope turns color into comparable lines. Hydrogen Balmer lines and their alignment with selected solar absorption lines explain how light can carry evidence about distant matter. The prism is an optical symbol: the linear wavelength axis is not a physical prism-dispersion calculation.

The textbook’s 1933 optical work is distinguished from the 1947 Lamb–Retherford microwave experiment. The latter is a historical-method extension showing how resonance reduces a metastable signal.

<h2 id="try">Expand, align and scan</h2>

Expand the yellow region of the sodium spectrum, select a hydrogen upper level, or overlay emission on solar absorption. In precision spectroscopy, scan microwave frequency and locate the dip in the surviving metastable signal.

<h2 id="deeper">Numerical conventions and approximations</h2>

<details>

<summary>Wavelength references and the resonance model</summary>

<div className="formula">1/λ = R_H(1/2² − 1/n²); ΔE = hf</div>

Hydrogen uses vacuum wavelengths with R_H=1.0967758×10⁷ m⁻¹, omitting fine structure, the Lamb shift and broadening. Sodium uses NIST air wavelengths of 588.9950 and 589.5924 nm. These conventions are not interchangeable. Display widths and brightness are illustrative.

The microwave curve is centered at 1057.8 MHz: survival = 1−0.8/[1+((f−f₀)/50 MHz)²]. Its 100 MHz full width and 0.8 contrast are teaching choices, not original experimental parameters. Level spacing and apparatus geometry use display scales.
