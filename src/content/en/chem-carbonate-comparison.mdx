<h2 id="understand">Observations are clues; explanations must make predictions</h2>

Two similar white salts can give different temperature changes, amounts of remaining solid and indicator colours. These observations involve dissolution and hydration, as well as acid–base equilibrium. Saying that “a reaction happened” does not explain all three.

Separating existing interactions takes energy; forming new interactions between ions and water releases energy. The balance determines whether the surroundings warm or cool. With little water, anhydrous carbonate can also form hydrated crystals. The film shows the textbook’s qualitative temperature directions, without inventing measured changes.

When carbonate accepts a proton from water, bicarbonate and hydroxide can result. Sodium retains its identity and contributes to overall charge balance. Bicarbonate can both accept and donate protons. Its final alkalinity depends on the coupled equilibria of water, carbonate species and dissolved carbon dioxide. The word “hydrogen” in a name cannot decide acidity.

<h2 id="try">Ask the explanation to predict</h2>

- Scrub the proton map and follow the hydrogen with the gold ring. Water’s oxygen, the carbon skeleton and spectator sodium must remain accounted for.
- Add only water in the dilution view. Total carbon stays fixed while concentration and equilibrium composition change. Faint or absent pink does not prove that salt vanished, or that pH is exactly 7.
- Select carbonate in the heating view. The same apparatus and progress control cannot create carbon dioxide without the corresponding reaction.
- Compare partial and complete conversion in the mass view. Unreacted bicarbonate remains in the residue, so its potential further mass loss differs.

<h2 id="deeper">Keep the three models distinct</h2>

<details>
<summary>The textbook recipe and the separate dilute comparison</summary>

Experiment 2-4 uses approximately 1 g of each solid, first with little water and then with more. Equal mass is not equal amount of substance, and undissolved solid also matters. The textbook sample view is qualitative.

The numerical comparison is a separate model at 25°C and 0.010 mol/L. It assumes ideal activities in a closed system, with no additional exchange of carbon dioxide with air. Total carbon and sodium are conserved while acid–base equilibria and electroneutrality are solved together. It gives approximately pH 11.13 for carbonate and pH 8.34 for bicarbonate. These are not measurements of the textbook recipe.

The constants are K<sub>w</sub> = 10<sup>−14</sup>, K<sub>1</sub>\* = 10<sup>−6.35</sup> and K<sub>2</sub> = 10<sup>−10.33</sup>. The first constant belongs to the **combined CO₂(aq) and H₂CO₃ pool**, not the intrinsic dissociation of pure carbonic acid. The visible H₂CO₃ proton map is also connected to CO₂(aq) + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃; neither branch alone represents the whole equilibrium. See the [USGS carbonate speciation method](https://water.usgs.gov/water-resources/memos/memo.php?id=2098) and [Pines and colleagues’ carbonic-acid study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5747581/).

Indicator tone is a qualitative transition, not absorbance or a universal concentration calibration. A nearly colourless sample can still be mildly alkaline. See the [RSC indicator and dry-ice demonstration](https://edu.rsc.org/experiments/indicators-and-dry-ice-demonstration/703.article).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Is the white residue the original substance?</summary>

<div className="formula">2 NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂</div>

The original sodium, carbon, hydrogen and oxygen enter solid carbonate, water and carbon dioxide. The atom map represents a net change, not an elementary mechanism. Reaction extent does not predict heating time or temperature.

Using molar masses of 84.0066, 105.9888, 18.0153 and 44.0091 g/mol, complete conversion of 1 g of pure bicarbonate leaves about 0.6308 g of solid. Half conversion leaves about 0.8154 g. Water and carbon dioxide account for the remaining mass. These are predictions for testing the explanation, not reported textbook measurements. Constant mass after reheating is a completion clue, not proof of purity.

Carbonate stability refers to the textbook heating conditions; the limewater display covers only a small carbon-dioxide dose. The atom map, mass account and apparatus follow the same net material balance; the close-up replays the atoms’ destinations. See the [Alameda thermal-decomposition experiment](https://alameda.edu/hubfs/Chem-1A-Exp.-7-Decomposition-of-Sodium-Carbonate.pdf). Its actual document title refers to bicarbonate, despite the file name.

</details>
