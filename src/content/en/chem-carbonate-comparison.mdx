<section id="understand">
## Similar appearance needs separate tests

Sodium carbonate is a white powder and sodium bicarbonate consists of fine white crystals. A little water can hydrate and clump carbonate with release of heat. Bicarbonate dissolution absorbs heat. Both solutions are alkaline and can give different phenolphthalein intensities. Names and appearance do not replace these observations.

Heating supplies a separate comparison. Carbonate does not readily decompose under the textbook conditions. Bicarbonate produces carbonate, water and carbon dioxide. A small amount of carbon dioxide clouds limewater by forming calcium carbonate.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Control the observation sequence

The water view compares the same stages: a little water, more water, then indicator. Thermometers show direction without fabricated temperatures. In the heating view, select a salt and change reaction extent; gas, cloudiness and the mass track follow the same model.

Initial mass scales product amounts. Partial extent leaves unreacted bicarbonate in the solid residue. Carbonate does not generate gas merely because the progress slider moves.

</section>

<details>
<summary>Model and numerical balance</summary>

<div className="formula">2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂</div>

Molar masses of 84.0066, 105.9888, 18.0153 and 44.0091 g/mol determine the mass balance. Complete decomposition leaves about 0.6308 of the initial mass as solid carbonate; water and carbon dioxide account for the rest. Extent is controlled directly; no unverified rate or temperature trajectory is predicted.

The dissolution display uses a room-temperature endpoint: about 9.6 g bicarbonate per 100 g water at 20°C. The selected carbonate reference gives about 17% salt by mass in saturated solution at 20°C, or a salt/water ratio of17/83. Hydration and heat effects are qualitative; temperature-dependent solubility is not coupled. Indicator colour is not a pH measurement. Limewater is restricted to the initial small-gas-dose stage.
