<h2 id="understand">From two spark gaps to several kinds of wave evidence</h2>

Hertz connected an induction coil to a metal oscillator with a small gap. Its discharge excited rapidly changing electromagnetic fields. A nearby wire loop with another gap could respond even though no wire joined the devices: energy travelled through space.

Reception alone does not establish every detail. Reflected and incident waves produce spatial maxima and minima; different media refract the waves; receiver orientation affects response; finite apertures produce diffraction. Together these observations support the shared wave behaviour of light and electromagnetic radiation.

<h2 id="try">Connect each observation to its apparatus</h2>

- Pause before the propagation delay: the receiving loop must not respond early.
- Compare nodes and antinodes near the ideal conducting reflector.
- Adjust incidence and check bending toward the normal in the higher-index medium.
- Rotate the receiving loop's normal and compare flux projection and relative power.
- Narrow the slit relative to wavelength and observe the broader normalized central lobe.

<h2 id="deeper">Explanatory models, not reconstructed historical measurements</h2>

<div className="formula">Delay Δt = r/c; sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂; relative power ∝ cos²α</div>

The transmitter uses a damped 100 MHz packet; reception includes delay, distance attenuation and loop orientation. Spark brightness only indicates relative response, without an air-breakdown calculation. Actual historical sparks were broadband, with more complicated near fields and coupling.

Reflection uses a one-dimensional perfect-conductor boundary, refraction uses a planar interface, and slit diffraction uses a scalar far-field approximation. Each isolates a testable wave property rather than solving one historical apparatus in full. Space and nanosecond time are enlarged for viewing; traces are model output.
