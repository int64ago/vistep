name: Apparent-weight experiments
title: Why does a scale change when body mass stays the same?
---

<section id="understand">

Near the bottom of a squat, you can still be moving downward while the scale reads higher than when standing still. Your mass has not suddenly increased; the support force has changed.

Starting the squat requires a downward net force, so support is smaller than weight. Braking that downward motion requires an upward net force, so support becomes larger than weight. With upward positive, both states follow N−mg=ma. **The reading depends on acceleration; travel direction alone is insufficient.**

PEP Required Book 1 §4.6 presents squatting and standing (pages 109–110), a scale in a lift (111), and a falling-bottle exercise (112). Each has a distinct apparatus and observation path here.

</section>
<section id="try">

Pause late in a squat: velocity is downward, acceleration upward, and the reading exceeds its rest value. Change to a force platform and trace the full squat and rise. In the lift, keep the person upright and compare start, cruise, and braking; then reverse travel direction.

For the bottle, watch both the hole and earlier droplets. Release removes the sustained jet, but water already outside continues under gravity with its existing velocity.

</section>
<section id="deeper">

<details>

<summary>What the instruments measure</summary>

Support is N = m(g + a). An ideal scale labelled in kilograms displays N/g, equal to true body mass only at zero acceleration. The force platform displays newtons. Mass and g = 9.8 m/s² remain fixed while motion changes.

The squat uses a quintic centre-of-mass trajectory with continuous position, velocity and acceleration. Fixed-length leg geometry keeps feet on the platform; it is illustrative rather than a biomechanical measurement. The lift uses sinusoidal acceleration, a zero-acceleration cruise, and reversed acceleration for braking. Generated curves are not digitized textbook measurements. Body sway and instrument filtering are omitted.

</details>

<details>

<summary>Water in the falling bottle</summary>

A vented stationary bottle has approximately Δp = ρgh across the hole. In the freely falling bottle frame, effective gravity is g + a = 0, so the original hydrostatic gradient cannot persist. A quasi-steady outlet approximation makes new-jet speed u = √[2(g + a)h] fall to zero.

Real release has a short fluid-inertia transient. This model omits it, along with surface tension, air drag, and level change. Previously emitted droplets retain their horizontal speed and downward gravitational acceleration; they are neither erased nor pulled back. Water head is12 cm. The scene covers a short fall and does not simulate impact.

</details>
