<section id="understand">

A sugar’s name is not an experimental result. Dissolution, iodine colour, a silver mirror and a brick-red precipitate answer different questions. Hydrolysis also changes the sample being tested.

<ChemSugarTests client:visible />

## Separate the questions

Glucose can reduce Ag(I) to metallic silver in an ammoniacal test, and Cu(II) to Cu(I), forming brick-red Cu₂O with fresh copper reagent under alkaline, warmed conditions. These observations support reducing behaviour; neither is a uniquely identifying fingerprint for glucose. Most glucose molecules in water are cyclic, while a small open-chain population reacts and is replenished by equilibrium.

The blue iodine–starch response asks whether starch remains. A partly hydrolysed sample may contain both long chains and reducing products, so both tests can be positive. Use separate sample portions rather than combining every reagent into one supposed test.

## Changed composition changes the interpretation

Untreated sucrose does not give a clear ordinary reducing-sugar test. Maltose retains a free hemiacetal end that can open, whereas sucrose uses both anomeric sites in its glycosidic connection.

Sucrose hydrolysis yields glucose and fructose. A positive result afterwards describes the changed sample, not the original sucrose. Fructose can also participate through rearrangement in the alkaline test; a positive result does not mean that every starting molecule already contained an aldehyde group.

Long starch and cellulose chains do not usually give a clear positive under these textbook qualitative conditions. They are not absolutely devoid of reducing ends. Hydrolysis increases the number of small reducing units and changes the evidence.

</section>
<section id="try">

## Make the conditions matter

Choose partially hydrolysed starch. Use iodine first, then choose copper for a separate portion and observe that both results can be positive. Disable alkaline testing: the display identifies invalid conditions rather than presenting the lack of precipitate as a valid negative.

Compare untreated sucrose with maltose, then increase hydrolysis. Silver and copper share a two-electron capacity account. Changing sample amount can make the fixed metal reagent limiting. The film always restores its own curricular conditions independently of exploration.

<details>
<summary>Model, limits and textbook positions</summary>

Hydrolysis mixes untreated and fully hydrolysed endpoints, omitting real oligosaccharides, dextrins and stepwise kinetics. Starch/cellulose use a linear chain of degree 1000 with explicit end groups: C₆ₙH₁₀ₙ₊₂O₅ₙ₊₁ consumes n−1 water units to yield n glucose units. The textbook repeat-unit equation omits that end correction. The eight displayed symbols are an abbreviated fragment, not a real chain length or branching topology.

Reducing capacity is normalised to equal hexose-unit amounts. One reactive end supplies two electron units; both metal accounts preserve inventory and limiting reagents. Real complexation equilibria, fructose product distributions, nucleation and deposition shape are omitted. A chosen 0.02-unit visibility threshold makes low end-group density undetectable in the model. Colour, mirror coverage and precipitate height are not quantitative measurements; real sensitivity depends on concentration and conditions.

Cool-water starch dispersion and warm gelatinisation are qualitative states. Acid hydrolysates must be cooled and adjusted to suitable alkalinity for the textbook copper interpretation. Cellulose needs its specified acid treatment; the animation does not imply that ordinary hot water suffices. Practical silver and copper testing is organised by a school laboratory.
