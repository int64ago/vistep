<h2 id="understand">Inspect where the gas enters</h2>

The aerobic route is air → NaOH → yeast culture → limewater. The anaerobic culture first consumes residual oxygen while sealed, then connects to its detector. Inlets extend below each liquid surface; outlets remain above it.

Both conditions can produce CO₂. A separate culture sample tests for ethanol. Residual glucose also reacts with acidified dichromate, so the textbook extends cultivation to exhaust it. The visualization exposes this confounder. It explains a supervised textbook assay rather than providing a home procedure.

<h2 id="try">Follow the same carbon identifiers</h2>

- Disable incoming CO₂ removal and inspect the additional source of detector response.
- Skip the anaerobic preparation and identify the unresolved oxygen condition.
- Pause the carbon pathway and count two pyruvates, two ethanols plus two CO₂, or two lactates.
- Switch to lactate fermentation and observe the absence of CO₂ release.

Required 1, Chapter 5, p. 86 and pp. 90–94. ATP and intracellular pathways are mechanism visualizations; the yeast comparison is a formal inquiry activity.

<h2 id="deeper">Separate energy from matter</h2>

<details>
<summary>What does the model simplify?</summary>

Carbon identifiers demonstrate conservation, not complete isotope-level reaction mapping. Mitochondria retain double membranes and cristae; glycolysis occurs outside them in cytosol. Terminal oxygen forms water while carbon enters CO₂. The diagram does not imply that every oxygen atom in CO₂ came from inhaled O₂.

Culture curves, cloudiness and colors are teaching states, not measured rates. The idealized aerobic group produces no ethanol, but real high-sugar yeast can ferment despite oxygen. Lactate illustrates another fermentation pathway, not lactate production by the depicted yeast.

Net ATP hydrolysis supplies free energy; breaking a bond itself requires energy. Total respiratory ATP yield depends on conditions, so only the net 2 ATP from glycolysis is fixed here.

</details>
