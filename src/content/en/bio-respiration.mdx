<h2 id="understand">Both cultures make gas. Must they obtain ATP in the same way?</h2>

Aerated and oxygen-depleted yeast cultures can both produce carbon dioxide. Explaining this requires three separate accounts: **where carbon goes, how electron carriers are recycled, and where ATP is made.** Bubbles are not an ATP counter.

Glycolysis retains the six carbons of glucose in two pyruvates. It invests 2 ATP and produces 4, a net gain of 2. NAD⁺ also accepts reducing equivalents and becomes NADH. If a finite carrier pool remains reduced, the next oxidation requiring NAD⁺ becomes limited. Fermentation restores the same carrier so earlier reactions can continue.

Yeast ethanol fermentation releases CO₂ and reduces acetaldehyde to ethanol. Those final reactions add no ATP. Homolactic fermentation retains three carbons and restores NAD⁺ without producing CO₂. No bubbles therefore does not imply no ATP gain. This does not mean that all lactic acid bacteria use only the homolactic route.

<h2 id="try">Test the prediction with a counterexample</h2>

- Replace ATP with free inorganic phosphate in the closeup. Containing phosphorus does not make the two donors interchangeable in the chosen enzyme reaction.
- Remove incoming CO₂ absorption from the gas route. Cloudiness can then have more than one source.
- Block carrier recycling late in the carrier scene, then restore it. Watch whether the next reaction has NAD⁺ available.
- Stop oxygen late in the membrane scene. Stored energy drains gradually; ATP synthesis does not instantly fall to zero.

<h2 id="deeper">Separate observations from explanatory models</h2>

<details>
<summary>What do the gas route and assays establish?</summary>

The aerated route absorbs incoming CO₂ before air reaches the culture and detector. Inlets into washing and detector liquids extend below their surfaces; outlets take headspace gas. A sealed culture still contains oxygen initially. A stopper alone is not evidence of an oxygen-free condition.

Residual glucose interferes with the acidified dichromate ethanol assay. Excluding that interference does not exclude every possible reducing substance. The film reconstructs qualitative textbook results, without original measured culture data. The ideal route contrast is also not a universal rule that oxygen prevents ethanol production: high-sugar yeast can ferment aerobically.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does the ATP transfer track?</summary>

The textbook uses phosphorylation of a membrane protein on p. 88. This film chooses hexokinase to connect a concrete ATP-coupled reaction to respiration. Terminal phosphorus and three nonbridging oxygens transfer; the original glucose C6 oxygen forms a new bond. The original ATP β—O—γ bridge oxygen stays in ADP. A proton is assigned to the buffered environment; magnesium coordination and acid-base steps are condensed.

This is a net atom map, not a free PO₃ intermediate or transition-state calculation. The complete reaction and conditions determine its free-energy change; breaking a bond itself requires energy. This single transfer shows only the first glycolytic ATP investment. A separate account includes both investments, four ATP-producing transfers, and inorganic phosphate incorporation.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Limits of the carbon, carrier and membrane models</summary>

C1–C6 refer to positions in the original glucose. In the depicted EMP ethanol route, C3/C4 enter CO₂; C1/C6 enter ethanol methyl groups and C2/C5 enter its hydroxymethyl groups. The diagram compresses many reactions and excludes glycerol, biomass branches and isotope fractionation.

The carrier close-up revisits glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate oxidation within glycolysis. Inorganic phosphate participates in forming 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate while NAD⁺ accepts a reducing equivalent. P symbols abbreviate phosphate groups, with their other oxygens omitted. Only carbon positions and N1/N2 carrier identities are tracked here; this is not a complete atom map or mechanism. The next substrate batch contains new molecules, rather than the previous products reversing.

The membrane view is specifically a local model of cytosolic NADH entering the budding-yeast respiratory chain through an external NADH dehydrogenase. Budding yeast lacks a human-style complex I. Cytosolic NADH passes electrons at the outside of the inner membrane; it does not cross that membrane. This is not the universal entry position for all mitochondrial NADH. Downstream electron transfer and proton transport use different components from the ATP synthase return path. Terminal oxygen forms water; this does not assign all oxygen atoms in CO₂ to inhaled O₂.

The proton distribution cues an electrochemical difference, not measured pH or voltage. The reservoir model illustrates input, storage, return and leakage in teaching units. It assigns no fixed proton-to-ATP ratio or universal respiratory yield of 30, 32, 36 or 38 ATP.

</details>
