<h2 id="understand">Why does corrosion move a water column?</h2>

Experiment 4-3 moistens cleaned iron nails with saturated salt solution and places them in a sealed side-arm tube. Its gas connection leads to a delivery tube immersed in water. Oxygen consumption lowers the internal pressure, so external water rises in the tube. The observation connects **oxygen uptake with pressure difference**; the nail is not pulling water directly. Gas tightness and temperature control matter because leaks and temperature changes can also affect the level.

In a weakly acidic or near-neutral water film, iron oxidation can couple to oxygen reduction producing hydroxide. In acidic conditions, hydrogen evolution can be an important cathodic process. Later corrosion products undergo further reactions: real rust is not one pure material with fixed composition, nor a dependable dense protective coating.

The second part keeps equal zinc samples in equal volumes and concentrations of dilute hydrochloric acid, then adds one or two drops of copper sulfate to one sample. This film uses two. Copper deposition begins only after the drops enter, and contact between copper and zinc can accelerate hydrogen evolution. Calling the entire sequence simply “copper sulfate catalysis” misses the initial displacement reduction of copper ions.

<h2 id="try">Which metal dissolves, and which becomes the cathode?</h2>

Experiment 4-4 connects iron and zinc by a conductor in acidified 3% sodium chloride. Zinc oxidizes as the sacrificial anode. Electrons reach iron through the metallic path and support reduction at its surface. The meter illustrates direction and the presence of current, rather than claiming a measured current value.

Take an aliquot from the iron region, transfer it to a separate tube and add two drops of K₃[Fe(CN)₆]. The film reconstructs two independent trials: connected iron–zinc, and iron alone. Ferrous ions from the latter can give the blue test. A negative visible result in the protected trial supports suppressed dissolution, but detection limits, sampling location and conditions prevent it from proving exactly zero corrosion current.

For the gel observation, the textbook uses 1 g agar, 50 mL saturated salt solution and 150 mL water. Stir and heat to dissolve, cool slightly and divide between dishes. Add five or six drops each of phenolphthalein and potassium ferricyanide to each dish, then place cleaned nails about 2–3 cm long in the prepared medium. Compare zinc-wrapped and copper-wrapped iron.

- With metallic zinc contact, zinc oxidizes preferentially. Cathodic reaction near iron can create local alkalinity and pink phenolphthalein.
- With copper contact, iron more readily acts as the anode. Blue near iron supports ferrous-ion formation; cathodic regions near copper can turn pink.
- Zinc merely placed nearby, without an electronic connection, does not establish the same protection circuit. The separated/contact comparison reconstructs independent trials; it does not suggest that an existing blue product disappears when zinc is later attached.

Blue and pink carry different evidence: **ferrous-ion testing** and **local alkalinity**. Do not reverse those assignments, or equate every colored pixel with an exact atom-release location. Indicator chemistry and diffusion affect the observed pattern.

<h2 id="deeper">A warmer investigation needs experiments, not a supplied temperature curve</h2>

The textbook research task calls for sources, mechanism, material and composition comparisons, construction, trial use, improvements and a report. Iron oxidation supplies heat. Water supports the reaction environment, salt affects ionic conduction, and carbon and water-retaining materials affect local reaction, moisture and heat transfer. The inner pouch also limits oxygen access.

This virtual example contains 5 g iron, 2 g carbon, 0.2 g salt, 3 mL water and 1 g vermiculite. **It is a model comparison, not a recommended fabrication recipe.** Opening the outer package allows oxygen through the inner pouch. The model follows remaining iron, oxygen entry and consumption, released heat, stored heat and environmental loss. All curves are labeled as model results; your research record starts blank.

Choose one independent variable for an actual investigation. Keep particle size, probe placement, ambient temperature and package area comparable. Record both ambient and material temperatures, retain oxygen-limited or dry controls, repeat the trials, and compare warming, peak temperature and duration. Actual sample curves must come from measurements rather than copying this illustration.

A complete report also considers contact temperature, package strength and use conditions. Teachers should organize practical trials; warm material should not remain directly against skin for prolonged periods. Use commercial products according to their instructions. Identify the cooled contents and follow the school's laboratory waste arrangements, preventing loose powder and documenting the disposal plan.

<summary>Additional numerical conditions</summary>

Manometry assumes a 50 mL initial headspace at 25°C, a delivery-tube internal radius of 1.5 mm and an external water surface of 5 cm². Ideal-gas behavior, hydrostatic pressure and transferred liquid volume are solved together. Rising water compresses the gas while the reservoir surface falls slightly. A fixed-volume pressure estimate therefore cannot be directly converted into the displayed height. The uptake law is illustrative; not every apparatus dimension is drawn to one physical scale.

The zinc calculation additionally assumes two granules totaling 0.50 g, 5 mL of 0.5 mol/L HCl, and two 0.05 mL drops of 0.1 mol/L CuSO₄. Copper, zinc and hydrogen-ion inventories are conserved. Rate constants and the acceleration factor are teaching assumptions. Protection assumes an ideal external current of 2 mA and an equivalent local iron-alone corrosion current of 1 mA. It does not calculate actual mixed potentials, mass transfer or residual corrosion under protection.

The warmer uses a lumped heat capacity of 40 J/K, an initial ambient temperature of 25°C and an adjustable heat-loss coefficient. Kinetics and pore transport are illustrative. Even the sealed control contains limited initial oxygen and can react briefly. NIST-JANAF hematite formation enthalpy, about −825.50 kJ/mol, supplies the reference energy scale: complete conversion of 5 g iron to Fe₂O₃ corresponds to about 36.96 kJ. Real hydrated products differ, so this is not measured warmer heat. The calculation consistently checks released heat against stored plus lost heat.
