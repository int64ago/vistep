title: 'Why can absorbing heat freeze water beneath a beaker?'

<section id="understand">

Experiment 6-1 records the acid’s initial temperature, then adds magnesium and observes both reaction and warming. Magnesium and hydrogen ions form magnesium ions and hydrogen gas; released energy warms solution and apparatus. The vessel can subsequently lose heat to its surroundings without reversing the chemical reaction.

Experiment 6-2 mixes barium hydroxide octahydrate and ammonium chloride crystals. Endothermic cooling can freeze water between the beaker and a wooden block, allowing the block to follow when the beaker is lifted. A dry-wood comparison lacks that water layer and therefore lacks the same ice connection, even if it becomes cold.

</section>
<section id="try">

Select the reaction and vary model time and final reaction extent. A smaller endothermic extent can cool the system without freezing the interface water. Increasing heat exchange with the surroundings brings temperature back toward room temperature faster after reaction completion.

Choose a wet or dry wood interface. Both use the same reaction heat budget, but water adds heat capacity and latent heat, so their temperatures need not match. Compare ice formation rather than treating low temperature alone as an attachment condition.

</section>
<section id="deeper">
<details>
<summary>Independent reaction and thermal models</summary>

The magnesium inventory follows Mg+2H⁺→Mg²⁺+H₂, retaining spectator chloride. The crystal inventory uses Ba(OH)₂·8H₂O+2NH₄Cl→BaCl₂+2NH₃+10H₂O as an overall composition balance. Actual hydration, dissolution and ammonia gas–liquid partitioning are more complex; this compact equation alone does not determine the exact experimental heat.

Heat budgets, capacities, times and temperatures are teaching fixtures, not unreported textbook measurements. The magnesium model releases 96J into an effective 16J/K capacity, giving an independent adiabatic endpoint of 26℃ from 20℃. The crystal model absorbs 800J with a 20J/K effective reactor capacity and 0.5g interface water. Liquid-water heat capacity is 4180J/(kg·K), ice capacity 2100J/(kg·K), and latent heat 333500J/kg. These reference parameters explain conservation and phase change; they do not predict a temperature from a specified experimental reagent quantity.

A prescribed smooth extent curve directs the animation rather than fitting chemical kinetics. Environmental heat flow is proportional to its temperature difference. Every numerical step checks stored-energy change against reaction input plus environmental heat input. Reaction input to the thermal reservoir is positive for the magnesium case and negative for the endothermic crystal case. These signs describe the thermal reservoir; the chemical reaction releases or absorbs that energy respectively.

</details>
<details>
<summary>Why reaching zero does not freeze all the water at once</summary>

The mixture, vessel and interface water share ideal good thermal contact. With liquid water, energy changes temperature. During freezing, the temperature remains at 0℃ while ice fraction changes. After complete freezing, further energy removal lowers the combined temperature below zero. Spatial gradients, supercooling, contact resistance and phase changes of the mixture itself are omitted.

Visible attachment requires water and a frozen fraction above one half in this model. This criterion distinguishes an ice connection from no ice connection, without calculating mechanical strength. Real attachment also depends on area, interface geometry, weight and ice strength. The threshold is not an experimental load standard. Dry wood does not prevent the endothermic reaction; it simply lacks freezable interface water.

</details>

Required 2, printed 32–33/PDF 37–38: full text and photographs of Experiments 6-1 and 6-2 were checked, including thermometer and wet-wood evidence. The book establishes heat effects without supplying the model temperature trajectories shown here.

The official [RSC metal–acid experiment](https://edu.rsc.org/experiments/exothermic-metal-acid-reactions/1731.article) supports magnesium/acid warming. The Wisconsin page and official indexed RSC/UCSD text were read; direct UCSD access was restricted.

The film explains evidence without repeating smell or touch as action prompts, or supplying a household replication procedure.
