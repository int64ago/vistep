<section id="understand">
## Concentration needs two reliable quantities

Amount concentration is c=n/V. Preparing 100 mL at 1.00 mol/L requires 0.100 mol of sodium chloride. The textbook rounded atomic masses, Na=23.0 and Cl=35.5, give M=58.5 g/mol and a required mass of 5.85 g. More precise atomic masses give a slightly different value.

Dissolve the solid in a beaker, then transfer along a glass rod. Rinsing both surfaces and transferring the washings recovers the weighed solute. Making up to volume controls the total solution volume, not the volume of water added. The flask typically specifies 20 °C; the solution should approach the calibration conditions before adjustment.

</section>
<section id="try">
## Separate recovery from final volume

Hold volume at 100 mL and reduce recovery to see concentration fall. Restore full recovery, then vary final volume to observe the inverse relationship. Mixing changes local distribution while the mean concentration of equal-volume layers stays constant.

For dilution, 50.0 mL of 1.00 mol/L stock contains 0.0500 mol of solute. Making this up to 100 mL gives 0.500 mol/L. The textbook practical permits a measuring cylinder under school conditions, with a footnote recommending a pipette for accurate transfer. Measuring, complete transfer and final-volume adjustment all contribute to uncertainty.

</section>

<summary>Reading, conservation and model assumptions</summary>

Read the concave meniscus bottom at eye level and align it with the calibration line. Removing some well-mixed solution after overfilling does not restore the intended concentration. Mixing makes concentration uniform throughout the flask.

An idealized surface of revolution connects volume and height by cross-sectional integration, normalized to 100 mL at the line. It is not a calibration of a real flask, and the small meniscus-cap volume is omitted. The 95% transfer recovery and initial concentration gradient deliberately amplify the causal effects. Salt solution is colourless; gold marks and green shading track solute rather than actual appearance. Fluid motion during inversion is not simulated.
