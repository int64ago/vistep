<section id="understand">

An upper layer suggests ester formation, but cannot reveal atom destinations. Label the oxygen in ethanol: will it enter the ester or the water? This question connects esterification, hydrolysis and reversible equilibrium.

## Preserve the combined activity

Required book 2 activity 9 includes combustion-product tests, copper-catalysed ethanol oxidation and an ethanoic-acid investigation as well as ester preparation. The first three chapters retain these separate observations: water droplets and limewater; one cycling copper coil; and a designed litmus/carbonate comparison.

Complete ethanol combustion produces water and carbon dioxide, whereas the copper cycle accounts for ethanal. Ethanoic acid releases carbon dioxide from carbonate while forming acetate, which also explains removal of acidic impurities in the receiver.

## What a layer cannot tell us

Elective book 3, page 76, uses oxygen-18 tracing: the labelled ethanol oxygen remains in ethyl ethanoate. The alcohol carbon–oxygen bond survives, and the labelled oxygen connects to the acid’s acyl carbon. The drawing preserves that oxygen and both carbon groups as continuous objects.

In the textbook net account, the acid hydroxyl and the alcohol hydroxyl hydrogen form water. Each side contains four carbons, ten hydrogens and three oxygens: C₄H₈O₂ in ester and H₂O in water. Assigning the two displayed hydrogens to water is representative net bookkeeping. The oxygen-label evidence does not establish individual proton trajectories through solution.

Actual acid catalysis involves carbonyl activation, alcohol addition, proton transfers and elimination of water. The acid participates and regenerates. The animation explains evidence and changes in connectivity, not a single collision or a microscopic movie.

## One connected path, separate accounts

`CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O`

Mixed vapour travels from the heated reaction tube through its stopper and delivery line. The tip remains above the receiver liquid. The textbook uses saturated sodium carbonate solution to neutralise acid, take up ethanol and reduce ester solubility. The upper liquid is ester-rich, not automatically pure ester.

The model keeps reaction and receiver amounts separate: ester formed, ester collected and ester in the upper layer are different quantities. Ethyl acetate has a specific gravity near 0.90, supporting the upper ester-rich layer in this system; layer identity always depends on the mixture.

## Does faster change the endpoint?

Acidic hydrolysis reverses the net connections, returning acid and alcohol. Compare closed systems with identical starting amounts and temperature, adding a catalyst to one. It hydrolyses more ester early, but both reach the same equilibrium composition. Catalysis changes the approach in both directions without changing the equilibrium constant at that temperature.

Removing product changes the material conditions; that is a different comparison. The closed model has no receiver and removes nothing. In real preparation, concentrated sulfuric acid may also change water availability and the medium, so not every effect of adding acid is purely catalytic.

## Why hydrolysis has two accounts

Acid-catalysed hydrolysis is reversible. Basic hydrolysis consumes hydroxide and produces acetate plus ethanol:

`CH₃COOC₂H₅ + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + C₂H₅OH`

Formation of carboxylate favours forward conversion. Saying that base accelerates hydrolysis does not mean that it remains unconsumed.

## Change one condition at a time

Predict the labelled oxygen’s destination, reveal it, then scrub the atom mapping. In the closed comparison, changing starting water changes the equilibrium composition; toggling catalysis changes the approach to a shared endpoint. With zero water, this model has no hydrolysis reactant.

In preparation, change heating or acid catalysis and observe collected ester. The layer view continues the same account. Pause to inspect the space between the delivery tip and liquid surface.

Hydrolysis compares three media with the same initial ester and bath temperature. Lowering initial hydroxide can make it limiting. The temperature view holds medium fixed while comparing 25°C with 75°C. The chosen rate ordering represents one teaching scenario, not a universal ranking of acid and base concentrations.

<summary>Model limits, activity differences and curriculum</summary>

Preparation starts with one model unit each of acid and ethanol and 0.2 of water. Mass-action-style reversible reaction is combined with transfer of each component to a receiver. Forward/reverse coefficients 0.7/0.175, transfer coefficients and heating/catalysis multipliers are chosen teaching parameters, not experimental kinetics or vapour–liquid equilibrium. The receiver begins with 0.3 carbonate units. A 93%/7% illustrative ester split preserves material without claiming a distribution coefficient or measured solubility. Real work additionally involves transport, evaporation, temperature gradients and phase equilibria.

The new closed comparison separately uses an ideal mass-action model: one initial ester unit, adjustable water, and no initial acid or alcohol. Chosen hydrolysis/esterification coefficients are 0.175/0.7, giving an esterification equilibrium constant of 4. The catalyst multiplier scales both directions. Time is a model coordinate, not measured seconds. The bath’s acid and neutral branches call this same analytic material model with the same equilibrium constant. Bath temperature and observation duration map only to the rate–time coordinate. Temperature dependence of the real equilibrium constant is not modelled; this is not evidence that temperature cannot shift equilibrium. Both comparisons use relative uncatalysed/catalysed multipliers of 0.18 and 1, with no separately chosen endpoint.

The retained bath model starts with one ester and ten water units. Acid/neutral branches share the analytic reversible balance above; base hydrolysis is limited by both ester and hydroxide. The temperature factor uses a selected 30 kJ/mol activation parameter, not a fit to the textbook experiment. A small residual ester threshold hides a separate layer, distinguishing an observation from conversion; actual ternary phase equilibrium is not modelled. Neither the percentages nor layer sizes are measured data.

The inquiry proposes comparing layer-disappearance time. Elective activity 1 uses one 70–80°C bath, discusses odour change and asks for alternative observations. This film uses visible layers instead of prompting direct smelling. Practical work involving volatile organics, heating and concentrated acid belongs in a school laboratory.
