<h2 id="understand">How does thickness become brightness and color?</h2>

A soap film does not emit its own yellow stripes. Light reflects from its boundaries, and the returning electric fields add at the same location. Some thicknesses reinforce the return; others suppress it. Follow one location through a section and the fields at one instant to connect the bands to thickness.

## Compare phases at the same wavefront

At the air-to-film boundary, the reflected field reverses by half a cycle. Light entering the film returns from its rear boundary without that reflection reversal, but propagation adds phase. Comparing both paths at the same outgoing wavefront gives

<div className="formula">Δ = 2nd cos θ₁</div>

where d is thickness, n the film index, and θ₁ the internal angle. At oblique incidence, the two internal lengths alone are insufficient: subtract the exterior reference path traveled by the front reflection. The dashed section line joins the same outgoing wavefront; π and 0 label the interface phase changes.

As thickness tends to zero, the returning fields cancel and reflection becomes dark. At normal incidence, the first bright condition is d = λ/(4n). Like neighboring fringes differ by λ/(2n) of thickness. Change wavelength and the same position generally changes brightness.

## Follow a fringe, then change the illumination

The film uses a prescribed thickness profile that increases downward. Reducing that profile moves the marker for one fixed interference order. Its new location still meets the same bright condition. This is not a fluid simulation or an observational drainage record.

One thickness reflects different wavelengths by different amounts. White light therefore produces colors. The image samples 650, 530 and 460 nm, and the bars use the same calculation at the selected thickness. The color closeup places that thickness at the central marker; adjusting it changes this local patch and its color. This three-wavelength encoding is not a measured spectrum or calibrated colorimetry.

<h2 id="try">Predict the air-wedge spacing</h2>

Two paper sheets separate two glass plates. The air gap grows from contact toward the spacer. Glass-to-air reflection has no phase reversal; air-to-glass reflection has a half-cycle reversal. The contact end is consequently dark in reflection.

For the fringe comparison, return to normal incidence. Like neighboring fringes differ by λ/2 of air-gap thickness. With spacer thickness h and plate length L, the small-angle profile is d = hx/L, giving

<div className="formula">Δx = λL / (2h)</div>

Remove one identical sheet: h halves while L and λ stay fixed, so spacing doubles. Watch the upper plate settle, predict, then reveal the new fringes. Doubling wavelength at fixed geometry also doubles spacing, through a different controlled change.

<summary>Multiple returns and model limits</summary>

The section follows the first two reflected paths. The amber waveform instead combines every internal return. For front-boundary field coefficient r and propagation phase β = 4πnd cos θ₁ / λ, the symmetric lossless film gives

<div className="formula">r_film = r(1 − exp(iβ)) / (1 − r²exp(iβ))；R = |r_film|²</div>

The three traces show one shared, slowed period. A vertical cursor samples the same instant on all three; the lower field equals the sum of the upper two.

This includes the complete internal-reflection series. Its exact reflectance must not be labeled a first-two-ray approximation. The calculation conserves power, R + T = 1. Incident and exit media are identical, indices are constant, and oblique views use s polarization below total internal reflection. Evanescent-film propagation is excluded. The displayed optical clock is deliberately slowed.

Drainage is a prescribed thickness change. The wedge uses 40 μm per paper sheet and 80 mm plate length as teaching inputs. The upper strip shows only the first 4 mm from contact. Section thickness and angle are enlarged; settling interpolates supported geometry rather than solving friction, glass elasticity or paper compression.
