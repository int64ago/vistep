## Concept and direction

**Question:** How does electrical power cross the glass and become heat in the pan?

Follow two closed circuits that never make electrical contact. A pale glass sheet separates a connected copper spiral from the blue equivalent current loop inside a cutaway pan. The coil has an actual underpass, supply, return and source-internal closing branch. The front pan half is transparent for inspection; it does not represent a broken conductor. Restrained copper, blue-green glass and matte steel replace flames, glowing metal and invented moving heat particles.

The observation changes from geometry, to the derivative of linked flux, to where electrical loss occurs, then to slow thermal storage. The final shot removes the electrical apparatus and gives the entire composition to the pan/glass thermal exchange. It makes residual heating legible without a permanently stacked apparatus, chart and control panel.

## Independent model and units

SI units are used internally. The primary is a twelve-turn Archimedean spiral, 25–105 mm in radius, with a 1.8 mm wire radius. Rounded below-plane lead centerlines are shared by rendered tubes and the Neumann integral. The equivalent pan loop has radius 75 mm and effective width 40 mm, inside a 3 mm base. The full pan remains conducting across the visual cutaway.

The primary has R₁ = 0.04 Ω and L₁ = 30 µH; secondary external inductance is 0.28 µH. Mutual inductance is calculated from the complete closed air-core circuit geometry, rather than an arbitrary distance multiplier. Default M ≈ 1.5184 µH. This is a declared reduction of a distributed electromagnetic problem: **ferromagnetic field concentration is not solved**. Material permeability affects the surface impedance only. The resulting material comparison is not a prediction for a particular commercial hob.

The one-sided finite slab has γ = (1 + j)/δ and surface impedance Zs = (γ/σ)coth(γt), with δ = 1/√(πfμσ). The opposite-face magnetic-field boundary is zero. Stable small-argument and thick-slab limits avoid numerical cancellation. The equivalent resistance and internal reactance use the same Zs. The normalized depth curve comes from that slab solution; integrating Joule loss through the effective area recovers circuit pan power. It is cycle-average electrical dissipation, not optical emission or a solved spatial temperature field.

RMS phasors satisfy I₂ = −jωMI₁/Z₂ and U₁ = (R₁ + jωL₁)I₁ + jωMI₂. Real source input equals primary plus pan electrical loss. Within a fixed-frequency on-state, the effective secondary inductance gives an instantaneous reciprocal energy balance, including both self-energy terms and M i₁i₂. Flux has the orientation of the explicitly parameterized circuit. Source regulation, matching, electronics and construction details are omitted.

Material parameters are illustrative constants: steel σ = 6×10⁶ S/m, μr = 40; nonmagnetic stainless steel σ = 1.4×10⁶ S/m, μr = 1; aluminum σ = 3.5×10⁷ S/m, μr = 1. Hysteresis, saturation and temperature-dependent material properties are omitted. This scene explains **electrical metal loss**, not a complete separation of measured appliance losses. Specialized products can heat nonmagnetic pans; compatibility remains device-specific.

At 25 kHz and 28 A RMS the example gives about 144.10 W of pan electrical loss and 31.36 W of coil loss. These are teaching values, not cooking settings. The same drive gives about 65.85 W for the stainless example and 14.02 W for aluminum; the source has not been retuned for either load.

The thermal model contains two uniform nodes, metal pan and glass, starting at 20 °C. Pan capacity follows its modeled base and wall mass; glass capacity is 500 J/K. Contact conductance decreases with lift, with a small residual conductance retained. Pan and glass each exchange heat with the environment. Exact two-by-two matrix-exponential advancement reconstructs every constant-power segment and burst boundary. Pan electrical loss enters this thermal domain; coil loss does not. There is no food, boiling, convection field, local glass temperature, or temperature-dependent radiation model. The four-second burst model omits brief switching transients and their small stored electromagnetic energy.
