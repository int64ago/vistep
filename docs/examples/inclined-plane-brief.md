# Inclined plane — a smaller force can cost more work

Owner: `/root/hundred_mechanisms`. Original side elevation and work ledger, with a fixed geometric scale within each viewport. Track a 120 N crate to the same 1 m height. Prerequisites: force, distance, multiplication. Distinct contribution beyond lever/pulley: kinetic friction reverses the naive inference from lower force to lower energy.

Primary sources: [OpenStax University Physics 1 §6.2, Friction and the Inclined Plane](https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-1/pages/6-2-friction) for normal force and kinetic friction; [OpenStax Physics §9.3, Calculating Mechanical Advantage and Efficiency](https://openstax.org/books/physics/pages/9-3-simple-machines) for force/travel and work. Equations apply to constant-speed upward sliding with a parallel pull, not static friction or an angled pull.

Benchmark: at 30°, W = 120 N and h = 1 m, an ideal ramp takes 60 N over 2 m, or 120 J. Counterexample: adding μ = 0.30 makes a 12° ramp require less force but more total work than 30°. Reject θ = 0 at nonzero height. Direct lift at 90° has zero normal force.

Six shots: direct lift; 30° ramp; geometry and force change with angle; ideal work conserved through the whole trip; progressively introduce friction; make the rough ramp shallower and compare increasing heat with falling pull. The final two are complete-trip comparisons, not moving simulations with unaccounted parameter work.

Desktop 760×340 and phone 340×255 use separate fixed scales that include the full 12° ramp. Text is 21 SVG units on phone and ledger labels remain 16 CSS px. Crate bottom is tangent to the computed plane. Its position and all energy counters derive from the same progress. Cover follows the same ramp geometry.
